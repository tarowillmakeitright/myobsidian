# 絵唐津の筆は、野の風を連れてくる

晩夏から初秋へ向かう朝、器の上を走る一本の草の線に心を澄ませてみましょう。

唐津へ向かう話をするとき、旅人は海や城下町や朝市を思い浮かべるかもしれません。けれども茶の湯の人にとって唐津は、まず土の手触りから始まる土地です。きめ細かく整えられすぎた白ではなく、少しざらつきを残した土。塗り込めて均した表面ではなく、焼かれてなお呼吸をやめていないような肌。そのうえに、鬼板と呼ばれる鉄の液で草や花や鳥がさっと描かれ、灰釉の下で少しにじみ、少し途切れます。絵唐津の魅力は、その絵が上手であることよりも、描かれた線が完全には言い切らないことにあります。

唐津観光協会の解説では、唐津焼は近年の研究では一五八〇年代ごろに現在の唐津周辺で焼かれ始めたとされ、のちに朝鮮陶工の技術も取り入れながら広がっていったと説明されています。また同協会は、絵唐津を鬼板で絵を描いて釉をかける、唐津を代表する種類のひとつとして紹介しています。佐賀県の伝統工芸の解説でも、唐津焼には彫り、櫛目、象嵌、掻き落とし、絵付けといった技法が受け継がれていることが示されています。つまり絵唐津は、ただ素朴な器なのではありません。荒々しく見えるところの奥に、土地に根ざした技法の選択と、焼成の見極めと、筆を止める勇気が重なっています。

茶席で絵唐津が特別に感じられるのは、その絵が説明ではなく気配として働くからでしょう。草文が一本あるだけで、器の外に風景が続く気がします。鳥が一羽いれば、余白は空になります。蔓がさらりと流れれば、手の中の茶碗が露地の続きのように見えてきます。しかもその線は、琳派の装飾のように場を支配するためではなく、土と釉のあいだに「ここに野が通った」とだけ記すためにあります。茶の湯が器に求めてきたのは、しばしば完成された絵画より、むしろ一碗の内側に残る未完の余情でした。絵唐津は、その未完を上手に引き受ける焼物だと思います。

海外の美術館に収蔵された古い作例を見ても、その性格はよく伝わります。スミソニアン国立アジア美術館には、佐賀県の作で一五九六年から一六一五年ごろとされる絵唐津系の天目茶碗があり、木灰釉の下に鉄絵が置かれています。またメトロポリタン美術館には、桃山期から江戸期にかけての唐津の茶碗や鉢が複数収蔵され、幾何文や鉄絵、ひびの入った釉など、それぞれ異なる表情が確認できます。ここで大切なのは、唐津がいつも同じ顔をしていないことです。絵唐津、斑唐津、朝鮮唐津、三島唐津。呼び名ごとに景色は違い、同じ「唐津らしさ」もひとつには決まりません。だからこそ今日の一碗を前にしたときは、「唐津とはこういうもの」と急いでまとめず、その器にいま現れている土の息づかいと線の軽さを受け取るほうが、茶の見方としては誠実です。

土ものの旅は、派手な名所巡りとは少し違います。窯場を歩くと、名物の名より先に坂道の角度や、海から入る湿り気や、乾かした器を並べる場所の光が気になってきます。絵唐津の線を見ていると、その筆は机上で考え抜かれた意匠というより、土地の空気に押されてすっと出た身ぶりに近いのではないかと思えてきます。茶人が唐津を愛したのは、豪華さがないからではなく、土地の気配が器の表面に薄く、しかし消えずに残るからかもしれません。目立ちすぎないのに、忘れにくい。言い切らないのに、場の季節を動かす。そういう器は、朝の一服によく似合います。

## 今日の見どころ

絵そのものより、絵が止まるところを見てみましょう。筆が細くなる箇所、釉の下でにじむ輪郭、描かれなかった余白が、器を「景色」に変える境目です。茶碗の外側に野が続いていくように感じられるなら、その一碗はもう十分に語っています。

## 小さな実践

紙と鉛筆、あるいは指先だけでもかまいません。今日、草一本を「うまく描く」のではなく、「言い切りすぎない線」として一度だけ表してみてください。紙がなければ、窓の曇りや机の上に指でなぞるだけでも大丈夫です。

そのあと、次の三つを静かに確かめます。

1. どこで線を勢いよく進め、どこで止めたくなったか。
2. 余白が寂しいのか、息をしやすいのか。
3. もう一本足したくなる衝動は、豊かさのためか、不安を隠すためか。

茶の湯の器を見る稽古は、鑑定眼を持つことだけではありません。少なく描いて、なお残るものを感じる稽古でもあります。

# The Brush of E-Karatsu Carries the Wind of the Fields

On this morning between late summer and early autumn, let your attention settle on a single painted blade of grass running across a bowl.

When people speak of traveling to Karatsu, they may think first of the sea, the castle town, or the morning market. For tea people, though, Karatsu often begins with the touch of clay. Not a flawlessly refined white surface, but earth that keeps a slight roughness. Not a skin polished into silence, but one that still seems to breathe after firing. Across that body, an iron-rich liquid called `onita` is brushed in quick images of grasses, flowers, or birds. Beneath the ash glaze, the lines blur a little, break a little, and refuse to become too complete. The charm of `E-Karatsu`, painted Karatsu ware, lies less in painterly virtuosity than in its refusal to finish the sentence.

According to the Karatsu Tourism Association, recent research places the beginning of Karatsu ware around the 1580s in the area around present-day Karatsu, after which it expanded while incorporating techniques associated with Korean potters. The same official guide introduces `E-Karatsu` as one of the representative Karatsu types: designs painted in `onita` and then covered with glaze. Saga Prefecture's description of the craft also notes inherited techniques such as carving, comb marks, inlay, slip-carving, and painted decoration. So `E-Karatsu` is not merely rustic pottery. Beneath its rough appearance lies a precise chain of choices: local materials, technical inheritance, firing judgment, and the discipline to stop the brush before it says too much.

What makes `E-Karatsu` especially moving in the tea room is that its painting works not as explanation but as atmosphere. A single grass motif can make you feel that a wider landscape continues beyond the bowl. One bird can turn empty clay into sky. A trailing vine can make the tea bowl feel like an extension of the garden path. These motifs do not dominate the room as decorative spectacle. They simply record, in the space between clay and glaze, that the field has passed here. Tea has often valued not a finished painting but a remainder of feeling, something left slightly unresolved within the bowl. `E-Karatsu` is one of the wares that carries that unresolved feeling with unusual grace.

That character is visible even in old museum pieces abroad. The Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art holds an `E-Karatsu`-type tenmoku tea bowl from Saga Prefecture dated about 1596 to 1615, with iron pigment beneath a wood-ash glaze. The Metropolitan Museum of Art likewise preserves multiple Karatsu bowls from the Momoyama and Edo periods, showing varied personalities through geometry, iron-painted motifs, and crackled or uneven glazes. The important point is that Karatsu does not wear only one face. `E-Karatsu`, `Madara Karatsu`, `Chosen Karatsu`, `Mishima Karatsu` each open a different landscape, and even "Karatsu-like" beauty does not settle into a single formula. That is why, when facing a bowl today, it is more honest not to rush toward "Karatsu means this." Better to receive the breathing of the clay and the lightness of the line that this particular bowl presents now.

Journeys through clay regions are different from tours of famous landmarks. Walking near kiln sites, you begin to notice the incline of a road, the moisture coming in from the sea, the quality of light where vessels are left to dry. Looking at the lines of `E-Karatsu`, one starts to feel that the brushwork is less an intellectual design than a movement released by the air of the place itself. Tea people may have loved Karatsu not because it lacked splendor, but because the climate of a region remains thinly, quietly, and indelibly on the vessel's surface. It does not insist, yet it lingers. It does not explain, yet it shifts the season of the room. That kind of bowl belongs beautifully to a morning cup of tea.

## Today's Point of Attention

Look not only at the painted motif but at the place where the motif stops. Where the brush thins, where the glaze softens an edge, and where empty space is left untouched: this is where a vessel becomes a landscape. If the outside of the bowl seems to open into a field, it is already speaking enough.

## Small Practice

Use a sheet of paper and a pencil, or simply your fingertip. Today, try expressing one blade of grass not as a "good drawing" but as a line that does not say too much. If you have no paper, trace it once into window mist or lightly on a tabletop.

Then quietly ask yourself three things:

1. Where did the line want to move quickly, and where did you want to stop?
2. Does the empty space feel lonely, or does it feel easier to breathe in?
3. Is the urge to add one more line coming from richness, or from anxiety?

Learning to look at tea utensils is not only a matter of connoisseurship. It is also practice in sensing what remains after very little has been drawn.

## Vocabulary

1. `絵唐津`（えがらつ）: painted Karatsu ware. Usage: A Karatsu type decorated with brushed iron designs under glaze.
2. `唐津焼`（からつやき）: Karatsu ware. Usage: Pottery from the Karatsu area in Saga, long associated with tea utensils.
3. `鬼板`（おにいた）: iron-rich pigment or iron solution. Usage: Used in Karatsu ware to paint motifs before glazing.
4. `灰釉`（はいゆう）: ash glaze. Usage: A glaze made with ash that can soften and blur painted decoration.
5. `鉄絵`（てつえ）: iron-painted design. Usage: Refers to designs brushed in iron pigment on ceramics.
6. `土もの`（つちもの）: earthen, clay-centered ware. Usage: A casual but useful way to speak of pottery valued for clay character.
7. `余白`（よはく）: empty space, margin. Usage: In tea aesthetics, the unpainted area may matter as much as the motif.
8. `景色`（けしき）: landscape, visual character. Usage: Tea people use this word for the expressive surface or appearance of a bowl.
9. `露地`（ろじ）: the tea garden path. Usage: A bowl's motif can evoke the same quiet transition as the path to the tea room.
10. `窯場`（かまば）: kiln site or pottery area. Usage: Refers to the working place where ceramics are made and fired.
11. `土味`（つちあじ）: the felt quality of clay. Usage: Used when describing how strongly the clay itself is sensed in a piece.
12. `未完`（みかん）: incompletion, unfinished quality. Usage: In tea, a slightly unresolved feeling can deepen rather than diminish beauty.
