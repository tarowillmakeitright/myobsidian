# 茶道マガジン｜2026-05-28

## 特集（日本語）
**雨音と一碗――「にじり口」がつくる心の姿勢**

今朝の東京は、雲が低く垂れこめ、細かな雨粒が空気をしっとりと包んでいます。こんな朝に思い出したいのが、茶室の入口にある**にじり口**です。茶道を学び始めると、点前の順番や道具の名前に意識が向きがちですが、実は「どう入るか」が「どう一服を受け取るか」を大きく左右します。

にじり口は、身をかがめなければ通れない小さな入口です。武家も町人も、身分や立場をいったん外して、同じ高さで茶室に入る。ここには、茶の湯が大切にしてきた平等の思想が、建築として具体化されています。頭を下げる動きには、礼だけでなく、心の速度を落とす働きがあります。普段の生活では、私たちは「次へ、次へ」と急ぎがちです。けれど、にじり口の前では自然に立ち止まり、呼吸を整え、膝と腰の感覚に注意を向けます。

茶道では、こうした身体の所作が、そのまま内面の準備になります。亭主が炭を置き、湯を沸かし、茶碗を温める動きも、客が襖の前で一礼し、畳の縁を意識して進む動きも、すべてが「今ここ」に心を戻すための技法です。派手な演出はありません。しかし、単純な反復には不思議な力があります。繰り返すうちに、雑音の多い思考が静かになり、目の前の湯気や香りに感覚が開いていきます。

にじり口の思想は、現代の朝にも応用できます。たとえば、家を出る前に30秒だけ「小さな入口」をつくるのです。スマホを置き、肩の力を抜き、息を4拍で吸って6拍で吐く。あるいは、マグカップを両手で持ち、湯気を一度見てから口をつける。この小さな儀式は、茶室に入る前の身支度に似ています。外の世界へ向かう前に、内側の姿勢を整える――それだけで一日の質が変わります。

また、にじり口は「選び取る」感覚も教えてくれます。狭い入口を通るには、余計な荷物を持ち込みにくい。茶室に不要なものを持たないように、私たちの朝も、不要な情報や焦りを抱え込みすぎない工夫ができます。通知を全部見るのではなく、まず最重要の3つだけ確認する。予定を詰め込みすぎず、移動の余白を10分取る。茶道的に言えば、これは「間」を尊ぶ設計です。

茶道は、古い作法の保存だけではありません。むしろ、時代が変わっても人の心身を調えるための、実践的な知恵の束です。にじり口という小さな入口は、私たちに静かに問いかけます。今日のあなたは、どんな姿勢で一日へ入りますか。速さではなく、深さを選ぶ朝。たった一碗でも、世界の見え方は変わります。

## Feature (English)
**One Bowl in the Rain: What the Nijiriguchi Teaches About Entering the Day**

Tokyo this morning feels soft and saturated—low clouds, damp air, and intermittent drizzle. On mornings like this, one of the most meaningful tea concepts to revisit is the **nijiriguchi**, the small crawl-in entrance of a traditional tea room. Beginners often focus first on procedure: sequence, utensils, memorization. But in tea, *how you enter* shapes *how you receive* everything that follows.

A nijiriguchi is intentionally small. You cannot pass through it standing tall. You must lower your body—head, shoulders, ego, momentum. Historically, this created symbolic equality: samurai, merchants, monks, and townspeople all entered the same way, at the same level. The architecture itself became philosophy. In tea, values are not only spoken; they are built into movement and space.

What is striking is how physical action changes mental state. Bowing and crouching naturally slow your pace. Your attention shifts from abstract thought to immediate sensation: where your knees are, how your breath moves, where your hands land. In modern life, mornings are often velocity-driven—alerts, headlines, micro-decisions before breakfast. The nijiriguchi offers an opposite rhythm: pause, descend, arrive.

This is one reason tea practice remains deeply relevant. The host’s gestures—placing charcoal, preparing water, warming the bowl—are not theatrical decoration. The guest’s gestures—bowing at the threshold, moving along tatami edges, receiving the bowl with care—are not empty formality. They are attentional technologies. Repetition reduces cognitive noise. Simplicity sharpens perception. The steam becomes visible again; the aroma becomes specific, not generic.

You can apply this without owning a tea room. Create a **30-second personal nijiriguchi** before stepping into your day. Put your phone down. Relax your jaw and shoulders. Inhale for four counts, exhale for six. Hold your cup with both hands and look once at the rising steam before drinking. This tiny ritual functions like a threshold: you stop being pulled by the outside and begin entering from the inside.

There is another lesson: selective carrying. A narrow entrance makes excess difficult. In tea terms, you do not bring what the room does not need. In modern terms, you do not let every input enter your morning mind. Check only the three most important messages first. Leave intentional space between commitments. Build ten-minute margins for movement. Tea calls this respect for *ma*—meaningful interval, living space between actions.

So tea is not merely conservation of old manners. It is a durable method for calibrating human attention. The nijiriguchi, though physically small, asks a profound question each morning: **How will you enter today?** With haste, or with depth? With accumulation, or with clarity? Even one bowl, received with a settled posture, can reorganize the day.

10. **間（ま）** — meaningful interval/space
11. **姿勢（しせい）** — posture; attitude
12. **一碗（いちわん）** — one bowl (of tea)
