# 港から来た土を、手の中で読み直す朝

朝の東京は二十五度前後、雲の間から日が差しています。こんな朝は、涼しさを飾るより、手に伝わる土の落ち着きで気持ちを整えてみたくなります。

## 日本語特集

唐津焼の茶碗を前にすると、まず華やかな名品というより、どこか「使うこと」に前向きな顔つきだと感じます。景色はあるのに、景色だけで終わらない。見込みの深さ、口縁のやわらかな揺れ、少し粗い土の手ざわり、鉄絵が走るときのためらいのなさ。眺めていると、これは鑑賞棚の上で完成する器ではなく、茶が入り、両手で受けられ、口が触れてはじめて本気を出す器なのだとわかってきます。今朝はこの「使われて完成する」という感覚から、唐津焼を読んでみます。

唐津焼の始まりについては細部に諸説がありますが、唐津観光協会の案内では、近年の研究として一五八〇年代ごろに波多氏の領地で焼かれたのが始まりとされ、その後、文禄・慶長の役の前後に朝鮮半島から渡った陶工たちの技術が加わって生産が広がったと紹介されています。ここで大事なのは、唐津焼が最初から茶室の中だけで閉じていたわけではないことです。港町から各地へ運ばれ、日常の器としても広く使われながら、同時に茶人たちに愛された。唐津という土地の面白さは、この二つが矛盾せずに同居しているところにあります。

その性格は、茶の湯での受け取られ方にもよく表れます。唐津観光協会は、茶の湯の世界で唐津焼が古くから愛されたこと、そして「作り手八分、使い手二分」といわれるほど用の美が重んじられてきたことを伝えています。これは、作家の仕事が足りないという意味ではありません。むしろ逆で、作り手は使われる余地を残すほど深く考えねばならない、という厳しい思想です。料理を盛る、酒を受ける、抹茶をたたえる。そのどれにも耐えるように土を選び、形を抑え、景色を語りすぎない。器が自分だけで完結しないからこそ、使い手の時間や場の空気が最後の仕上げになるのです。

私はこの感覚を、唐津焼の土っぽさの中にいちばん強く見ます。青山スクエアの工芸紹介でも、唐津焼の魅力は土の風合いと素朴な作風にあると説明されています。たしかに唐津の茶碗には、楽焼のような凝縮した密度とも、萩焼のようなやわらかな滲みとも違う、もう少し外気に開いた感じがあります。土が完全に磨き上げられていないぶん、手が入る余白がある。きれいに整いきっていないのに、乱暴ではない。その半歩ぶんの野性が、茶室の静けさに不思議とよく合います。わびとは、ただ小さく控えることではなく、整えすぎない勇気でもあるのだと教えられます。

もうひとつ面白いのは、唐津焼が「旅の記憶」を帯びた茶陶だということです。唐津港から西日本へ広がったため、かつて焼き物一般を「からつもの」と総称するほどだったと公式案内にあります。つまり唐津焼は、特定の窯場の閉じた自意識だけで育ったのではなく、人の移動、技術の移動、荷の移動のなかで名を広げた器でした。その背景を思うと、一碗の唐津茶碗の中には、山の土だけでなく、港の気配、運ばれる途中の揺れ、他土地で使われる未来まで含まれているように思えてきます。地域の茶の湯を考える土曜日に唐津を取り上げたくなるのは、土地に深く根ざしながら、同時に土地の外へ開かれているからです。

とはいえ、唐津焼をひとことで説明しきるのは難しい。絵唐津、斑唐津、朝鮮唐津など表情は幅広く、茶の湯での好まれ方も流派や時代、席の趣向によって変わります。さらに、「一井戸二楽三唐津」といった言い回しは広く知られていても、どの場でも同じ重みで共有される普遍的な物差しとは限りません。こうした揺れを残したまま見られるのも、私は唐津のよさだと思います。名物の序列で固めるより、手に取ったときに何が起こるかで語れる器だからです。

メトロポリタン美術館の所蔵品を見ると、唐津の茶碗は十七世紀や十八世紀の作例がいまも具体的な物として残り、白化粧や透明釉、絵付けの技法の違いまで読み取れます。けれど資料画像をいくら見ても、最後のところは手の記憶に及びません。どれくらい高台が指に収まるか。口縁のわずかな凹凸が、飲む前の呼吸をどう遅くするか。見込みの深さが茶の色をどう受け止めるか。茶の湯において器を学ぶとは、作品名を覚えること以上に、こうした身体の返事を育てることなのだと思います。

今朝、もし唐津焼の茶碗を思い浮かべるなら、「派手でないのになぜ残るのか」と自分に尋ねてみてください。答えはたぶん、景色が弱いからではなく、景色が使い手のために少し引いているからです。前へ出すぎないものほど、長く一緒にいられる。茶の湯では、強く主張する美しさだけでなく、相手の手の中で深まる美しさが尊ばれます。唐津焼は、そのことをずいぶん率直に教えてくれる茶碗です。港から来た土は、茶室に入ってもまだ少し外の風を含んでいる。その外気の細い通り道が、一服を息苦しくさせないのかもしれません。

## 今日の見どころ

唐津の茶碗を見るときは、絵や銘より先に「どこが使い手のために残されているか」を探してみてください。高台の持ちやすさ、口縁の当たり、土のざらりとした気配、景色の余白。完成しきった美ではなく、手に渡って完成する余地が見えてくると、唐津焼の静かな強さが急に立ち上がります。

## 小さな実践

家にある湯のみ、飯碗、マグカップのどれでもよいので、一つだけ選び、三十秒ほど黙って両手で持ってみてください。きれいかどうかを評価する代わりに、「どこに指が落ち着くか」「口をつける前に呼吸が速くなるか遅くなるか」を観察します。茶道具でなくても、器がこちらの速度を変えることはわかります。今日はその変化だけを、静かに見つけてみてください。

## Full English Edition

# Morning with a Bowl of Earth from the Port

This morning in Tokyo is around 25°C, with sunlight breaking through the clouds. On a morning like this, instead of decorating coolness, it can feel more honest to settle the mind through the grounded calm of clay in the hands.

When I sit before a Karatsu tea bowl, it rarely feels like an object that exists only to dazzle as a masterpiece. It seems, rather, strangely willing to be used. It has scenery, yet does not end in scenery. The depth of the interior, the soft wavering of the rim, the slightly rough touch of the clay, the unhesitating sweep of iron-painted brushwork. The longer one looks, the clearer it becomes that this is not a bowl completed on a display shelf. It becomes fully itself when tea enters it, when two hands receive it, when someone finally drinks from it. This morning, I want to read Karatsu ware from that feeling: that it is completed through use.

The exact beginnings of Karatsu ware are still discussed in detail, but official Karatsu guides describe recent research that places its origins around the 1580s in the territory of the Hata clan. They also explain that production expanded after techniques brought by potters from the Korean peninsula were added around the time of the Imjin War period. What matters here is that Karatsu ware was never confined only to the tearoom. It spread outward from a port town, circulated as everyday pottery, and at the same time became beloved by tea practitioners. The fascination of Karatsu lies in the fact that those two lives, ordinary use and refined tea culture, do not cancel one another out.

That character appears clearly in the way chanoyu receives it. Official Karatsu descriptions note both its long-standing esteem in the tea world and the phrase often associated with it: that a vessel is “eight parts made by the maker, two parts completed by the user.” This does not mean the potter’s work is incomplete in a careless sense. It means something much stricter. The maker must think deeply enough to leave room for use. A bowl must be able to hold food, receive sake, or contain whisked tea. Clay is chosen, form is restrained, and visual effect is prevented from speaking too loudly. Because the vessel does not end with itself, the user’s time, touch, and setting become part of its final finish.

I feel this most strongly in Karatsu’s earthiness. Aoyama Square’s traditional craft profile describes the appeal of Karatsu ware in terms of the texture of the clay and its rustic simplicity. That seems right. Karatsu bowls have a quality different from the compressed density of Raku and different again from the soft, permeable glow of Hagi. Karatsu feels slightly more open to the outside air. Because the clay is not polished into total closure, there is room for the hand to enter. It is not fully smoothed, yet it is not crude. That half-step of wildness harmonizes beautifully with the quiet of the tearoom. It suggests that wabi is not only the art of making things smaller and quieter, but also the courage not to over-refine them.

Another reason Karatsu is compelling is that it is tea pottery carrying the memory of travel. Official guides note that Karatsu ware spread widely through western Japan via Karatsu Port, and that pottery itself was once broadly called “karatsumono.” In other words, Karatsu did not grow only through the closed self-consciousness of a single kiln site. Its name expanded through the movement of people, techniques, and goods. With that in mind, a single Karatsu tea bowl begins to seem as though it contains not only mountain clay, but also the air of the harbor, the swaying of transport, and the future of being used in other places. On a Saturday devoted to regional tea culture, Karatsu feels especially appropriate because it is deeply rooted in place while also remaining open beyond place.

Even so, Karatsu ware cannot be honestly reduced to one formula. There are many expressions: E-Karatsu, Madara Karatsu, Chosen Karatsu, and more. Different schools, different periods, and different gathering styles have appreciated it in different ways. And while sayings such as “first Ido, second Raku, third Karatsu” are widely known, they should not be treated as universally binding measures in every setting. I prefer leaving that uncertainty intact. Karatsu is a kind of pottery that can be understood less through rigid ranking than through what actually happens when it enters the hand.

Museum collections such as the Met preserve concrete examples of Karatsu bowls from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, allowing us to study white slip, transparent glaze, and painted technique with care. Yet no number of images reaches the final layer of understanding. How naturally does the foot settle against the fingers? How does a slight unevenness at the rim slow the breath before drinking? How does the depth of the bowl receive the green of the tea? To study utensils in chanoyu is, I think, not merely to memorize names and classifications, but to cultivate these bodily replies.

If you imagine a Karatsu bowl this morning, try asking yourself: why does something so unshowy remain in the mind? The answer may be that its scenery is not weak, but tactful. It steps back slightly for the sake of the user. Things that do not push too hard can stay with us for a very long time. In tea, beauty is not only the beauty that asserts itself, but also the beauty that deepens inside another person’s hands. Karatsu ware teaches that with unusual directness. The clay that came from the port still seems to carry a trace of outside wind even after it enters the tearoom. Perhaps that narrow passage of outer air is what keeps a bowl of tea from ever feeling closed or suffocating.

## What to Notice Today

When you look at a Karatsu bowl, do not begin with decoration or prestige. First ask what has been left open for the user: the ease of the foot, the touch of the rim, the rough breath of the clay, the silence within the visual field. Once you notice the space left for completion through use, the bowl’s quiet strength comes alive.

## Small Practice

Choose one ordinary vessel at home, a cup, a rice bowl, or a mug, and hold it silently with both hands for about thirty seconds. Instead of deciding whether it is beautiful, observe where your fingers settle and whether your breathing becomes faster or slower before you would drink from it. Even outside tea, vessels change our pace. Today, simply notice that change.

## Vocabulary

1. **唐津焼**（からつやき） - Karatsu ware  
   Usage: A traditional pottery style from Saga; often discussed in relation to tea ceramics and everyday utility.

2. **茶碗**（ちゃわん） - tea bowl  
   Usage: In chanoyu this means more than a bowl; it is a vessel to be received, turned, held, and read with the hands.

3. **用の美**（ようのび） - beauty in use  
   Usage: A key phrase for objects whose beauty emerges through practical use, not display alone.

4. **高台**（こうだい） - foot ring, bowl foot  
   Usage: Useful when noticing how a bowl sits, balances, and settles in the hand.

5. **口縁**（こうえん） - rim  
   Usage: The edge that meets the lips; important when describing how a vessel guides drinking.

6. **見込み**（みこみ） - interior of the bowl  
   Usage: Often used when observing the depth, color, or space inside a tea bowl.

7. **鉄絵**（てつえ） - iron-painted design  
   Usage: Refers to brushwork painted with iron-based pigment under or with glaze.

8. **白化粧**（しろげしょう） - white slip  
   Usage: A ceramic term for a white clay slip applied to the surface before glazing.

9. **釉**（ゆう） - glaze  
   Usage: A basic but important word when reading the surface and atmosphere of pottery.

10. **登り窯**（のぼりがま） - climbing kiln  
    Usage: A traditional stepped kiln built on a slope; often associated with historical firing methods.

11. **わび**（わび） - restrained, unforced tea aesthetic  
    Usage: Best used carefully; it suggests disciplined simplicity, not mere plainness.

12. **土もの**（つちもの） - earthenware, clay-based pottery  
    Usage: A common way to speak about pottery with an earthy body and tactile presence.
