# 冷え枯れの気配を、一碗へ移す

朝の一碗の前に、華やかさを減らすことでかえって深まる美しさについて、武野紹鴎の静かな工夫をたどってみましょう。

今朝の東京は雨の気配を含んだ26度ほどの湿った朝で、少し冷えた言葉がかえって心を整えるという紹鴎の感覚を思い出させます。

## 日本語特集

茶道史の大きな名前をたどると、どうしても村田珠光、武野紹鴎、千利休という一直線の系譜として覚えたくなります。もちろん、その流れは大切です。けれども今朝は、紹鴎を「利休以前の名人」として整理するよりも、ひとつの感覚を別の器へ移しかえた人として見つめてみたいのです。その感覚とは、歌や連歌の世界で語られた「冷え枯れた」美しさでした。

表千家の解説でよく知られる『山上宗二記』の一節に、紹鴎の言葉として「連歌之仕様ハ枯かしけ寒かれと云（中略）茶ノ湯ノ果ハ如此有度物をな」という趣旨が伝えられています。大意としては、連歌の境地が「枯れ、寒やかである」なら、茶の湯の行きつくところもまたそのようでありたい、ということです。ここで大切なのは、紹鴎が茶の湯を文学に変えたということではありません。むしろ逆で、歌において育った感受性を、湯の音、道具の取り合わせ、席の沈黙へと移しかえようとしたところに、彼の歴史的な独自性があります。

表千家の別の案内によれば、紹鴎は三十歳頃まで連歌に親しみ、三条西実隆から『詠歌大概』の序を伝授されたと伝えられます。室町後期から戦国にかけて、堺の町衆文化のなかでは、茶と文芸はまったく無縁の別世界ではありませんでした。だから紹鴎の試みは、道具の趣味替え以上のものです。豪奢な唐物の価値を知らない人ではなく、それを知り尽くしたうえで、そこへ白木の釣瓶や青竹の蓋置のような、肩の力の抜けた見立てを差し込んだ。表千家は、紹鴎が名物を六十種も所持する一方で、こうした白木の美を茶の湯に生かしたと説明しています。つまり紹鴎の「わび」は、貧しさの代用品ではなく、見慣れた価値の温度を意識的に少し下げる仕事だったのです。

この「温度を下げる」という感覚が、私はとても朝向きだと思います。早朝の茶室に入ると、まだ空間は一日の熱を帯びきっていません。声を張らず、道具を多く語らず、まず湯の気配から場を立ち上げていく。そのとき、紹鴎のいう「冷え枯れた」気配は、単なる古びや貧相とはまるで違うものとして感じられます。削ぎ落とされたものの奥に、むしろ神経の細やかさが増していく。にぎやかな美を否定するのではなく、それとは別の深まり方を選ぶ。その選択が、のちのわび茶の土台のひとつになりました。

東京国立博物館に伝わる紹鴎作の竹茶杓の解説を読むと、その姿は「すらりと伸びた美しい茶杓」とされ、象牙の茶杓を手本にしたであろうことまで言及されています。ここにも、紹鴎らしい移しかえがあります。最初から粗野なものだけを尊んだのではなく、洗練された手本を知ったうえで、それを竹へ移し、茶席の静けさにふさわしい線へと変えていく。だから紹鴎の歴史を語るとき、「侘びの人」という要約だけでは足りません。彼は、価値を捨てた人というより、価値の響き方を変えた人でした。

同時に、ここは少し慎み深く読んでおきたいところでもあります。私たちが今日「紹鴎の思想」として口にするものの多くは、紹鴎自身の自筆理論書ではなく、後代に伝わった言葉や、弟子筋の記録、家元の整理を通して受け取っています。とくに『山上宗二記』に見える語りは非常に重要ですが、そこには引用や伝聞の層もあります。だからこそ面白い、とも言えます。茶の湯の歴史は、完璧に一人称で閉じた思想史ではなく、弟子が聞き、後世が書き留め、各家が読み継いできた声の重なりだからです。

それでも、紹鴎を読むときに確かに残る輪郭があります。にぎやかなものを一度静めること。高価なものと素朴なものを対立としてではなく、呼吸の調整として組み合わせること。茶室に入った人の心拍を半歩だけ遅くすること。こうした働きが、のちの利休の徹底へつながっていったのだと思います。利休はしばしば結論として語られますが、その前段には、場の温度をそっと下げ、言葉の詩情を道具の線へ移す紹鴎の工夫がありました。

今朝、もし紹鴎の仕事を身近に感じたいなら、「地味なものを選ぶ」ことより、「美しさの音量を少し下げる」ことを意識してみるとよいかもしれません。茶の湯は、ときに何を足すかより、何を少し静めるかで深くなります。紹鴎の歴史的な大きさは、その静め方を、一時の趣味ではなく一つの文化の方向として示したところにあります。冷え枯れた気配とは、命の乏しさではありません。余計な熱を引かせたあとに、なお残るものの強さです。

## 今日の見どころ

紹鴎の名が出るときは、「質素だった」という説明だけで終わらせず、何の熱を少し下げたのかを考えてみてください。唐物を知ったうえで白木を見るのか、詩を知ったうえで沈黙を聴くのか。その一段深い比較のなかに、紹鴎らしさがあります。

## 小さな実践

今朝は机の上から、いちばん目立つものを一つだけ脇へ移してみてください。そして残った景色を三十秒眺めます。何かを減らしたことで、かえって見えやすくなった線や余白があるかを確かめてみましょう。特別な道具は要りません。片づけの練習ではなく、音量を下げたときに立ち上がる美しさを観察する、小さな稽古です。

## Full English Edition

# Carrying the Feeling of Cool Withered Poetry into a Bowl of Tea

Before your morning bowl, let us spend time with the kind of beauty that deepens not by becoming more ornate, but by becoming quieter. Today’s guide is Takeno Jōō.

On this damp Tokyo morning, with rain lingering in the air and the temperature around 26°C, it feels especially natural to remember Jōō’s intuition that a slightly cooled, restrained mood can steady the heart.

When we trace the great names of tea history, it is tempting to memorize them as a neat line: Murata Jukō, Takeno Jōō, Sen no Rikyū. That lineage matters. But this morning I would rather not place Jōō on the page merely as “the important master before Rikyū.” I want to see him as someone who carried one sensibility from one art into another. The sensibility was the “cool, withered” beauty spoken of in poetry and linked verse.

In a well-known passage preserved in *Yamanoue Sōji ki* and introduced by Omotesenke, a saying attributed to Jōō explains that if the ideal of linked verse is to be “withered” and “chilled,” then the ultimate state of tea should also be something like that. The point is not that he turned tea into literature. In a sense, it is the opposite. He tried to move a poetic sensitivity into the sound of boiling water, the combination of utensils, and the quiet of the room. That act of transfer is one of his great historical achievements.

Omotesenke’s explanatory materials also say that Jōō was deeply involved with linked verse until around the age of thirty and that he is said to have received the preface to Fujiwara no Teika’s *Eiga Taigai* through Sanjōnishi Sanetaka. In the merchant culture of Sakai during the late Muromachi and Sengoku periods, tea and literary culture were not sealed off from one another. That is why Jōō’s work was more than a change in taste. He was not a man ignorant of prestige objects. He knew them thoroughly. Yet into a world that prized famous Chinese wares, he inserted things such as an unpainted wooden well-bucket used as a water container and a fresh green bamboo lid rest. Omotesenke describes him as a man who owned some sixty famous utensils while also bringing the beauty of plain unfinished wood into tea. His wabi was not a substitute for poverty. It was a deliberate lowering of the temperature of established value.

That phrase, “lowering the temperature,” feels especially suited to the morning. When one enters a tearoom early in the day, the space has not yet absorbed the heat and noise of many hours. One does not need to raise the voice. One does not need to explain every utensil. The room begins first with the presence of water and kettle. In such an atmosphere, the “cool and withered” quality associated with Jōō feels nothing like mere shabbiness or lack. It is a state in which, after things are pared down, sensitivity becomes finer. It does not deny more vivid beauty. It chooses another path toward depth. That choice helped create one of the foundations of what would later be called wabicha.

An explanation from the Tokyo National Museum for a bamboo tea scoop attributed to Jōō describes it as “beautifully slender and extended,” and even suggests that its model may have been an ivory tea scoop. Here again we see Jōō’s characteristic act of transfer. He did not simply praise rough materials from the beginning. He knew refined models, then translated them into bamboo and into a line suitable to the stillness of the tea room. That is why calling him only “a master of wabi” is too blunt. He was not merely someone who rejected value. He changed the way value resonates.

At the same time, this is an area where honesty matters. Much of what we speak of today as “Jōō’s thought” comes to us not through a self-contained theoretical treatise written in his own hand, but through later transmissions, records by students, and the interpretive work of tea schools. The testimony in *Yamanoue Sōji ki* is extremely important, yet it also contains layers of quotation and reported speech. In a way, that makes tea history more interesting, not less. It is not a perfectly closed intellectual system spoken only in the first person. It is a culture of remembered voices, heard by disciples, written down later, and reread across generations.

Even so, a clear outline remains. Jōō quieted what was too loud. He placed prestigious things and humble things together not as a simple opposition, but as a matter of breath and balance. He seems to have known how to slow the heartbeat of a room by half a step. I think that is one reason his work matters so much for what came after. Rikyū is often spoken of as a conclusion, but before that conclusion there was Jōō’s gentler work of lowering the temperature of the setting and carrying poetic awareness into the line of utensils and the hush of practice.

If you want to feel Jōō’s work near you this morning, do not focus only on “choosing plain things.” Instead, try noticing how beauty changes when its volume is turned down slightly. Tea often becomes deeper not through addition, but through the soft calming of what is already there. Jōō’s historical importance lies in having shown that this calming could become not a passing preference, but a cultural direction. The cool withered quality is not lifelessness. It is the strength of what remains after excess heat has been drawn away.

## What to Notice Today

When Jōō’s name comes up, do not stop at the phrase “he preferred simplicity.” Ask instead what kind of heat he lowered. Was it the heat of luxury, or the heat of explanation, or the heat of display? Jōō becomes clearer when read through that quieter comparison.

## Small Practice

Move just one visually dominant object off your desk this morning. Then look at what remains for thirty seconds. Notice whether a line, interval, or patch of emptiness becomes easier to see once a little visual heat has been removed. No special equipment is needed. This is not tidying as productivity. It is a small lesson in perceiving the beauty that rises when volume is lowered.
