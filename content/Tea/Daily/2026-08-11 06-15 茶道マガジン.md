# 名を与えるという稽古――小堀遠州と中興名物の朝

朝の一碗に向かう前に、ものの形だけでなく、そのものに誰がどんな言葉を託したのかを静かにたどってみましょう。

今朝の東京は曇り、雨の気配を含む移ろいやすい空模様が、定まらぬものにそっと名を与える茶の心を思わせます。

## 日本語特集

小堀遠州の茶を語るとき、よく「綺麗さび」という言葉が先に立ちます。たしかに遠州は、古田織部に学んだあと、江戸初期の新しい秩序にふさわしい、伸びやかで端正な茶の湯を形づくった人物として記憶されています。けれども今朝、静かに見つめたいのは、遠州が茶の湯にもたらした美意識そのものよりも、むしろ「名づける」という仕事です。茶入や茶碗に和歌や古典に由来することばを与えることによって、彼は器物を単なる道具以上の存在へと移し替えていきました。

桃山の時代まで、名物といえば、長い伝来をもち、中国由来の唐物を中心に価値づけられることが多くありました。もちろんその流れは江戸に入ってすぐ消えたわけではありません。しかし、平和な時代が定まり、茶の湯が武家や町人、寺院や公家のあいだへ細やかに広がっていくと、「何を尊ぶか」の基準も少しずつ変わります。遠州はその変化の只中にいました。彼が日本で作られた茶入や茶碗に歌銘を与えたという事実は、国産の器物を唐物の代用品としてではなく、日本語の記憶をまとった一個の景色として見立て直したことを示しています。

ここで大切なのは、名が説明ではないということです。歌銘は、器の用途を合理的に示す札ではありません。むしろ、器の色、面のやわらかな揺れ、釉の薄いかげり、手に取ったときの余白の感じを、ことばの側からそっと照らす小さな灯です。たとえば、同じ白でも、ただ「白い」と言うのではなく、秋の月を思わせるのか、朝霧の淡さを思わせるのかによって、客の見る速度は変わります。名が先にあることで、器は鑑賞されるだけでなく、心のなかで一度読まれるようになるのです。

こうした仕事は、侘びを裏切る華やかさだったのでしょうか。そこは少し慎重に考えたいところです。遠州は利休の侘びを否定して別の道へ進んだ、というより、利休以後の茶の湯が置かれた社会の変化に応じて、侘びを別の明るさのもとで持続させようとした、と見るほうが自然です。織部の強い歪みや際立つ意匠が戦国の気分を残していたとすれば、遠州の均衡と気品は、泰平の世の呼吸に寄り添っていました。だから彼の命名は、飾りの追加というより、道具を時代の言葉へ通訳する行為だったと言えるかもしれません。

のちに松平不昧は『古今名物類聚』のなかで名物を整理し、遠州が見立て、選び、名を与えた器物群を「中興名物」として位置づけました。つまり遠州は、ただ名人であっただけでなく、何を後世へ受け渡すべきかを選別する目そのものになったのです。茶の湯の歴史では、だれが点てたか、だれが教えたかに目が向きがちですが、同じくらい大切なのは、だれが「これを覚えておきたい一品」と見なしたかということです。名づけは、記憶の器をつくる仕事でもありました。

茶席で道具の名を聞くとき、私たちはつい、その由来を知識として回収したくなります。しかし本当は、名を知ったあとに少しだけ沈黙するほうがよいのかもしれません。その沈黙のあいだに、器は説明から離れ、景色へ戻ります。遠州が名づけたかったのは、道具の格付けだけではなく、客が一碗に近づくときの心の歩幅だったのではないでしょうか。ものに名を与えるとは、ものを見る速度を整えることでもあるのです。

## 今日の見どころ

- 茶碗や茶入の「名」を聞いたあと、すぐに意味を解こうとせず、まず色の明るさ、口縁の薄さ、景色の偏りを一呼吸ぶん見てみる。
- 名が器を派手にするのではなく、見る側の注意を細く深くしているかどうかに注目する。
- 「唐物か、和物か」という分類よりも、その器がどの時代にどんな言葉をまとわされたのかを想像してみる。

## 小さな実践

家にある湯のみ、茶碗、グラス、あるいは普段使いの器をひとつ選び、三十秒ほど黙って眺めてください。そのあと説明的な名前ではなく、季節や景色に寄せた短い呼び名を一つだけ考えます。たとえば「朝ぐもり」「薄月」「木蔭」など、正解のない名前でかまいません。名づけたあと、もう一度その器を見て、見え方がどう変わるかを確かめてみましょう。特別な道具は不要ですし、書き留めなくても大丈夫です。大切なのは、見ることと言葉のあいだに、小さな往復をつくることです。

## Full English Edition

### Giving a Thing Its Name: Kobori Enshu and the Morning of Chuko-meibutsu

Before you face your morning bowl of tea, try tracing not only the shape of an object, but also the question of who entrusted words to it, and why.

This morning in Tokyo is cloudy, with rain in the air. That unsettled sky feels close to one of tea culture’s quietest acts: giving a name to something that does not stay still.

When people speak of Kobori Enshu, the phrase that often appears first is *kirei-sabi*, “elegant simplicity” or “beautiful austerity.” That is fair enough. Enshu is remembered as the early Edo-period tea master who, after learning from Furuta Oribe, shaped a form of chanoyu suited to a new age of stability: poised, balanced, and expansive in feeling. But the quieter work worth noticing this morning is not only his aesthetic style. It is his act of naming.

By giving poetic names derived from waka and classical literature to tea containers and tea bowls, Enshu helped move utensils from the category of mere implement into the realm of remembered presence. He did not simply classify things. He altered how they could be seen.

Up through the Momoyama period, celebrated tea objects were often valued through long pedigree, especially as imported Chinese objects, the *karamono* that carried prestige through lineage and transmission. That hierarchy did not disappear the moment the Edo period began. Yet once peace had settled in, and chanoyu spread more delicately among warriors, townspeople, temples, and court circles, the standards of what deserved admiration began to shift. Enshu stood directly inside that shift.

The fact that he gave poetic names to Japanese-made tea bowls and tea containers matters because it suggests a change in cultural attention. These objects were not treated as substitutes for imported prestige. They were re-seen as complete presences of their own, clothed in the memory of Japanese language. A name drawn from poetry allowed a vessel to enter an atmosphere, not just an inventory.

That point is easy to miss if we think of naming as explanation. A poetic name is not a practical label. It does not tell you what an object does in the way a museum tag might. Instead, it works like a small lamp. It casts light from the side. It helps the viewer notice the pale hesitation of glaze, the softness of a contour, the restraint of a surface, the way emptiness gathers in the hand. The same pale bowl may be merely “white” until a name makes it feel like autumn moonlight, or morning mist, or a clearing after rain. Once the name is present, the object is no longer only looked at. It is, in some quiet way, read.

Was this a betrayal of wabi, then? It is better not to say that too quickly. Enshu did not simply reject Rikyu’s world and replace it with ornament. A more careful reading is that he tried to sustain the spirit of wabi under changed social conditions and under a different quality of light. If Oribe’s daring asymmetry still carried some of the nervous energy of the age of war, Enshu’s balance and refinement suited an era of political calm. In that sense, his naming was not decorative excess added from outside. It was a translation of utensils into the language of a new age.

Later, Matsudaira Fumai systematized the world of celebrated tea utensils in his *Kokon Meibutsu Ruiju*, and the objects selected, appreciated, or named through Enshu’s eye came to be understood as *chuko-meibutsu*, “rediscovered” or “reestablished” famous utensils. This means Enshu was not only a master of taste. He became, in effect, a standard of remembrance: someone whose discernment helped decide what later generations should continue to hold in mind.

In tea history, attention often goes to who prepared tea, who taught it, and who founded a school. Those are important questions. But another question matters just as much: who recognized that a certain object was worth carrying forward into memory? Naming is part of that work. To give a thing a name is also to build a vessel for remembrance around it.

So when you hear the name of a tea utensil in a tearoom, it may be worth resisting the urge to convert it immediately into information. It may be better to let a small silence follow. In that pause, the object can slip free from explanation and return to being a landscape. Perhaps what Enshu truly shaped through naming was not only the status of utensils, but the pace of mind with which a guest approaches them. To name a thing is to regulate the speed at which it is seen.

### Today’s Highlights

- After hearing the name of a tea bowl or tea container, resist interpreting it at once. For one breath, first notice the brightness of its color, the fineness of its rim, and any asymmetry in its surface landscape.
- Consider whether the name makes the object more ornate, or instead narrows and deepens the viewer’s attention.
- Look beyond the simple distinction between *karamono* and *wamono*, and imagine when the utensil acquired its name and what language its age placed around it.

### A Small Practice

Choose one cup, bowl, glass, or other everyday vessel at home and look at it silently for about thirty seconds. Then give it a short name drawn from a season or a landscape rather than a descriptive label—perhaps “morning cloud,” “pale moon,” or “tree shade.” There is no correct answer. Look at the vessel once more after naming it and notice whether your way of seeing has changed. No special utensil is needed, and you do not have to write the name down. What matters is creating a small movement back and forth between seeing and language.

## Vocabulary

1. **名づけ（なづけ）** — naming
   A reflective act of giving a thing a meaningful name rather than a label.
2. **中興名物（ちゅうこうめいぶつ）** — “rediscovered” or reestablished famous utensils
   Used for utensils later recognized and classified through Edo-period connoisseurship.
3. **歌銘（かめい）** — poetic name
   A name drawn from waka or classical literature and attached to an object.
4. **綺麗さび（きれいさび）** — elegant simplicity
   Often associated with Kobori Enshu’s refined aesthetic, though interpretations vary.
5. **唐物（からもの）** — imported Chinese object
   In tea history, often refers to prestigious imported utensils or art objects.
6. **和物（わもの）** — Japanese-made object
   Useful when contrasting domestic utensils with imported ones.
7. **景色（けしき）** — surface “landscape” or visual character
   A tea term for the scenic qualities seen on a bowl or utensil.
8. **伝来（でんらい）** — transmitted lineage
   Refers to an object’s history of ownership and preservation.
9. **見立て（みたて）** — aesthetic re-seeing or reinterpretation
   Not simply “comparison,” but a creative act of recognizing new meaning.
10. **侘び（わび）** — cultivated austerity, inward quiet
   A central but nuanced tea term that should not be reduced to “simple” or “poor.”
11. **気品（きひん）** — elegance, dignity
   Useful for describing Enshu-style balance without implying mere luxury.
12. **余白（よはく）** — intentional empty space
   Can describe visual, spatial, or emotional openness in tea aesthetics.
