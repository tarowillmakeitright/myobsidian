# 土の粒から一服へ——信楽を歩き、見立ての始まりを読む

晩秋の土曜日、滋賀県甲賀市の信楽へ想像の旅に出ます。目当ては完成した名碗を数えることではありません。土に混じる長石の粒、窯へ続く坂、壺や甕を作った手、そして日用の器を茶席へ迎えた眼差し。その順にたどると、茶陶は最初から「茶道具」として生まれるだけではなく、土地の仕事と茶人の見立てが出会って成立することが見えてきます。

## 今日の見どころ

- 信楽は瀬戸・越前・常滑・丹波・備前と並ぶ日本六古窯の一つで、中世から窯業が続く土地です。
- 室町時代後期以降、壺や甕などの日用雑器が水指などへ見立てられ、信楽は茶陶として評価を得ました。
- 信楽らしい石はぜ、蟹の目、緋色は、土に含まれる石英・長石・鉄分と薪窯の火が作る景色です。欠点の一覧ではなく、土地と焼成の記録として観察します。

## 日本語特集

信楽の器を前にすると、まず表面の粒へ目が向きます。なめらかに均された白い面ではなく、小石のような長石がのぞき、ところどころに裂け目やガラス質の光が生まれています。甲賀市の電子展示は、石英の粒の周囲に亀裂が生じる「石はぜ」、長石が熔けて粒状のガラスになる「蟹の目」、素地の鉄分が赤茶色を帯びる「火色（緋色）」を、信楽の土と薪窯による窯変として説明しています。

この景色は、職人が筆で描いた模様とは違います。しかし偶然だけでもありません。どの土を選び、どの厚さに成形し、窯のどこへ置き、どのように火を通すか。人の判断が自然条件へ働きかけ、そのすべてを完全には支配しないところに焼締陶の表情が現れます。器を見るとは、作者の意図を当てることではなく、土・火・時間・手の共同作業を読むことです。

文化庁の文化遺産オンラインによれば、信楽は十三世紀には成立したと考えられる中世の窯場です。初期には壺、甕、鉢など、暮らしのための器が作られました。室町時代後期以降、侘び茶の広まりとともに、備前や信楽の日用雑器が水指などへ見立てられ、信楽は茶陶としての評価を確立していきます。

ここに茶の湯の大切な転換があります。豪華な材料や専用の意匠だけが美を保証するのではありません。もともと水や穀物を収める器に、茶席で水を蓄える役割と新しい美を見いだす。見立ては、物の来歴を消して別物に装うことではなく、元の用途を感じながら、別の関係へ置き直す眼の仕事です。

水指は点前の中心で激しく動く道具ではありません。それでも席中の水を預かり、釜の湯を支える静かな要です。粗い土肌の信楽水指を清浄な水の容れ物として置くと、荒さと清らかさが対立せず、一つの景色になります。土の粒、蓋の材、棚や畳との取り合わせによって、客は水そのものを直接見なくても、その冷たさや深さを想像します。

信楽の歴史は茶陶だけに閉じません。甲賀市は、鎌倉時代後半に常滑の影響を受けて生産が始まり、穴窯から連房式登り窯へ移るなかで製品も変化したと紹介しています。江戸時代には、将軍家への献上用宇治茶を詰める腰白茶壺や、施釉した小物も作られました。産地が続いたのは、一つの名品を守り続けたからではなく、時代の需要に応じて技術と品目を組み替えたからです。

町を歩くなら、店先の完成品だけでなく、工房、窯跡、坂道、土を運んだ経路へ目を向けたいものです。甲賀市の調査報告は、採土、製土、成形、施釉、焼成、販売まで、多様な専門職が網の目のように結びつき、暮らしと密接に関わる産地の姿を伝えます。一つの水指の背後には、一人の陶工だけでなく、町全体の分業と記憶があります。

旅先で器を選ぶとき、「信楽らしい色か」だけで判断しない方法があります。まず一か所、土の粒や灰の流れを見つける。次に器を少し離して輪郭を見る。そして何を入れ、どこへ置けば、その表情が生きるかを考える。名札から価値を受け取るのではなく、器と自分の暮らしの間に関係を作るのです。

茶の湯が日用の器を迎え入れた歴史は、私たちにも小さな自由を残します。高価な茶陶がなくても、普段使いの器を丁寧に洗い、季節の枝を一つ入れ、光のよい場所へ置けば、ものの見え方は変わります。ただし、何でも茶道具になるという意味ではありません。寸法、安全性、清潔さ、扱いやすさを確かめたうえで、なぜこの器を今日ここへ置くのかを説明できること。それが見立てを思いつきで終わらせない稽古になります。

信楽を歩く旅は、完成品を買う旅から、ものが生まれる条件を読む旅へ私たちを連れ出します。土の粒から窯の炎へ、日用の壺から水指へ、工房から茶席へ。移動するたびに器の意味は変わります。一服の前で立ち止まり、その器がどこから来て、いま何を支えているかを考えるとき、旅は茶室の中でも続いています。

## 小さな実践

手元の焼物を一つ選び、三十秒ずつ三つの距離から見ます。まず指先ほど近くで土や釉の粒、次に腕一本分離れて輪郭、最後に部屋の入口から周囲との関係を観察します。「素材」「火の痕跡」「今日の役割」を一語ずつ書き、その器をいつもの場所から少しだけ移してください。用途は無理に変えず、置き方を変えることで見立ての入口を稽古します。

## Complete English Edition

# From Grains of Clay to a Bowl of Tea — Walking Shigaraki and Reading the Beginnings of Mitate

On this late-autumn Saturday, we make an imagined journey to Shigaraki in Kōka, Shiga Prefecture. Our aim is not to count celebrated vessels. We will follow grains of feldspar in the clay, slopes leading to kilns, hands that formed jars for daily life, and the gaze that welcomed such wares into the tea room. In this order, tea ceramics appear not only as objects designed for chanoyu from the start, but as the meeting of regional labor and the tea practitioner’s act of creative re-seeing.

## Today’s Highlights

- Shigaraki is one of Japan’s Six Ancient Kilns, alongside Seto, Echizen, Tokoname, Tamba, and Bizen, and has sustained pottery production since the medieval period.
- From the late Muromachi period onward, everyday jars and storage vessels were creatively repurposed as fresh-water containers and other tea utensils, helping establish Shigaraki’s reputation as tea ware.
- Stone bursts, glassy “crab’s eyes,” and warm fire color record the meeting of quartz, feldspar, iron-bearing clay, and wood-kiln firing. Read them as traces of place and process rather than a list of defects.

## Feature

Before a Shigaraki vessel, the eye first approaches its particles. The surface is not a smooth, uniform white. Feldspar appears like small stones; cracks form around grains; here and there the clay carries a glassy glint. An online exhibition by the City of Kōka explains three characteristic kiln effects: *ishihaze*, cracks around quartz particles; *kani no me*, feldspar melted into bead-like glass; and *hiiro*, the reddish-brown fire color produced as iron in the body changes during firing.

These effects are not painted decoration, but neither are they mere accidents. The potter chooses clay, thickness, form, placement in the kiln, and a way of carrying fire through the chamber. Human judgment meets natural conditions without fully controlling them. To look at the vessel is therefore not simply to guess the maker’s intention. It is to read a collaboration among earth, fire, duration, and hand.

Japan’s Cultural Heritage Online dates the formation of Shigaraki as a representative medieval kiln to the thirteenth century. Its early production centered on jars, large storage vessels, and bowls for daily use. From the late Muromachi period, as wabi tea flourished, ordinary Bizen and Shigaraki wares were reimagined as fresh-water containers and other tea utensils, and Shigaraki gained recognition as tea ceramics.

This marks an important turn in chanoyu. Expensive materials and purpose-made ornament are not the only guarantees of beauty. A vessel once used to hold water or grain can be seen anew as the source of water in a tea gathering. *Mitate* does not erase an object’s former life or disguise it as something else. It senses that earlier purpose while placing the object into a new relationship.

The fresh-water container is not the most dramatically moving utensil in the procedure. Yet it quietly holds the water that supports the kettle and the preparation of tea. When a rough Shigaraki vessel contains clear water, roughness and purity need not oppose each other. Through clay texture, lid material, shelf, and tatami, the guest imagines coolness and depth even when the water itself is scarcely visible.

Shigaraki’s history extends beyond tea ware. Kōka’s cultural-history resources describe production beginning in the late Kamakura period under influence from Tokoname, followed by a shift from tunnel kilns to multi-chamber climbing kilns and changing product lines. In the Edo period, Shigaraki made white-waisted tea jars for Uji tea presented to the shogunal house, as well as smaller glazed wares. The region endured not by preserving a single famous form unchanged, but by recombining skills and products as needs changed.

When walking the town, look beyond finished pieces in shops toward workshops, kiln sites, slopes, and routes once used to move clay. A City of Kōka research report describes a dense network of specialized work—clay extraction, preparation, forming, glazing, firing, and sale—closely connected to everyday life. Behind one water jar stands not only a named potter but the division of labor and memory of an entire community.

There is another way to select a vessel than asking whether its color looks characteristically Shigaraki. First find one grain, ash trail, or mark of fire. Then step back and read the whole outline. Finally ask what the vessel might hold and where its character could become clear. Instead of receiving value from a label, create a relationship between the object and your own life.

The history of chanoyu welcoming ordinary vessels leaves us a modest freedom. Even without costly tea ceramics, washing an everyday vessel carefully, placing one seasonal branch in it, and moving it into good light can change how it is seen. This does not mean that anything can automatically become a tea utensil. Dimensions, safety, cleanliness, and ease of handling matter. You should also be able to say why this object belongs here today. That explanation keeps *mitate* from becoming a passing whim.

A journey through Shigaraki shifts travel from buying finished objects to reading the conditions that produced them: from particles of clay to kiln fire, from a daily jar to a water container, from workshop to tea room. With each movement, the vessel’s meaning changes. When we pause before tea and consider where the utensil came from and what it now supports, the journey continues inside the tea room.

## A Small Practice

Choose one ceramic object at hand and view it for thirty seconds from each of three distances. First, look closely at particles in the clay or glaze. Next, step one arm’s length away and see its outline. Finally, view it from the doorway in relation to the room. Write one word for its material, one for a trace of fire, and one for its role today. Move it slightly from its usual place without forcing a new function. Practice *mitate* by changing the relationship of placement.

## 今日のことば — Tea Vocabulary (12)

1. **信楽焼（しがらきやき / Shigaraki-yaki）** — pottery made in the Shigaraki area of Shiga Prefecture
2. **六古窯（ろっこよう / rokkoyō）** — the Six Ancient Kilns of Japan
3. **焼締（やきしめ / yakishime）** — high-fired unglazed stoneware
4. **長石（ちょうせき / chōseki）** — feldspar, a mineral that can melt into glassy accents in firing
5. **石はぜ（いしはぜ / ishihaze）** — cracks or bursts formed around stone or quartz particles in the clay
6. **蟹の目（かにのめ / kani no me）** — bead-like glassy spots produced by melting feldspar
7. **緋色（ひいろ / hiiro）** — a warm reddish fire color on the clay body
8. **窯変（ようへん / yōhen）** — changes in color or surface created during firing
9. **穴窯（あながま / anagama）** — a single-chamber tunnel kiln built into a slope
10. **登り窯（のぼりがま / noborigama）** — a multi-chamber climbing kiln built along a slope
11. **水指（みずさし / mizusashi）** — the fresh-water container used in tea preparation
12. **見立て（みたて / mitate）** — creatively seeing and using an object in a new role
