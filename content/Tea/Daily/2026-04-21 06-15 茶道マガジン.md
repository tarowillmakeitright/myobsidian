## 日本語版｜朝の特集

### 今日のテーマ
**「湯の温度」が一碗を変える——同じ抹茶でも、なぜ味わいが違うのか**

茶道を学び始めると、多くの人がまず道具の違いに目を向けます。茶碗の形、茶筅の穂数、茶杓の銘、釜の肌。どれも確かに大切です。けれど、稽古を重ねるほどに気づくのは、**見えない条件こそ、味と場の空気を大きく左右する**という事実です。

その代表が「湯の温度」です。

「薄茶はだいたいこのくらい」「濃茶はもう少し熱く」——こうした目安はありますが、実際の席では、季節・湿度・茶の銘柄・客の年齢・菓子との相性で、最適点は微妙に動きます。温度は単なる数値ではなく、**亭主の思いやりが最も繊細に現れる調整項目**なのです。

たとえば同じ抹茶でも、湯が高温すぎると渋みが立ち、香りの輪郭が鋭くなりすぎることがあります。逆に低すぎると、甘みは出やすい一方で、立ち上がりの香りが弱く、全体が平板に感じられることもある。つまり温度は、味を「良くする」一本のつまみではなく、**どの個性を前に出すかを選ぶミキサー**のような役割を持っています。

茶の湯の美学で言えば、これは「加減」の技です。やりすぎず、足りなさすぎず、一期一会の相手に合わせて整える。点前の動きが正確であることと同じくらい、あるいはそれ以上に、加減の読みは稽古の核心にあります。

---

### 温度を読む三つの視点

#### 1) 季節の体感を先に読む
同じ20℃でも、春先の20℃と梅雨前の20℃では体感が違います。乾いた空気の日は湯気が軽く、湿度が高い日は熱がこもって感じられる。客が着席して最初に受け取る印象は、茶そのものより先に「空気感」です。だからこそ、**温度は季節の翻訳**だと考えると判断しやすくなります。

#### 2) 菓子との対話で決める
甘みの強い主菓子の後は、少し輪郭を立てた一服が心地よいことがあります。反対に、香りの繊細な干菓子なら、湯の角をやわらげて、茶の柔らかな旨みを前に出すほうが余韻が整う場合もある。茶と菓子は「セット」ではなく「会話」です。湯温はその会話のテンポを決めます。

#### 3) 一服目と二服目を同じにしない
複数人の席では、最初の一服と最後の一服で、客の口内温度や集中の深さが変わります。最初は緊張をほどく設計、後半は余韻を残す設計へ。ほんのわずかな湯温差で、席全体の物語が自然につながります。

## English Version | Morning Feature

### Today’s Theme
**How Water Temperature Transforms a Bowl — Why the Same Matcha Can Taste Different**

When people begin learning sadō, they usually focus on visible differences: the shape of the tea bowl, the number of whisk tines, the style of the scoop, the character of the kettle. All of these matter. But with sustained practice, a deeper truth appears: **invisible conditions often shape taste and atmosphere even more strongly than visible tools**.

One of the most important is water temperature.

We do have common rules of thumb—“around this level for usucha,” “a bit hotter for koicha.” Yet in a real gathering, the sweet spot moves subtly depending on season, humidity, tea cultivar, guest profile, and pairing with sweets. Temperature is not just a numeric setting; it is often **the most delicate expression of the host’s consideration**.

With the same matcha, overheated water can push bitterness forward and sharpen aromatic edges too aggressively. Water that is too cool may reveal sweetness, but can flatten aromatic lift and make the cup feel less dimensional. In other words, temperature is not a one-way “better taste” knob. It works more like **a mixer that chooses which personality of the tea steps onto center stage**.

In tea aesthetics, this is the craft of *kagen*—calibration. Not excessive, not insufficient, but tuned to this moment, this guest, this meeting. Precision in temae matters; so does this subtle reading of balance.

---

### Three Lenses for Reading Temperature

#### 1) Read Felt Seasonality First
The same 20°C can feel different in early spring versus pre-rainy season. Dry air makes steam feel light; humid air retains heat around the body. Guests first receive the room’s “air feeling” before they analyze the tea itself. So it helps to think of **temperature as a translation of seasonality**.

#### 2) Decide Through Dialogue with Sweets
After a rich, sweet wagashi, a cup with slightly clearer structure can feel refreshing. With delicate dry sweets, a softer thermal profile may better highlight gentle umami and leave a cleaner aftertaste. Tea and sweets are not a fixed set—they are a conversation. Water temperature sets that conversation’s tempo.

#### 3) Don’t Make Every Bowl Identical
In multi-guest settings, the first and later bowls are not received the same way. Palate temperature changes; attention deepens; mood settles. The first bowl can ease tension, while later bowls can emphasize lingering resonance. A small thermal shift can connect the whole narrative of the gathering.

### 10-Minute Morning Practice Experiment

Try this short experiment today:

1. Use the same matcha, same amount, same bowl.  
2. Prepare three bowls: slightly hotter, standard, slightly cooler.  
3. For each bowl, write one short note on:  
   - Aromatic lift  
   - Bitterness/sweetness balance  
   - Length and quality of aftertaste

The goal is not to “pick the correct answer.” The goal is to **describe differences in your own language**. In sadō, improvement comes from both procedure and perception. What you can clearly describe, you can more reliably reproduce.
