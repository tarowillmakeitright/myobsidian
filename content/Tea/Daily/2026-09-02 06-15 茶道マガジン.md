# 茶筅通しは、泡立てる前に静けさを通す

初秋の入口、朝の静けさが少しずつ深まるころです。今日は、茶を点てる前に湯の気配で穂先をひらく茶筅通しに目を向けてみましょう。

## 日本語特集

茶道を習い始めた人が、最初に不思議に感じる動きのひとつに茶筅通しがあります。まだ抹茶は入っていないのに、なぜ先に湯を注ぎ、茶筅を動かすのだろう。急いで一服を点てたい朝には、とくにそう見えるかもしれません。けれど、この短い所作をよく見ると、茶筅通しは単なる準備ではありません。一碗が始まる前に、道具と手と気持ちの速度をそろえる、小さな前奏のような動きです。

表千家の公式用語集では、茶筅通しを「茶をたてる前、茶碗に入れた湯で茶筅を清め、穂先をやわらげる所作」と説明し、さらに穂先が折れていないかを確かめる意味にも触れています。裏千家の公開案内でも、茶筅通しは割稽古の早い段階で学ぶ基本の所作として挙げられています。まず事実として、この動きには実用があります。乾いた穂先をそのまま強く扱わないこと。茶碗の中であらかじめ穂をひらき、点茶の前に具合を確かめること。ここまでは、とても合理的です。

しかし茶の湯の面白さは、合理的な動きがそれだけで終わらないところにあります。茶筅通しでは、湯を受けた茶碗の内側に、まだ茶のない静かな円が生まれます。茶筅はその中を走りすぎず、ためらいすぎず、手の熱だけを畳へこぼさないように動きます。客の目には、いまから本番が始まることが見えています。けれど亭主は、すぐに抹茶へ行かない。ほんのわずかに留まり、穂先のひらき具合と湯の手触りを確かめる。この寄り道のような数秒が、一服を雑な仕事にしないための時間になります。

茶の湯には、いきなり核心へ飛び込まない知恵がたくさんあります。露地を歩くこと、躙口で身を低くすること、扇子を膝前に置いて一礼すること。どれも目的地へ最短で着くための動きではありません。茶筅通しも同じです。茶を点てるという結果だけ見れば、省けそうに見えるかもしれない。けれど、茶の湯が大事にするのは結果の速さより、そこへ入っていく手前の整えです。いきなり泡を立てるのではなく、その前に湯の温度、竹の弾力、茶碗の深さ、自分の呼吸を一度だけ結び直す。その順番があるから、一服は「作った飲みもの」ではなく「通ってきた時間」を持ちます。

ここで気をつけたいのは、茶筅通しの細部をひとつの正解にまとめないことです。茶筅の形そのものにも流儀差があり、裏千家の道具紹介でも、用いる竹の種類や形状は流儀によって異なると説明されています。実際の手の運びや見せ方も、流派や点前、稽古と正式な席とでニュアンスが変わります。だから今日は、「何回振るか」を覚えるより、なぜ茶の湯が本番の直前にこうした静かな確認を置くのかを見たいのです。違いがあるからこそ共通して見えてくるのは、どの系統でも、茶筅通しが道具への乱暴さを避け、点前の呼吸を整える入口になっていることです。

私はこの所作を見るたびに、音楽家が演奏の直前にひとつだけ音を確かめる姿を思い出します。その一音は観客のためである以前に、自分の耳と楽器を同じ部屋にそろえるためにあります。茶筅通しもよく似ています。客へ向けて派手に示す動きではなく、自分の手がいまからどの速度で一碗に入るかを決めるための、ごく短い対話です。だから、美しい茶筅通しは速いとも遅いとも感じません。ただ、もう茶は始まっているのだと、静かにわからせます。

初心者の目には、茶筅通しは「まだ何も起きていない時間」に見えるかもしれません。けれど実際には、その数秒に、道具を傷めない慎み、湯への注意、これから客へ差し出すものへの責任が集まっています。泡立てる前に静けさを通す。その薄い準備の層があるからこそ、最後に出される一碗は、ただ混ぜられた抹茶ではなく、丁寧に始められた朝の出来事になります。

## 今日の見どころ

茶筅そのものより、茶筅通しのあいだに亭主の手の速度がどう変わるかを見てみてください。まだ茶を点てていないのに、場が少し静まり、茶碗の内側に「これから」が集まり始めるなら、その所作はもう十分に働いています。

## 小さな実践

家のコップか湯のみをひとつ用意し、そこへ少量のぬるま湯を入れてください。箸でもスプーンでもよいので、すぐ強く混ぜず、まず器の底にそって一呼吸ぶんだけ静かに動かします。そのあとで少しだけ速く回し、音や手首の力みがどう変わるかを見ます。目的は茶筅通しの再現ではなく、「始める前に速度を整える」と、手の感じ方がどう変わるかを知ることです。熱湯は使わず、安全な温度で試してください。

## Full English Edition

# Chasen-toshi Passes Quiet Before It Raises Foam

At the threshold of early autumn, as the quiet of morning begins to deepen, it may be a good time to notice *chasen-toshi*, the small act of opening the whisk tines with hot water before tea is made.

One of the first movements that feels mysterious to beginners in tea is *chasen-toshi*. There is no matcha in the bowl yet, so why pour hot water first and move the whisk before anything has really begun? On a hurried morning, it can look especially puzzling. But if we watch the gesture closely, it is not merely preparation. It is a brief prelude that brings the speed of the utensils, the hands, and the mind into alignment before a bowl truly starts.

Omotesenke's official glossary explains *chasen-toshi* as the action of cleansing the whisk in the hot water placed in the bowl before tea is whisked, softening the tips and also checking whether any tines are broken. A public Urasenke guide likewise lists *chasen-toshi* among the fundamental introductory exercises taught early in practice. So at the factual level, the gesture is practical. It prevents the dry tines from being handled harshly. It opens the whisk in advance inside the bowl. It lets the host quietly test its condition before making tea. All of that is sensible.

What makes tea interesting, though, is that sensible actions rarely remain only sensible. During *chasen-toshi*, a quiet circle appears inside the bowl, filled with hot water but not yet with tea. The whisk moves there without rushing and without hesitation, as though careful not to spill the heat of the hand carelessly into the room. The guest already knows the main action is coming. Yet the host does not leap toward the matcha. For a few seconds, there is a pause just long enough to feel how the tines have opened, how the water feels, how the bowl receives the movement. That slight detour keeps the coming bowl from becoming rough work.

Chanoyu is full of forms that do not rush directly into the center of the matter. One walks the roji instead of entering abruptly. One lowers the body at the *nijiriguchi*. One places the fan before the knees and bows. None of these are the shortest path to the outcome. *Chasen-toshi* belongs to the same wisdom. If all we care about is the end result, it may seem dispensable. But tea cares less about speed than about the manner of entering the act. Instead of whisking at once, one briefly reconnects the temperature of the water, the resilience of the bamboo, the depth of the bowl, and one's own breathing. Because that sequence exists, the bowl that follows holds not only tea, but the time that has passed through it.

It matters, however, not to force all the details of *chasen-toshi* into a single universal rule. Even the whisk itself differs by school. Urasenke's public introduction to utensils notes that the bamboo type and form of the *chasen* vary depending on tradition. The exact handling, rhythm, and visual emphasis also shift with school, temae style, and the difference between practice and a formal gathering. So today's concern is not memorizing a fixed number of strokes. It is understanding why tea places such a quiet confirmation immediately before the main act. Because there are differences, one common thread becomes clearer: across traditions, *chasen-toshi* serves as an entrance that avoids roughness toward the utensil and steadies the breath of the procedure itself.

Whenever I watch this movement, I think of a musician sounding a single note just before performance. That note is not primarily for the audience. It is for bringing the instrument and the ear into the same room. *Chasen-toshi* feels similar. It is not a dramatic display aimed outward. It is a short conversation through which the hand decides the speed at which it will enter the bowl. That is why a beautiful *chasen-toshi* feels neither fast nor slow. It simply lets us know, quietly, that tea has already begun.

To a beginner's eye, *chasen-toshi* may look like time in which nothing has happened yet. In truth, those few seconds gather restraint toward the utensil, attention to the water, and responsibility toward what will soon be offered to the guest. Quiet is passed through the whisk before foam is raised. Because that thin layer of preparation exists, the final bowl is not merely mixed matcha. It becomes a morning event that was begun with care.

## What to Notice Today

Rather than looking only at the whisk, notice whether the host's speed changes during *chasen-toshi*. If the room grows slightly quieter and the inside of the bowl starts to gather a sense of "what is about to happen," the gesture is already doing its work.

## A Small Practice

Take a cup or tumbler at home and pour in a small amount of lukewarm water. Use a spoon or chopstick if you like, but do not stir vigorously right away. First move it quietly along the bottom of the vessel for the length of one breath. Then stir a little faster and notice how the sound changes, and how your wrist feels different. The aim is not to imitate tea procedure exactly, but to observe how adjusting your speed before "really beginning" changes the quality of the action. Use safe, moderate water rather than anything very hot.

## Vocabulary

1. **茶筅通し**（ちゃせんとおし） - preparing the tea whisk with hot water before whisking tea
   Used for the short action that softens and checks the whisk before matcha is made.
2. **穂先**（ほさき） - the tips or tines of the whisk
   Often mentioned when discussing whether the chasen has opened properly or is damaged.
3. **点てる**（たてる） - to whisk and prepare matcha
   In tea, one "makes" tea by whisking it, not simply by pouring it.
4. **割稽古**（わりげいこ） - foundational practice in separated basic actions
   Refers to introductory training where core movements are learned one by one.
5. **点前**（てまえ） - the tea procedure or temae
   A broad word for the patterned act of preparing and serving tea.
6. **茶碗**（ちゃわん） - tea bowl
   The bowl is not just a container; its depth and surface affect the movement.
7. **湯加減**（ゆかげん） - the state or feel of the hot water
   Useful when speaking about temperature, touch, and readiness in tea.
8. **躙口**（にじりぐち） - crawl-through entrance
   A small entrance that slows the body before entering a tea room.
9. **露地**（ろじ） - tea garden approach
   The path to the tea room, often understood as part of the mental transition.
10. **呼吸**（こきゅう） - breathing, rhythm
   In tea writing, this often refers to timing and inner pace, not only literal breath.
11. **慎み**（つつしみ） - restraint, careful modesty
   A good word for the attitude of not handling tools harshly or showily.
12. **前奏**（ぜんそう） - prelude
   Used metaphorically here for a movement that prepares the whole atmosphere.
