# 宗旦は、侘びを家の未来へ渡した

今朝は、千宗旦を「清貧の茶人」という一枚の肖像から解き放ち、受け継いだ茶を、場所と人と次代へどう手渡したのかを見つめます。

## 日本語特集

千宗旦は、千利休の孫として一五七八年に生まれ、一六五八年に没しました。裏千家の歴代解説では、父・少庵の後を継ぐ千家三代として、利休の茶の道統を守り、千家茶道の礎を築いた人物とされています。この短い説明には、「守る」と「築く」という二つの動詞が並びます。宗旦を考える鍵は、まさにその間にあります。継承とは、形を凍らせることではありません。壊れやすいものが次の時代にも生きられるよう、暮らしの器をつくり直すことです。

宗旦の生きた時代は、利休と豊臣秀吉の緊張に満ちた桃山から、徳川の秩序が整う江戸初期へ移る時期でした。京都国立博物館は、利休と秀吉の後、茶の湯が武家、公家、僧侶、町人それぞれの立場へ広がり、宗旦も公家や僧侶との交流のなかでその展開に関わったと説明しています。つまり宗旦は、ひとつの完成形を守る番人ではなく、茶の湯が異なる社会へ根を伸ばす時代の結節点に立っていました。

石川県立美術館の作家解説によれば、宗旦は幼少期に大徳寺で得度し、のちに再興された千家へ戻って利休の侘茶を広め、生涯仕官しませんでした。その徹底ぶりから「乞食宗旦」の異名で呼ばれます。ただし、この呼称だけを手がかりに、宗旦を貧しさそのものを美化した人物と見るのは危険です。侘びは、物が少ないという数量の問題ではありません。限られたものを粗末に扱わず、余計な権威で一碗の時間を覆わないという、注意の質にかかわる態度です。

宗旦の竹の仕事は、その態度を具体的に見せます。メトロポリタン美術館が所蔵する一重切竹花入《吉野山》は、宗旦作とされる一七世紀の作品で、自然な竹の肌と色を残しています。そこには豪華な材料で作者の力を示す方向とは別の道があります。節を読み、切る位置を定め、掛けられた一本の花が生きる余地を残す。手数が少なく見えるものほど、どこで止めたかが露わになります。宗旦の簡素さは、不在ではなく、判断の密度です。

空間にも同じことが言えます。裏千家の解説では、一畳台目の今日庵、利休四畳半を基にした又隠、八畳敷の寒雲亭はいずれも宗旦の創建とされます。大きさも用途も異なる三つの席を並べると、宗旦の茶が「小さければよい」という単純な思想ではなかったことが見えてきます。一人ひとりの距離が鋭く感じられる小間も、多くの人を迎える広間も必要でした。侘びの核心を保ちながら、場面に応じた器を用意する。その柔軟さもまた、継承の技術でした。

さらに宗旦は、家の未来を一つの線に閉じ込めませんでした。裏千家の歴代解説と石川県立美術館の資料は、晩年に三男・江岑宗左へ母屋を譲り、北側の隠居屋敷に移ったこと、二男・一翁宗守が官休庵を興し、四男・仙叟宗室へ今日庵のある屋敷が譲られたことを伝えています。これが、のちの表千家、武者小路千家、裏千家へつながります。今日の三千家を宗旦が一度に「創設した」と言うより、息子たちがそれぞれの場で茶を生かせる条件を整え、その分岐の基礎を置いた、と見るほうが丁寧でしょう。

ここに宗旦の大きな仕事があります。中心を強く囲い込むのではなく、核を保ちながら複数の入口をつくったことです。道統は一本でも、実践の家は一つでなくてよい。受け継ぐ者が違えば、迎える客も、支える地域も、時代への応答も変わります。宗旦は変化を拒むことで利休を守ったのではなく、変化に耐える骨組みをつくることで守ったのです。

今朝の一碗に引き寄せるなら、継承は大きな宣言から始まりません。茶筅を乾かす、茶碗を元の場所へ戻す、覚えた所作を誰かに押しつけず丁寧に見せる。小さな反復が、文化を明日へ渡します。宗旦の静けさは、何も動かさない静けさではありません。目立たない場所で、次の人が続けられるように整える静けさです。

## 今日の見どころ

宗旦ゆかりの茶室や竹花入を見るときは、「質素」という一語で終えず、何が残され、何が省かれ、次に来る人のためにどんな余地があるかを見てください。簡素さのなかの設計が見えてきます。

## 小さな実践

今日使う茶碗か湯のみを一つ選び、使い終えたら洗って拭き、次に手に取る人が迷わない向きと場所へ戻してください。最後に十秒だけ眺め、「私は何を残し、何を整えたか」と心の中で問いましょう。片づけを、継承の最小単位として行う稽古です。

## Full English Edition

# Sōtan Passed Wabi into the Future of the Sen House

This morning, let us release Sen Sōtan from the single portrait of an impoverished tea master and look instead at how he passed inherited tea practice into new places, new hands, and a new age.

Sen Sōtan, a grandson of Sen no Rikyū, lived from 1578 to 1658. Urasenke’s account of its lineage identifies him as the third-generation head of the Sen house after his father Shōan, a figure who preserved Rikyū’s transmission while laying the foundation of Sen-family tea. Two actions sit side by side in that description: preserving and building. Sōtan’s significance lies between them. Inheritance is not the freezing of a form. It is the work of making a vessel in which something fragile can remain alive in another era.

Sōtan lived across the transition from the charged Momoyama world of Rikyū and Toyotomi Hideyoshi into the more settled order of the early Edo period. The Kyoto National Museum explains that after Rikyū and Hideyoshi, chanoyu spread among warriors, court nobles, priests, and townspeople, and that Sōtan participated in this expansion through contact with aristocratic and religious circles. He was therefore not merely the guard of a finished model. He stood at a junction while tea practice was putting down roots in different parts of society.

According to the Ishikawa Prefectural Museum of Art, Sōtan entered Buddhist training at Daitokuji while young, later returned to the restored Sen household, spread Rikyū’s wabi tea, and remained without official employment throughout his life. His commitment earned him the sobriquet “Beggar Sōtan.” Yet the name should not tempt us to imagine that he simply glorified poverty. Wabi is not a count of how few objects one owns. It is a quality of attention: refusing to treat limited things carelessly and refusing to cover the time of one bowl with borrowed authority.

Sōtan’s work in bamboo makes that attitude tangible. The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds *Mount Yoshino*, a seventeenth-century single-cut hanging flower container attributed to him. The surface and tones of the bamboo remain natural. It follows a path different from displaying power through expensive materials. The maker reads the joint, chooses the cut, and leaves enough space for a single flower to live. The fewer the visible gestures, the more clearly the decision to stop is exposed. Sōtan’s simplicity is not absence; it is concentrated judgment.

The same is true of space. Urasenke identifies three representative rooms as Sōtan’s creations: the one-and-three-quarter-mat Konnichian, Yūin based on Rikyū’s four-and-a-half-mat plan, and the eight-mat Kanuntei. Placing these rooms of different sizes and purposes beside one another dispels the idea that Sōtan’s tea simply meant “smaller is better.” An intimate room, where every person’s distance is acutely felt, was necessary; so was a larger room able to receive more people. Preparing different vessels for different encounters while keeping the core of wabi intact was itself a technique of transmission.

Sōtan also did not confine his household’s future to a single line. Urasenke and the Ishikawa museum recount that late in life he transferred the main residence to his third son, Kōshin Sōsa, and moved to a retirement compound to its north. His second son, Ichiō Sōshu, established Kankyūan, while the compound containing Konnichian passed to his fourth son, Sensō Sōshitsu. These developments led to the later Omotesenke, Mushakōjisenke, and Urasenke traditions. Rather than saying that Sōtan created all three schools at once, it is more careful to say that he prepared conditions in which his sons could sustain tea in their respective places and laid the foundation for that branching.

This was Sōtan’s great work: not enclosing the center ever more tightly, but keeping a core while making several entrances. A transmission may be continuous even when its houses are plural. Different inheritors receive different guests, depend on different regions, and answer their times differently. Sōtan did not protect Rikyū by refusing change. He protected the inheritance by giving it a structure capable of enduring change.

Drawn into this morning’s bowl, transmission begins without a grand declaration. Dry the whisk, return the bowl to its place, and show a learned movement carefully without forcing it on someone else. Small repetitions carry culture into tomorrow. Sōtan’s quiet was not the quiet of leaving everything untouched. It was the quiet work of arranging things so that another person could continue.

## What to Notice Today

When looking at a room or bamboo vessel associated with Sōtan, do not stop at the word “rustic.” Ask what has been kept, what has been removed, and what room remains for the next person. The design within simplicity will begin to appear.

## A Small Practice

Choose one bowl or cup you will use today. Afterward, wash and dry it, then return it in an orientation and place that will make sense to the next person who reaches for it. Look at it for ten seconds and ask silently, “What did I preserve, and what did I prepare?” Treat tidying as the smallest unit of transmission.

## Vocabulary

1. **千宗旦（せんのそうたん）** — Sen Sōtan; Rikyū’s grandson and the third-generation head of the Sen house
2. **道統（どうとう）** — an inherited line of teaching or practice
3. **侘茶（わびちゃ）** — tea practice grounded in restraint, attention, and unpretentious materials
4. **継承（けいしょう）** — inheritance or transmission to a later generation
5. **仕官（しかん）** — entering official service under a lord or government
6. **得度（とくど）** — receiving Buddhist ordination
7. **一重切（いちじゅうぎり）** — a bamboo flower container with one opening cut into its side
8. **今日庵（こんにちあん）** — Konnichian, the compact tea room associated with Sōtan and Urasenke
9. **又隠（ゆういん）** — Yūin, a tea room based on a four-and-a-half-mat plan
10. **三千家（さんせんけ）** — the three Sen-family traditions: Omotesenke, Urasenke, and Mushakōjisenke
11. **分岐（ぶんき）** — branching into distinct paths
12. **礎（いしずえ）** — a foundation on which later practice is built
