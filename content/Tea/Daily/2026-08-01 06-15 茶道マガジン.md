# 美濃の白に、火の指あとを読む

朝の気持ちを少しだけ静かにして、今日は岐阜の窯場へ心を運び、志野の白がどうやって一碗の景色になるのかを見つめてみましょう。

## 日本語特集

土ものの茶碗を前にすると、私たちはつい、色や形を先に言葉にしたくなります。白い、やわらかい、素朴だ、あたたかい。けれど志野茶碗の前では、そのどれも少し足りません。志野の魅力は、白さがただ清潔に整っていることではなく、むしろ白の中に、火の揺れや土の荒さや、人の手のためらいがそのまま残っているところにあります。きれいに消された仕上がりではなく、焼かれた痕跡が景色になっている。そのところに、茶の湯が志野を深く愛してきた理由のひとつがあります。

志野は美濃、いまの岐阜県東濃地域で、十六世紀末ごろから大窯で焼かれたやきものとして知られます。厚く掛かった長石釉が、不透明な乳白色をつくり、その下に置かれた鉄絵は、くっきり描線というより、どこか霧の向こうから現れるように見えます。さらに、窯の火のまわり方によって、釉の際や土の表面に赤褐色の火色があらわれることがあります。この火色は彩色ではありません。焼成の時間と炎の働きが残した、いわば窯の筆づかいです。だから同じ調子で完全に再現することが難しく、一碗ごとの差異が、そのまま見どころになります。

この「完全に均一でない」ことは、工業製品に慣れた目には、最初は不安定にも映るかもしれません。けれど茶の湯は、そうした不均一を欠点として急いで消そうとはしませんでした。むしろ、白釉のたまり、縮れたような肌、鉄絵のにじみ、火色の立ち上がりを、ひとつの場の中でどう響かせるかを見てきました。志野の白は、雪のように無垢な白というより、火をくぐったあとの白です。静かに見えて、内側には熱の記憶が残っています。朝の席でこの白に向き合うと、涼しさだけでなく、焼かれたものだけが持つ深いぬくみも感じられます。

東京国立博物館の「振袖」と呼ばれる志野茶碗の解説には、白釉の下に野の草が描かれ、霧の野を思わせるとあります。この見え方は、志野を理解するうえでとても大切です。志野は、白い器である前に、景色を起こす器です。文様は説明的に主張しすぎず、釉の厚みがかえって風景を遠くへ引かせる。近くで見れば土と釉の物質感があり、少し離れて見れば野や霞のような気配になる。その往復があるから、客はただ文様を読むのではなく、器の中に空気まで感じ取ろうとします。

もうひとつ、志野のおもしろさは、作り手の手つきが完全には隠されないことです。メトロポリタン美術館の「神橋」と呼ばれる志野茶碗には、釉掛けの際に指で支えた部分が無釉のまま残り、それが前面にさりげない「指あと」として見えると紹介されています。茶碗は本来、完成品として私たちの前に出ます。けれど志野では、ときどき完成の顔の中に制作の時間がのぞく。きっちり隠し切らないことで、むしろ器が生きたものとして感じられるのです。茶の湯が重んじるのは、完璧な無菌状態ではなく、人と土と火が出会った痕跡なのだと、こういう細部は教えてくれます。

もちろん、志野の見方や好みは一様ではありません。ある人は乳白のやわらかさを愛し、ある人は火色の強さに惹かれ、また別の人は鼠志野や絵志野の変化をたどります。流派や席の趣向によっても、どのような志野をどう合わせるかは違います。ただ、どの立場から見ても共通しやすいのは、志野が「白く整った器」ではなく、「焼成の出来事を抱えた器」だということです。美濃の山あいで土を掘り、釉を掛け、炎に託した時間が、そのまま一碗の表情になる。だから志野を見ることは、器の表面を見るだけでなく、窯の時間を読むことでもあります。

土岐や多治見の窯場を歩く想像をすると、志野の白は急に遠い博物館の展示ではなくなります。山の土、薪の炎、窯道具の扱い、そして焼き上がったものを手に取って「この火色が出たか」と確かめる人の目。その土地の仕事が、茶席では静かな景色として立ち上がります。今日の一碗をもし志野でいただくなら、白を白として眺めるだけでなく、その白の奥に残る熱と手の気配まで、少しゆっくり受け取ってみてください。茶道は、器を鑑賞する文化である前に、器のうちに残った時間へ耳を澄ます文化でもあるのです。

## 今日の見どころ

- 志野の白を「単色」としてではなく、釉の厚み、透けない感じ、やわらかな陰りとして見てみること。
- 口縁や見込み、高台まわりに火色が出ていれば、それを装飾ではなく焼成の痕跡として読むこと。
- 絵志野の文様を見るときは、線そのものより、白の中に文様が遠く浮かぶ感じに注目すること。
- 茶碗のどこかに、手で支えた気配、ゆがみ、釉だまりなど「作られた時間」が見えるか探してみること。

## 小さな実践

白いマグカップか小皿をひとつ用意して、朝の自然光の入る場所へ置いてみてください。触れなくて大丈夫です。正面から一度、少し斜めから一度、さらに立ち上がって上から一度見ます。そのたびに、白が同じ白には見えないことを確かめてみてください。もし余裕があれば、「光の白」「影の白」「手に近い白」の三つを心の中で言い分けてみましょう。志野を見る練習は、まず白をひとつに決めつけないところから始められます。

## Full English Edition

# Reading the Fingerprint of Fire in Mino White

Quiet your mind just a little this morning, and let it travel to the kiln sites of Gifu as we look at how the white of Shino becomes a landscape within a single bowl.

When we sit before an earthen tea bowl, we are often tempted to describe it too quickly. White, soft, rustic, warm. But in front of a Shino bowl, none of those words is quite enough. The appeal of Shino does not lie in a white surface that has been neatly perfected. It lies, rather, in the way that movement of flame, roughness of clay, and even hesitation of the human hand remain inside the white itself. The bowl is not beautiful because all traces of making have been erased. It is beautiful because traces of firing have become scenery. That is one of the reasons Shino has been loved so deeply in chanoyu.

Shino is associated with Mino, in the Tono area of present-day Gifu Prefecture, and is known as a ceramic type produced in large kilns from the late sixteenth century. A thick feldspar glaze creates its opaque milky white surface. Iron-painted designs beneath that glaze do not usually appear as sharp, graphic lines; they seem to emerge as if from behind mist. In addition, depending on how the fire moves through the kiln, reddish-brown areas called hi-iro, or “fire color,” may appear around the glaze edges or on the clay body. This is not painted decoration. It is the mark left by time and flame during firing, the kiln’s own brushwork, so to speak. Because of that, it is difficult to reproduce in exactly the same way, and the differences from bowl to bowl become part of what we appreciate.

To eyes trained by industrial uniformity, this lack of perfect consistency may at first seem unstable. Chanoyu, however, did not rush to eliminate such irregularity as a flaw. Instead, it learned to listen to the resonance between pooled white glaze, slightly contracted textures, blurred iron designs, and the rise of fire color within a single setting. The white of Shino is not the untouched white of snow. It is white after passing through fire. It appears quiet, yet it holds the memory of heat inside it. When you face that white in a morning tea gathering, you may feel not only coolness, but also the deep warmth that belongs only to something that has been fired.

The Tokyo National Museum describes the Shino tea bowl named *Furisode* as a bowl whose grasses, painted beneath the white glaze, evoke a misty field. This is an important key to understanding Shino. Before it is a white vessel, it is a vessel that generates landscape. Its designs do not insist too loudly. The thickness of the glaze actually pushes the image farther away, allowing it to recede into distance. Up close, you see clay and glaze as physical matter. From a little farther back, they become field, haze, and air. Because that movement is possible, the guest does not merely “read” the motif. The guest tries to sense atmosphere within the bowl.

Another fascinating feature of Shino is that the maker’s handling is not always entirely hidden. The Metropolitan Museum of Art notes that on the Shino tea bowl known as *Shinkyo*, the place where the potter supported the bowl with a finger while dipping it into glaze remains unglazed on the front, leaving a subtle “fingerprint” of the process. A tea bowl is usually presented to us as a finished object. Yet in Shino, the time of making sometimes peers through the face of completion. Because everything is not perfectly concealed, the bowl can feel more alive. Details like this remind us that chanoyu values not a sterile idea of perfection, but the traces left where human hands, clay, and fire met one another.

Of course, ways of seeing Shino are not uniform. Some people love the softness of its milky white. Others are drawn to the strength of its fire color. Others follow the variations among gray Shino, painted Shino, and other related forms. Different schools and different tea settings also make different choices about which kind of Shino to combine with which utensils. Still, across those differences, one point remains widely true: Shino is not simply a well-finished white bowl. It is a bowl that carries the event of firing within it. Clay dug from the hills of Mino, glaze laid onto the surface, and hours entrusted to flame all become visible as expression in a single vessel. To look at Shino, then, is not only to look at a ceramic surface. It is also to read kiln time.

If you imagine walking the kiln regions around Toki or Tajimi, the white of Shino stops feeling like a distant museum object. Mountain clay, wood fire, kiln tools, and the eyes of someone lifting a finished piece to see what kind of fire color emerged: the work of that place rises again in the tearoom as quiet scenery. If today’s bowl were Shino, try not to look at its white as “just white.” Receive, a little more slowly, the lingering heat and the touch of the hand that remain inside it. Before tea is a culture of admiring utensils, it is also a culture of listening to the time that remains within them.

## Vocabulary

1. 志野（しの）: Shino ware. Usage: A style of Mino ceramics, especially associated with thick white feldspar glaze in tea contexts.
2. 美濃（みの）: Mino. Usage: Historical region in present-day Gifu; often named when discussing Shino and Oribe ceramics.
3. 茶碗（ちゃわん）: tea bowl. Usage: In chanoyu, this is never just a container; it is a central object of aesthetic attention.
4. 長石釉（ちょうせきゆう）: feldspar glaze. Usage: The glaze that gives Shino its opaque milky white surface.
5. 火色（ひいろ）: fire color. Usage: Reddish-brown coloration created by the firing process rather than painted decoration.
6. 鉄絵（てつえ）: iron-painted design. Usage: Decoration painted in iron pigment beneath the glaze, often softly blurred in Shino.
7. 釉肌（ゆうはだ）: glaze surface/texture. Usage: Useful when describing whether a ceramic surface feels soft, pooled, tight, or uneven.
8. 見込み（みこみ）: the interior of a bowl. Usage: Often a key place to notice glaze depth, pooling, and marks of use.
9. 口縁（こうえん）: rim. Usage: The rim is often where shifts in glaze or traces of fire become especially visible.
10. 高台（こうだい）: foot ring. Usage: The trimmed base of a bowl; important when handling and viewing tea bowls.
11. 景色（けしき）: scenery, visual character. Usage: In tea and ceramics, it means the poetic “landscape” seen in a surface or form.
12. 窯変（ようへん）: kiln effect/change. Usage: Refers to changes produced in firing; use it carefully, since some traditions apply it more narrowly than others.
