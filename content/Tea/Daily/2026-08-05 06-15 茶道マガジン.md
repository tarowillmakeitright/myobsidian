# 正面を避ける、という小さな返事

雨の気配を含んだ朝、茶碗をほんの少し回す所作のなかに、礼が形になる瞬間を静かに見つめてみましょう。

今朝の東京は雨の気配を含む涼しめの朝で、茶碗の正面をそのまま受け取らずに少しよける心づかいが、湿った空気のなかでいっそうやわらかく感じられます。

## 日本語特集

茶道を知らない人が茶席を見て、まず不思議に思うことの一つが、客が茶碗を少し回してからいただく動きです。なぜそのまま飲まないのか。なぜ回すのか。そして、何回回すのが正しいのか。こうした問いは、じつは茶の湯の入口にとてもふさわしい問いです。というのも、この小さな所作のなかには、茶道が単なる手順の集まりではなく、相手の気持ちを受け取るための文化であることがよくあらわれているからです。

裏千家の入門向け公開説明では、薄茶をいただくとき、客は「茶碗の正面からいただくことをさけるため、右手で手前に二度まわして」いただくと案内されています。別の裏千家の案内では、亭主は茶碗の正面を客に向けて出し、客は謙虚な心でその正面からいただくことを避けるために茶碗を回す、と説明されています。ここで大切なのは、「回す」という動きの中心にあるのが器用さではなく、正面を避けるという遠慮と感謝だ、という点です。茶碗にはただの表裏ではなく、その茶碗がもっとも美しく見える側、迎える側、いわば「顔」があります。亭主はそこを客へ向けて差し出します。客は、そのもっとも大切にされた面にいきなり口をつけることを避ける。そのために、少しだけ手の中で向きを変えるのです。

この所作は、礼儀作法として説明すると簡単に終わってしまいます。しかし、実際にはもっと繊細なやりとりです。亭主は、道具組を考え、季節を読み、菓子との取り合わせを考え、一碗の前にすでに長い準備をしています。そのうえで、茶碗の正面を客へ向けるという最後の配慮をします。客が茶碗を回すのは、その配慮を無視するためではありません。むしろ「あなたが大切にした面を、私は大切に受け取ります」と返事をするためです。真正面から受けてすぐ飲むのではなく、ほんの少し避けることで、亭主の心づかいを見落としていないことを身体で伝える。そう考えると、回すという動作は、道具の向きを変える以上に、関係の温度を整える行為に見えてきます。

面白いのは、同じ裏千家の公開案内のなかでも、「二度回す」という具体的な説明と、「何回回すかは重要ではない」という説明が並びうることです。これは矛盾というより、茶道の型の持ち方をよく示しています。初心者にとっては、まず身体を迷わせないために具体的な手順が必要です。けれど、型は数字そのものを信仰するためにあるのではなく、その型が何を表しているかへ進むためにあります。二度回す、時計回り、反時計回り、といった順序には学ぶ価値がありますが、それだけで作法の意味が尽きるわけではありません。公開説明にあるとおり、より深いところでは、亭主の気持ちにどう感じ、どう応えるかが問われているのです。

ここで、他流のすべてが同じ数え方をしていると早合点しないことも大切です。茶の湯は流儀、稽古場、場面によって言い回しや扱いの細部が異なります。今日ここで見ているのは、少なくとも現在公開されている裏千家の説明に基づく一つの明快な読み方です。だからこそ、この所作を「茶道では必ずこうする」と強く言い切るより、「この流れでは、こういう心の動きが形になっている」と受け止めるほうが、かえって茶の湯らしい慎みを保てます。

それにしても、なぜ「正面」を避けることが、ここまで大事にされるのでしょう。私はそこに、茶の湯が美術鑑賞と飲食を分けずにひとつにしている面白さを見ることがあります。茶碗は使う器であると同時に、拝見されるものでもあります。口をつける前から、形、釉、口造り、高台、そして正面が見られている。つまり客は、飲む人である前に、まず見る人なのです。見ることを先に置くからこそ、正面をそのまま塞がない。そのわずかな配慮のうちに、茶碗をただの容器にしない感覚が宿っています。

朝の稽古でこのことを思うと、茶碗を回す所作は、案外いまの生活にも近いと感じます。現代では、相手の好意をすぐ消費してしまうことが多い。いただいたものを便利さだけで受け取り、その背後の手間や選択を見落としがちです。けれど茶の湯では、いきなり「使う」前に、まずひと呼吸おいて「どう差し出されたか」を見る。そのひと呼吸があるだけで、一碗はただの飲み物でなくなります。茶碗を回す動きは、ものの向きを変えるより先に、自分の受け取り方の速度を変えているのかもしれません。

しかもその礼は、大げさではありません。深い説教も、長い説明もいらない。ただ手のなかで器を少し動かすだけでよい。茶の湯が美しいのは、心の話を大きな言葉にせず、小さな動きに預けるところです。正面を避けるという所作は、へりくだりの演技ではなく、相手の心を真正面から踏まないための距離感の技術と言えるでしょう。その距離があるから、客は遠くなるのではなく、むしろ亭主の準備へ静かに近づいていけるのです。

ですから今朝、もし「二度回す」という知識だけが頭に残ったなら、もう半歩だけ先へ進んでみてください。大事なのは、二という数ではなく、正面を避けるあいだに生まれる一瞬のためらいです。そのためらいは迷いではなく、敬意のための余白です。茶の湯は、その余白をとても大切にします。すぐに飲めるものを、すぐには飲まない。そこに、人と物と季節が同じ席へ落ち着くための時間が生まれます。

## 今日の見どころ

茶席の映像や写真、あるいは日常の器でもかまいません。器のどこが「正面」と感じられるかを見てみてください。絵付けの位置、歪みの見せ場、口縁のひらき方など、正面は単なる真ん中ではなく、「こう見てほしい」という器の声として立ちあらわれることがあります。

## 小さな実践

家のマグカップや湯のみをひとつ選び、飲む前に十秒だけ眺めてみてください。持ち手や模様の位置から、自分なりの「正面」を決め、その面を少しだけ避けるように器を回してからひと口飲みます。正しい作法の再現ではなく、どう差し出されたかを一呼吸見てから受け取る感覚を試すのが目的です。熱い飲み物では無理をせず、手首に負担があれば目で向きを追うだけで十分です。

## Full English Edition

# Turning Away from the Front: A Small Reply in the Hands

On a morning touched by the feeling of rain, let us quietly watch the moment when courtesy takes visible form in the simple act of turning a tea bowl just slightly in the hands.

Tokyo feels cooler this morning, with rain nearby in the air, and that small gesture of not receiving the front of the bowl exactly as it is offered seems especially gentle in such damp stillness.

One of the first things that puzzles people watching chanoyu is the way a guest turns the bowl a little before drinking. Why not drink from it as it is? Why turn it at all? And how many turns are correct? These are excellent beginner’s questions, because inside this tiny movement we can see very clearly that tea is not just a collection of procedures. It is a culture of receiving another person’s intention.

In Urasenke’s introductory public guidance, a guest drinking usucha is told to avoid drinking directly from the front of the bowl by turning it toward oneself twice with the right hand. In another Urasenke introductory explanation, the reasoning is stated even more plainly: the host presents the bowl with its front facing the guest, and the guest turns the bowl in order to avoid drinking from that front in a spirit of humility. The important point is that the center of the movement is not dexterity. It is restraint, gratitude, and regard for what has been offered.

A tea bowl does not merely have an outside and an inside. In tea, it is often understood to have a *shomen*, a “front,” the side considered the face of the object or the side intended to be seen most beautifully. The host places that face toward the guest. The guest, rather than immediately putting the lips to the most honored side, changes its orientation slightly. That small redirection is not a refusal. It is a form of care.

If explained only as etiquette, the gesture can sound simple and finished. In practice, however, it is a much finer exchange than that. Before the bowl ever reaches the guest, the host has already made countless decisions: the arrangement of utensils, the reading of the season, the pairing with sweets, the tone of the gathering. Presenting the bowl with its front directed toward the guest is part of that final layer of consideration. When the guest turns the bowl, the meaning is not, “I disregard what you offered.” The meaning is closer to, “I receive even the care with which you offered it.” By not drinking straight from the front, the guest shows bodily awareness of the host’s intention. The movement changes more than the orientation of the object. It adjusts the temperature of the relationship.

What is especially interesting is that within Urasenke’s own public explanations, we can find both a concrete instruction such as “turn twice” and the reminder that how many times one turns is not the most important thing. This is not really a contradiction. It reveals something essential about how form works in tea. Beginners need concrete guidance so that the body does not become lost. But form is not there to make us worship numbers. It is there to lead us toward what the form is expressing. The sequence matters. The movement matters. Yet the deeper question remains the one Urasenke itself states openly: how do you feel the host’s heart, and how do you respond to it?

It is also important not to rush from one public explanation into a universal claim about all tea practice. Chanoyu differs in detail from school to school, from teacher to teacher, and from situation to situation. What we are looking at today is one clear reading grounded in currently public Urasenke material. For that reason, it is more respectful to say, “In this line of teaching, this is how the movement carries meaning,” than to insist that all tea everywhere must do exactly the same thing in exactly the same way.

Still, the deeper reason this courtesy matters reaches beyond school difference. Why does “avoiding the front” carry such weight? Part of the answer is that chanoyu does not sharply separate aesthetic looking from bodily use. The bowl is both an object to drink from and an object to be viewed. Before it is used, it is seen: its shape, glaze, lip, foot, and front are all part of the meeting. In that sense, the guest is a viewer before becoming a drinker. Because seeing comes first, the front is not immediately covered by the mouth. Inside that small hesitation lives a refusal to reduce the bowl to mere function.

When I think about this in the morning, the gesture feels surprisingly close to ordinary life. We often consume kindness too quickly. We receive what is offered for its convenience alone and overlook the labor, thought, and selection behind it. Tea teaches something else. Before using, look. Before taking, pause. Before drinking, notice how it was placed before you. That single breath changes the bowl from a container back into a meeting.

And the courtesy is wonderfully modest. No lecture is needed. No grand declaration of gratitude is required. Only a slight movement in the hands. Chanoyu is beautiful because it entrusts large feelings to small gestures. Avoiding the front is not a theatrical performance of humility. It is a technique of distance, a way not to step directly on the face of another person’s care. Because that distance exists, host and guest do not become colder. They actually come nearer, but in a quieter and more attentive way.

So if today you remember only the phrase “turn twice,” try taking one step further. The number is not the final point. What matters is the brief hesitation created while the bowl is turned away from its front. That hesitation is not uncertainty. It is a margin reserved for respect. Tea gives great importance to such margins. Something ready to be drunk is not drunk immediately. In that pause, person, object, and season are allowed to settle into the same place.

## What to Notice Today

Whether you are looking at a tea bowl, a cup in a photograph, or an ordinary mug at home, ask yourself where its “front” seems to be. It may be suggested by a painted motif, a favored distortion, the opening of the rim, or simply by the side that feels as if it wants to greet you. In tea, the front is not always the geometric center. It is often the place where the object seems to say, “Look here.”

## Small Practice

Choose a mug or teacup at home. Before drinking, spend ten seconds simply looking at it. Decide for yourself where its “front” is, perhaps from the handle, decoration, or shape. Then turn it slightly away from that front before taking a sip. The goal is not to reproduce formal tea procedure perfectly. It is to experience what changes when you pause to notice how something was offered before receiving it. If the drink is hot, move carefully, and if your wrist is uncomfortable, let the eyes do the turning instead.

## Vocabulary

1. 正面（しょうめん）- front; honored face of an object. Usage: In tea, often the side of the bowl meant to be seen first or presented to the guest.
2. 茶碗を回す（ちゃわんをまわす）- to turn the tea bowl. Usage: Refers to the guest’s movement before drinking.
3. 謙虚（けんきょ）- humility. Usage: Often used when explaining why the guest avoids the bowl’s front.
4. お点前ちょうだいいたします（おてまえちょうだいいたします）- “I gratefully receive your tea preparation.” Usage: A formal phrase said to the host before drinking.
5. 正客（しょうきゃく）- principal guest. Usage: The lead guest seated closest to the alcove.
6. 次客（じきゃく）- second guest. Usage: The guest seated after the principal guest.
7. 懐紙（かいし）- folded paper carried by guests. Usage: Used for sweets and for wiping fingers or the bowl rim in some settings.
8. 飲み口（のみくち）- drinking spot; place where the lips touch. Usage: Often mentioned after finishing the tea.
9. 拝見（はいけん）- respectful viewing. Usage: The act of appreciating the bowl or utensils after use.
10. 心づかい（こころづかい）- considerate care; thoughtfulness. Usage: A key word for the host’s preparation and the guest’s response.
11. 余白（よはく）- margin; open interval. Usage: Useful for describing the respectful pause inside a small movement.
12. 一服（いっぷく）- one bowl of tea; a moment of tea. Usage: Can mean both the literal serving and the quiet moment it creates.
