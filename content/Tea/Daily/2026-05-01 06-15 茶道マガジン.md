## 本日の特集（日本語）
### 雨の朝に学ぶ――「露地」という静かな準備

おはようございます。今朝の東京は雨。空気はしっとりして、音がやわらかくなっています。こんな朝にぴったりなのが、茶道でいう**露地（ろじ）**の感覚です。露地は、茶室へ向かう庭の通路であり、同時に「日常から、一期一会の時間へ心を移すための道」です。

茶道を始めたばかりだと、どうしても点前（てまえ）や道具の名前に意識が集中します。もちろん、それは大切です。でも、茶の湯は「茶碗をどう持つか」だけで完成する世界ではありません。茶室に入る前から、もう茶会は始まっています。露地を一歩ずつ歩く間に、話し声が小さくなり、視線が下がり、呼吸が整っていく。その変化そのものが、茶の湯の稽古です。

露地には、派手な演出はありません。苔、飛び石、手水鉢（ちょうずばち）、待合。雨の日はそこに水の気配が加わります。石の表情が濃くなり、葉に落ちる雨の音がリズムを作り、足元への注意が自然に生まれます。つまり、雨は「不便」ではなく、身体をいまに戻す先生になります。

茶道の美意識には、しばしば「見せる美」より「整える美」があります。露地もその代表です。露地は、客を驚かせるための場所ではなく、客の心を鎮めるための場所。主人が丁寧に掃き清め、余計なものを置かないのは、空間を小さくするためではなく、心のノイズを減らすためです。

現代の生活で露地の精神を取り入れるなら、朝の5分で十分です。玄関を出る前にスマホ通知を閉じる。歩く速度を少し落とす。エレベーターの待ち時間に深呼吸する。会社や学校に着いたら、席につく前に肩の力を抜く。これらは全部、日常版の露地です。

茶の湯では、客は茶室のにじり口を通って入ります。身体を少しかがめる動作は、身分や役職をいったん外し、ただの一人として席に入る象徴でもあります。露地の道を歩き、にじり口をくぐる。この二つの動きで、人は「社会的な自分」から「今ここにいる自分」へ移行します。

今日は雨です。足元に気をつけながら、ぜひ一度「露地を歩くように」移動してみてください。目的地に着く速さではなく、心が静まる速さを基準にする。たったそれだけで、同じ朝が少し深くなります。

茶道は、特別な道具を持つ人だけのものではありません。忙しい朝に、1分だけでも自分を整える人の中に、すでに茶の湯の芯はあります。露地は庭の中だけでなく、毎日の動作の中にも作れるのです。

## Today’s Feature (English)
### Learning from a Rainy Morning: *Roji*, the Quiet Preparation

Good morning. Tokyo is rainy today. The air feels moist, and even sounds are softer than usual. A morning like this is perfect for understanding one of tea culture’s most subtle ideas: **roji**.

In tea practice, *roji* is the garden path that leads to the tea room. But it is more than a path. It is a psychological transition space—a place where you leave ordinary life behind and step, gradually, into a once-in-a-lifetime moment.

Beginners in sadō often focus on visible techniques: how to hold the tea bowl, how to turn it, how to fold the cloth, how to memorize procedure. All of that matters. But tea is never only about technical correctness. The gathering begins before anyone drinks tea. It begins as people approach.

As a guest walks through the roji, several things happen naturally: voices become quieter, eyes lower toward the ground, breathing slows, attention returns to the body. This shift is not an optional extra—it is part of the practice itself.

A roji is usually simple: moss, stepping stones, a stone basin for rinsing hands, maybe a waiting bench. Nothing flashy. On rainy days, this simplicity becomes even more expressive. Wet stones darken. Leaves catch and release droplets. Small sounds of rain create a rhythm that pulls awareness into the present.

In modern life, rain is often treated as inconvenience. In tea culture, rain can become instruction. It asks you to slow down, watch your footing, and move with care. In that sense, the weather is not an obstacle to practice—it is the practice.

Tea aesthetics often values “arranged calm” over spectacle. The roji is a clear example. It is not designed to impress guests. It is designed to settle them. The host cleans and prepares the path not to decorate space, but to reduce mental noise.

You can apply this idea without a tea garden. Try a “daily roji” in your own morning routine:

- Silence notifications before leaving home.
- Walk 10% slower than usual for one block.
- Take one intentional breath while waiting for a train or elevator.
- Relax your shoulders before sitting down at work or school.

These tiny actions create a threshold between “reactive mode” and “present mode.” That threshold is the heart of roji.

In traditional tea rooms, guests often enter through a small, low doorway (*nijiriguchi*). Physically bowing to enter symbolizes leaving social status outside—whether one is senior or junior, rich or poor, everyone enters the same space as an equal participant.

Walking the roji, then passing through the low entrance: these gestures move a person from “social identity” to “human presence.” That movement is one reason sadō still feels deeply relevant in contemporary life.

So today, in this rainy Tokyo morning, try moving as if you are walking through a roji. Measure your morning not only by speed, but by quietness of mind. Even one minute of deliberate transition can change the quality of an entire day.

Tea is not reserved for people with perfect utensils or formal training. If you can pause, breathe, and prepare your mind before the next moment, you are already touching the core of tea.
