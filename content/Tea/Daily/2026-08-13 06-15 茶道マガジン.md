# 喫茶去は、答えより先に湯気を差し出す

朝の雨がやわらかく残る東京では、濡れた空気まで一服の前座のように感じられます。

## 日本語特集

茶室の床に「喫茶去」とある朝、客はしばしばその三字を“意味の深い禅語”として受け取ります。もちろん、それは間違いではありません。けれども茶席でこのことばが本当に働き始めるのは、難しい解釈が立ち上がるより少し前です。客がまだ座りきらず、視線も心も完全には落ち着いていない、そのわずかな時間に、掛物の文字はまず「どうぞ、お茶を」と静かに差し出されます。

「喫茶去」は、唐代の禅僧・趙州従諗の公案に由来すると伝えられます。来たことのある僧にも、ない僧にも、趙州は同じく「喫茶去」と言った。来歴の違い、理解の深浅、準備の有無をいったん脇へ置いて、まず茶を勧める。福岡市博物館の解説が示すように、茶席ではこの語は、分け隔てなく「どうぞお茶を」と勧める真心として読まれてきました。ここで大切なのは、禅問答をありがたく飾ることではなく、相手を分類する心より先に一碗を出す順序です。

茶の湯で掛物が第一の道具といわれるのは、見栄えの中心だからではありません。床の墨跡が、その日の席で何を最も大切にするかを先に告げるからです。メトロポリタン美術館の茶掛に関する解説にもあるように、禅僧の書は床の間に掛けられ、茶会の静かな気分を定める役を担いました。つまり「喫茶去」は、亭主が客に向かって発する合図であると同時に、亭主自身への戒めでもあります。上手にもてなそうと力む前に、説明しすぎる前に、正しさで場を締める前に、まず一服を差し出せているか。掛物は客を迎えることばでありながら、亭主の気負いをほどく道具でもあるのです。

この三字には、茶の湯がしばしば大切にしてきた「前へ出すより、先に受け入れる」という姿勢がよくあらわれます。客が初心者であっても、長く学んだ人であっても、席に入った時点ではどちらも同じく、その朝の一会に向かう一人の客です。表千家や裏千家を含め流儀ごとに掛物の扱い方や読みの濃淡は異なりますが、「喫茶去」が広く好まれてきた理由のひとつは、この公平さにあるのでしょう。理屈を押しつけず、しかし空疎な愛想にもならず、湯気のある実際の一服へと人を戻してくれるからです。

「お茶でもどうぞ」という日本語は、日常では軽く聞こえることがあります。けれど茶室では、その軽さがかえって尊い。大仰な悟りの顔をせず、まず茶を出す。相手が整ってからもてなすのではなく、まだ整いきらない相手を迎え入れるためにもてなす。その順番に、茶の湯のやさしさと厳しさが同時にあります。やさしさとは甘さではなく、誰に対しても同じ入口をひらくこと。厳しさとは、その入口を自分の気分や判断で狭めないことです。

今朝もし床の「喫茶去」を思い浮かべるなら、そこに高度な答えを探しすぎなくてもよいのかもしれません。むしろ、答えの前に差し出される湯気、説明の前に置かれる茶碗、その順番に耳を澄ませてみる。茶の湯はしばしば、深い思想を小さな動作へ沈めて受け渡します。「喫茶去」はその代表のひとつで、言葉の顔をした一服なのだと思います。

## 今日の見どころ

床の間の掛物を見たとき、文字の意味を急いで解釈する前に、まずその語が席の空気をどう柔らかくしているかを感じてみてください。厳しい禅語に見えるのに、実際の茶席では入口を広くする働きを持つ。その逆説が「喫茶去」の見どころです。

## 小さな実践

手元に茶道具がなくてもかまいません。朝の飲みものを一口飲む前に、心の中で一度だけ「喫茶去」と唱え、今日最初に会う人を評価する前に迎えるつもりで息を整えてみてください。できれば、その一口の前後で肩や眉間の力がどう変わるかを観察します。宗教的な実践ではなく、注意の向きをやわらかくするための小さな観察です。

## Full English Edition

# Kissa-ko Offers Steam Before It Offers an Answer

In Tokyo this morning, light rain lingers in the air, and even the dampness feels like a quiet prelude to a bowl of tea.

When a tearoom alcove displays the phrase *kissa-ko*, “go have some tea,” guests often receive it first as a profound Zen expression. That is not wrong. But in a tea gathering, the phrase truly begins to work a little earlier than interpretation does. Before the guest has fully settled, before the mind has entirely come to rest, those three characters first arrive as a simple, gentle offering: please, have some tea.

The phrase is traditionally linked to a koan about the Tang-dynasty Chan monk Zhaozhou Congshen. He asked one monk whether he had been there before, then told him, “kissa-ko.” He asked another monk the same question; whether the answer was yes or no, he again said, “kissa-ko.” Differences of background, experience, preparedness, and understanding are set aside for a moment, and tea is offered first. As the Fukuoka City Museum explains, in the context of tea this phrase came to express sincere hospitality extended without discrimination: “please, have some tea.” The important thing here is not to admire the koan from a distance, but to notice the order of actions. Before categorizing the other person, offer the bowl.

In tea practice, the hanging scroll is often called the most important utensil, not because it is visually dominant, but because it declares what matters most in that particular gathering. The Metropolitan Museum of Art notes that calligraphy by Zen monks was displayed in the alcove as a tea hanging, helping set the quiet tone of the meeting. In that sense, *kissa-ko* is not only a signal from host to guest. It is also a restraint placed on the host’s own mind. Before trying too hard to impress, before over-explaining, before tightening the room with correctness, has the host actually offered tea? The scroll welcomes the guest, but it also loosens the host’s self-consciousness.

These three characters reveal a quality that tea has long cherished: receiving comes before asserting. A beginner and a long-practiced guest may enter the room with very different histories, but at the threshold of this morning’s gathering they are equally guests of the same occasion. Different schools may handle scrolls, language, and emphasis differently, and no single interpretation belongs to all traditions. Still, one reason *kissa-ko* has remained beloved across tea culture is its fairness. It does not impose a theory, yet it does not collapse into empty politeness either. It returns everyone to the concrete reality of tea actually being shared.

In ordinary life, “please have some tea” can sound casual, even light. In the tearoom, that lightness is precisely what becomes precious. No grand pose of enlightenment, no dramatic performance of depth: first, offer tea. Do not wait until the other person is fully composed before you receive them. Offer hospitality partly so that the not-yet-composed person may enter and settle. In that order, the kindness and discipline of tea appear together. The kindness is not softness without form; it is the opening of the same entrance to everyone. The discipline is not severity for its own sake; it is refusing to narrow that entrance according to one’s mood or judgment.

If you picture the phrase *kissa-ko* this morning, you may not need to force a lofty answer out of it. It may be enough to listen for what comes before the answer: the steam offered before explanation, the bowl placed before analysis. Tea often carries deep thought by sinking it into a small action. *Kissa-ko* is one of the clearest examples. It is, in a sense, a bowl of tea wearing the face of words.

## What To Notice Today

When you imagine or encounter a hanging scroll, try not to rush first toward the literal meaning of the characters. Notice how the phrase changes the room’s emotional temperature. It may look like a severe Zen saying, yet in tea it often widens the entrance rather than narrows it. That reversal is one of the most beautiful things about *kissa-ko*.

## Small Practice

You do not need tea utensils for this. Before taking the first sip of your morning drink, say *kissa-ko* once silently to yourself. Then, for a moment, imagine meeting the first person in your day before evaluating them. If possible, observe what changes in your shoulders, jaw, or brow before and after that sip. This is not a religious exercise, only a small way of softening the direction of attention.

## Vocabulary

1. 喫茶去（きっさこ） - go have some tea; a Zen phrase often read in tea as an invitation before explanation. Usage: Often seen on hanging scrolls in tea rooms.
2. 床の間（とこのま） - alcove in a Japanese room. Usage: The scroll and flowers are often arranged here.
3. 掛物（かけもの） - hanging scroll. Usage: In tea, it often sets the theme of the gathering.
4. 墨跡（ぼくせき） - calligraphy, especially by Zen monks. Usage: A valued kind of tea hanging.
5. 一碗（いちわん） - one bowl of tea. Usage: Suggests tea as a concrete act, not an abstraction.
6. 亭主（ていしゅ） - host of a tea gathering. Usage: The person who prepares and offers tea.
7. 客（きゃく） - guest. Usage: In tea, the guest also helps create the gathering.
8. 一会（いちえ） - one meeting, one occasion. Usage: Points to the unrepeatable quality of a gathering.
9. 真心（まごころ） - sincere heart; wholeheartedness. Usage: Used for hospitality offered without calculation.
10. 趣向（しゅこう） - thematic intention or aesthetic design. Usage: The host’s chosen direction for the gathering.
11. 禅語（ぜんご） - Zen phrase or saying. Usage: Often written on tea scrolls, though interpretations vary.
12. 受け入れる（うけいれる） - to receive, accept, welcome in. Usage: A key attitude in today’s theme.
