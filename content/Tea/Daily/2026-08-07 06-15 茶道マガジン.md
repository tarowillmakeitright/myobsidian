# 桔梗は、夏の席にどこまで秋を連れてくるのか

気象庁の今朝五時の東京地方予報は晴れ時々くもり。明るさの強い朝だからこそ、今日は真夏に咲きながら秋の気配を先に置く花、桔梗を静かに見つめてみましょう。

## Japanese Feature

八月初めの茶席には、少しむずかしい役目があります。まだ暑さはまぎれもなく夏のものなのに、季節の感覚はいつまでも真夏だけに留まりません。暦のうえでは立秋が近づき、光の角度や夕方の気配、菓銘や花の選び方のなかに、ほんのわずかな「先ぶれ」が入りはじめる。茶の湯が好きなのは、こういう微細な変化です。はっきり秋になってから秋を出すのではなく、夏のなかにすでに入りこんでいる秋の予感を、一輪、一語、一服のあいだに感じ取ること。今朝の主役にしたい桔梗は、その仕事をよく引き受ける花です。

桔梗は、だれの目にもまず涼しげに映ります。青紫の星のような輪郭、すっと立つ茎、開き切ってもどこか余白を感じさせる花のかたち。けれど茶席で桔梗が面白いのは、単に「涼しそうだから」ではありません。林野庁箕面森林ふれあい推進センターの紹介にもあるように、桔梗は秋の七草の一つでありながら、花期は七月から九月と、暑さの盛りのころから咲きます。つまりこの花は、植物としては真夏に現れながら、文化の記憶のなかでは早くも秋の側に立っている。その二重性こそ、茶の湯にとっての魅力です。目の前には夏の花があるのに、心はもう少し先の季節へ細く伸びてゆく。桔梗はその橋を、声高ではなく、きわめて静かに架けます。

秋の七草の話になると、よく知られた山上憶良の歌が思い出されます。国立国会図書館レファレンス協同データベースが整理しているように、秋の七草は『万葉集』巻八に収められた憶良の二首に由来すると考えられてきました。ただし、その歌に見える「朝貌」が現在のどの花を指すかについては、昔から議論があります。桔梗とみる説が有力とされる一方で、ムクゲやアサガオなどを挙げる見方も紹介されています。ここで大切なのは、断定を急がないことです。茶の湯の文章では、こうした曖昧さを不備ではなく、むしろ季節感の厚みとして受け止めるほうが自然に思えます。名が一つに固定されきらないからこそ、花は単なる図鑑上の標本ではなく、和歌の余韻を帯びた存在になる。桔梗を見ながら「これは秋の草だ」と言い切るのでなく、「秋を思わせる名の記憶が、この夏花に重なっている」と感じるほうが、床の間ではずっと豊かです。

茶席の花は、季節を説明するための見本ではありません。むしろ、季節の輪郭を少しだけずらして見せるために置かれます。たとえば真夏の席で向日葵の強さをそのまま前へ出せば、外の熱気と床の景色が競い合ってしまうかもしれません。けれど桔梗なら、暑さを否定せずに、そこへ別の方向をひらくことができます。青みのある冷静さ、線の細さ、そして秋の七草という文化的な記憶。そのため桔梗は、夏を消す花ではなく、夏を静かに整える花になります。暑い日に氷を増やすような冷やし方ではなく、視線の置き所をほんの少し先へ移して、体感まで和らげてしまう冷やし方です。

私は桔梗を茶席で思うとき、花そのものより、むしろ「間にある時間」を見ている気がします。春の花は立ち上がる季節の勢いを、晩秋の花は衰えの美しさを運びます。けれど桔梗がいるのは、もっと曖昧で、もっと繊細な場所です。まだ夏でありながら、夏だけではない時間。茶の湯はこの「あいだ」を愛してきました。だから立秋前後の菓子に、露、薄、初萩、野分、月など、少し早い秋語が忍びこむのも不思議ではありません。桔梗という花は、その言葉の側からも、花の側からも、同じ方向を指しています。季節は境目でいちばんよく見える、と。

ここで一つ丁寧に言っておきたいのは、桔梗の扱いがいつも同じであるとは限らないことです。どの花をいつ置くか、どこまで先取りを許すか、菓銘をどれほど早く秋へ寄せるかには、流儀、土地、先生、稽古の場の気候感覚によって差があります。ある席ではまだ盛夏として見せ、別の席ではごく軽く初秋の気配を添えるかもしれません。だから今朝の話も、「桔梗とはこう使うべきだ」という教則ではありません。むしろ、桔梗が持つ二重の時間感覚に耳を澄ませるための読み物です。花が文化になるとき、それは植物学だけでは決まりません。人がどんな季節をそこに託してきたかで、見え方が変わります。

また、桔梗は形のうえでも茶の湯に向いています。花弁は開いていても騒がしくならず、中心は深く、全体は平たく見えてどこか奥行きがある。星形と言えば華やかに聞こえますが、桔梗の星はきらびやかな星ではなく、夕方前の空に先に見える、まだ明るさの中にある星のようです。そのため床に一輪あるだけで、席の気分が少し遠くを見るものになります。近くの涼ではなく、これから来る涼しさを待つ目になる。私はそこに、茶の湯らしい節度を感じます。足りないから補うのではなく、満ちているものを少し控えて、その先にあるものを迎える準備をする節度です。

もし桔梗に合わせて菓子を考えるなら、露を打ったような表面、薄青や白、あるいは紫をほのかに含む意匠がよく似合います。ただし、花をそのまま写す必要はありません。むしろ「桔梗だから桔梗形」と短絡せず、涼味と早秋の気配だけを受け取るほうが、席は奥行きを持ちます。菓銘にしても同じで、露草のように夏を深める言い方もあれば、初風や野辺の月のように一歩先へ進む言い方もある。桔梗はその両方にまたがれる花です。夏の中にいて、秋の言葉を邪魔しない。だからこそ、一輪で場の季節を静かに調律できます。

今朝、桔梗を「秋の花」と決めてしまう必要はありません。むしろ、夏の最中にもう秋の記憶を帯びている花として見ること、その重なりを楽しむことが大切です。茶の湯では、季節は一枚の札ではなく、幾枚もの薄い紙が少しずつずれて重なったようにやって来ます。桔梗はそのずれ目を見せてくれる花です。暑さのただなかで、先に秋を言い過ぎず、それでも何も気づかない顔はしない。その控えめな予告こそ、盛夏の茶席にふさわしい品位なのだと思います。

## 今日の見どころ

桔梗を見かけたら、花の色より先に、形の「ひらき方」を見てみてください。大きく開いているのに広がりすぎず、中心へ視線がすっと戻るなら、その花は涼しさだけでなく、静かな集中も運んでいます。茶席での桔梗の良さは、派手に秋を主張しないのに、見ている人の心だけを半歩先の季節へ送るところにあります。

## 小さな実践

今朝は外に出なくてもかまいません。机の上でも窓の外でもよいので、「まだ今ここにあるもの」と「少し先を思わせるもの」を一組探してください。たとえば強い日差しと、まだ鳴き止まない蝉。冷たいコップと、その向こうの白い雲。見つけたら、三呼吸だけ、その二つを同時に見ます。どちらか一方に決めず、季節が重なっている状態をそのまま受け取る稽古です。桔梗を床に見るときの感覚を、道具なしで試す小さな練習になります。

## Full English Edition

# How Far Can a Bellflower Carry Autumn into a Summer Tea Gathering?

At the beginning of August, the tea room is given a slightly difficult task. The heat is still unmistakably summer, and yet our seasonal feeling no longer remains entirely inside high summer. On the calendar, the approach of *risshu*, the first day of autumn, is near. In the angle of light, in the mood of late afternoon, in the naming of sweets and the choice of flowers, the faintest advance notice begins to appear. Chanoyu has always loved this kind of subtle shift. It does not wait for autumn to become obvious before acknowledging it. Instead, it senses the first intimation of autumn already folded into summer, and lets that feeling appear in a single flower, a single word, a single bowl of tea. The flower I want to sit with this morning is the kikyo, the bellflower.

At first glance, kikyo seems simply cool and clear. Its blue-violet shape resembles a star. Its stem rises cleanly. Even when fully open, the flower seems to preserve a sense of empty space. But what makes kikyo so interesting in the tea room is not merely that it “looks refreshing.” As the Forestry Agency’s Minoo Forest Center notes, kikyo is one of the Seven Flowers of Autumn, yet its blooming season runs from July through September, beginning in the height of summer. In other words, as a plant it appears in midsummer, while in cultural memory it already stands on the side of autumn. That doubleness is exactly what makes it so appealing for tea. Before our eyes is a summer flower, yet the mind is quietly drawn toward the season just ahead. Kikyo builds that bridge without raising its voice.

When the Seven Flowers of Autumn are mentioned, people often recall the poems associated with Yamanoue no Okura. As summarized by the National Diet Library’s Reference Cooperative Database, the grouping of the autumn seven grasses has long been traced to two poems in Book 8 of the *Manyoshu*. Yet the flower called *asagao* or *asagao/asagao no hana* in that poetic context has been debated for a very long time. One influential interpretation identifies it with kikyo, while other views have named mukuge or the modern morning glory. It matters, I think, not to rush toward certainty here. In tea writing, this kind of ambiguity can be received not as a flaw but as a depth of seasonal feeling. Because the name does not settle too neatly into a single botanical answer, the flower remains more than a specimen in a guidebook. It keeps a poetic aftertaste. In the alcove, it is often richer not to say, “This is definitely an autumn flower,” but rather, “The remembered name of autumn overlays this summer bloom.”

Tea-room flowers are not display samples meant to explain the season. More often, they are placed in order to let us see the outline of a season slightly askew. If one brought the force of a sunflower directly into a midsummer tea room, the heat outdoors and the heat in the alcove might begin to compete with each other. Kikyo works differently. Without denying summer, it opens another direction inside it. Its cool blue cast, its linear delicacy, and the cultural memory of autumn carried in its name all help it do this. It does not erase summer. It quietly composes it. This is not the kind of cooling that comes from adding more ice. It is the kind that shifts the eye a little forward, and by doing so softens the body’s sense of heat as well.

Whenever I think of kikyo in tea, I feel I am seeing not the flower alone but the time that lies between times. Spring flowers carry the momentum of a season rising. Late-autumn flowers carry the beauty of decline. Kikyo lives somewhere more ambiguous and more refined: a time that is still summer and yet no longer only summer. Tea has always cherished that in-between. That is why it feels natural that around the approach of autumn, sweets and poetic names begin to admit words like dew, pampas grass, first bush clover, autumn wind, or moon before the weather has fully changed. Kikyo points in that same direction both as a flower and as a cultural sign. It suggests that seasons are seen most clearly at their edges.

One careful note is necessary here: the handling of kikyo is not always identical everywhere. The timing of flowers, the degree to which a host may anticipate the next season, and the question of how early a sweet may lean toward autumn all vary by school, region, teacher, and the climate-sense of a particular practice environment. In one gathering, kikyo may still be shown entirely as midsummer. In another, it may lightly carry the first breath of early autumn. So this morning’s feature is not a rulebook. It is a reading of the flower’s double sense of time. Once a flower enters culture, it is no longer determined by botany alone. It is also shaped by the seasons people have entrusted to it.

Kikyo is also well suited to tea in purely visual terms. Its petals open without becoming noisy. Its center feels deep. The whole flower can seem flat at first glance and yet still possesses interior depth. “Star-shaped” may sound decorative, but the star of kikyo is not a glittering one. It is more like the first star seen before evening has darkened, still suspended in remaining light. For that reason, even one stem in the alcove can make the room feel as if it is looking a little farther away. It is not only immediate coolness that we sense, but a readiness for the coolness that is still to come. To me, that is a particularly tea-like form of discipline: not compensating for lack, but gently restraining what is already abundant so that what is approaching may be welcomed.

If one were to pair a sweet with kikyo, a surface like dew, touches of pale blue, white, or the slightest violet would sit naturally beside it. But there is no need to copy the flower literally. In fact, the room often gains more depth when the host does not reduce the matter to “kikyo flower, therefore kikyo-shaped sweet,” but instead receives only the coolness and the hint of early autumn. The same is true of a sweet’s poetic name. One might choose a name that deepens summer, like dew-laden grass, or one that steps a little forward, like first wind or moon over the fields. Kikyo can stand with both. It remains inside summer while not obstructing the language of autumn. That is why a single bloom can tune the season of a gathering so quietly.

This morning, there is no need to decide once and for all that kikyo “is” an autumn flower. It is enough to see it as a flower that already carries the memory of autumn while still blooming in the middle of summer. In tea, the seasons do not arrive as a single card turned over at once. They arrive like several thin sheets of paper, slightly misaligned, layered one over another. Kikyo lets us notice the place where those sheets slip. In the center of heat, it does not speak of autumn too loudly, and yet it does not pretend not to notice what is coming. That restrained form of advance notice feels to me like the right dignity for a midsummer tea gathering.

## Vocabulary

1. 桔梗（ききょう） - bellflower. Usage: In tea and seasonal writing, it often suggests coolness and the first edge of autumn within summer.
2. 茶花（ちゃばな） - tea-room flower. Usage: Refers to flowers placed with restraint for chanoyu, not decorative floral display in general.
3. 秋の七草（あきのななくさ） - the Seven Flowers of Autumn. Usage: A cultural grouping often invoked in poetry and seasonal awareness rather than a practical set like the spring seven herbs.
4. 山上憶良（やまのうえのおくら） - Yamanoue no Okura. Usage: Best introduced as the poet associated with the classic poems behind the autumn seven grasses.
5. 万葉集（まんようしゅう） - *Manyoshu*, Japan’s early poetry anthology. Usage: A foundational source when discussing classical seasonal language.
6. 朝貌（あさがお） - *asagao* in classical texts. Usage: Important because its identification is debated; avoid assuming it always means the modern morning glory.
7. 立秋（りっしゅう） - the first day of autumn in the traditional calendar. Usage: Useful for describing when seasonal feeling begins to shift before the weather truly changes.
8. 床の間（とこのま） - alcove. Usage: The place where a scroll or flower quietly establishes the emotional key of the room.
9. 菓銘（かめい） - the poetic name of a sweet. Usage: Often carries seasonal nuance even before the sweet is eaten.
10. 涼味（りょうみ） - a sense of coolness. Usage: Can describe not only taste but also visual or atmospheric coolness in tea culture.
11. 先取り（さきどり） - anticipating the next season slightly ahead of time. Usage: In tea, this requires judgment; too much feels forced, too little feels dull.
12. 余韻（よいん） - lingering resonance or aftertaste. Usage: Helpful for describing how poetry, flowers, or gestures continue in the mind after the moment passes.
