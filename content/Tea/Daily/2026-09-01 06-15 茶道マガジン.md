# 宗和のやわらかさは、薄さではない

今朝は、強く語らない洗練がどのように茶の湯の歴史を動かしたのかを、金森宗和の静かな輪郭からたどってみましょう。

## 日本語特集

火曜日の朝に茶人をひとり選ぶなら、今日は金森宗和に向かいたいと思います。千利休のように茶の湯そのものの地軸を動かした人として語られることは少なく、小堀遠州のように広く名が通っているとも言いがたい。それでも、江戸初期の茶の湯が武家の緊張だけでもなく、利休の侘びの反復だけでもなく、もっと細やかな光を帯びて広がっていったことを知るには、宗和ほどよい入口はありません。

宗和は天正十二年、一五八四年に飛騨高山城主の家に生まれました。岐阜県図書館の解説では、のちに父から勘当されて京都へ移り、剃髪して「宗和」と号し、公家たちとの交わりのなかで茶人として名を成したと説明されています。ここで大切なのは、この経歴を単に数奇な身の上話として受け取らないことです。武家の家に生まれた人が、京都で宮廷文化の呼吸に長く触れた。その重なりが、宗和の茶を独特なものにしたからです。

京都国立博物館は、利休以後に広がる茶の湯の多様化を語る場面で、宗和の茶風を「姫宗和」と呼ばれる繊細で優美なものとして紹介しています。ただ、この呼び名は便利であるぶん、少し誤解も招きます。「姫」と言うと、すぐにやさしい、甘い、装飾的、という像へ流れてしまうからです。けれど宗和のやわらかさは、ただ人を喜ばせるための表面の美ではなかったはずです。むしろ、武家の茶が持っていた骨格を残したまま、ことさらに荒々しさを見せず、宮廷のことばや衣のような細い品位へ調律していく仕事だったのではないでしょうか。

相国寺承天閣美術館の英語解説では、宗和の系統の茶が “elegantly simple” な “kirei-sabi” として言及されています。ここでも興味深いのは、華美ではなく、あくまで簡素さを保ちながら別の明るさを獲得している点です。利休以後の茶の湯は、侘びを守るか、華やぎへ向かうか、という二者択一だけで進んだわけではありません。宗和の位置は、そのあいだにあります。削ぎ落とす感覚を失わず、それでいて場を痩せさせない。質素でありながら乾きすぎない。その中間の難しさを引き受けた人として見ると、宗和は急に現代的な茶人に見えてきます。

その感覚は、宗和と野々村仁清の関係を通しても見えてきます。岐阜県図書館は、宗和が仁清の御室焼を援助し、茶道界との仲介役を果たしたと述べています。さらに京都国立博物館には、金森宗和が天寧寺へ寄進した一揃いの茶道具のうちのひとつとして、仁清作の《銹絵水仙文茶碗》が伝わっています。ここから直線的に「宗和が仁清を育てた」とまで言い切るのは言い過ぎでしょう。焼き物の成立には、陶工自身の技術、注文主の趣味、当時の需要が複雑に重なります。ただ少なくとも、宗和がどんな器をよしとし、どんな気分を茶席へ招き入れたかったかを考えるうえで、仁清の端正さと余白のある意匠はよい手がかりになります。

宗和ゆかりの茶室についても、伝承は一枚岩ではありません。宗和流の公式史には、真珠庵の庭玉軒、東京国立博物館に移築された六窓庵、金閣寺の夕佳亭などが「宗和好」として伝わる一方、由来には諸説あって定かでないものもあると明記されています。この慎重さは大事です。茶の湯の歴史は、確かな年号だけでできているのではなく、「誰の好みとして伝わったか」という記憶の層でもできています。宗和を学ぶ面白さは、その曖昧さを嫌って切り捨てることではなく、むしろ、なぜ後代の人びとがその茶室に宗和らしさを見たのかを考えるところにあります。窓の取り方、光の入り方、こまやかな優美さ。そうした感覚の束が、「宗和らしい」という評価を後から形づくっていったのでしょう。

朝の一服へ引き寄せるなら、宗和が教えてくれるのは、強い個性だけが歴史を残すわけではないということかもしれません。声高な革新ではなく、場の調子を一段細く整えること。荒さを消すのではなく、粗さを品位へ変換すること。茶の湯には、ときどきそういう静かな編集者が現れます。宗和はその一人でした。やわらかいのに、弱くはない。優美なのに、空疎ではない。今朝の茶の湯史は、その微妙な強さに耳を澄ませるところから始めてみたいのです。

## 今日の見どころ

茶室や器を見るとき、まず「派手か地味か」で分けず、どこに骨格が残り、どこで力が抜かれているかを見てみてください。宗和的な美しさは、飾りを足したところより、強さをやわらかく言い換えたところに現れやすいはずです。

## 小さな実践

家にある湯のみや白い器をひとつ選び、真正面からではなく、少し斜めから一分ほど見てみてください。線のきついところ、やわらぐところ、光を強く返すところ、黙って沈むところを四つだけ心の中で数えます。そのあと、「きれい」と言う代わりに、どこがどう静かかを十秒だけ言葉にしてみましょう。特別な道具は要りません。評価するのでなく、品位がどこで生まれるのかを観察する小さな練習です。

## Full English Edition

# Sowa’s Softness Is Not Weakness

This morning, let us trace how an understated refinement helped move tea history, by following the quiet outline of Kanamori Sowa.

If I were to choose one tea figure for a Tuesday morning, I would turn to Kanamori Sowa. He is not often described as someone who shifted the axis of tea the way Sen no Rikyu did, nor is his name as widely familiar as Kobori Enshu’s. And yet, if we want to understand how early Edo tea spread beyond the tension of warrior culture and beyond a mere repetition of Rikyu’s wabi, into a subtler and more finely lit world, Sowa offers an unusually good entrance.

Sowa was born in 1584 into the family of the Takayama castle lords in Hida. The Gifu Prefectural Library explains that after being disinherited by his father, he moved to Kyoto, took Buddhist vows, adopted the name “Sowa,” and established himself as a tea figure through long association with court nobles. That biography matters for more than its drama. A man born in a warrior household came to spend much of his life breathing the air of Kyoto court culture. The overlap between those two worlds is what gave Sowa’s tea its distinct tone.

The Kyoto National Museum, in its discussion of the widening diversity of tea culture after Rikyu, describes Sowa’s style as the delicate and graceful manner later called *Hime-Sowa*. The phrase is useful, but it can also mislead. The word *hime* easily tempts us toward an image of prettiness, softness, or decorative charm. But Sowa’s gentleness was probably not a matter of surface sweetness. It seems more accurate to think of it as a disciplined recalibration: keeping the structural backbone of warrior tea while tuning it toward the finer dignity of courtly language, textiles, and social atmosphere, without displaying roughness for its own sake.

An official English text from the Jotenkaku Museum of Shokoku-ji refers to Kanamori Sowa’s school as an “elegantly simple” form of *kirei-sabi*. That phrasing is revealing. What is at stake is not luxury, but another kind of clarity achieved without abandoning simplicity. Tea after Rikyu did not develop through a simple opposition between preserving wabi and turning toward splendor. Sowa stands in the middle space between those poles. He does not lose the instinct to pare things down, yet he does not let the room become dry or thin. He keeps restraint, but refuses lifelessness. When seen that way, Sowa starts to feel surprisingly modern.

That sensibility also appears in the often-mentioned relation between Sowa and the potter Nonomura Ninsei. The Gifu Prefectural Library notes that Sowa supported Ninsei’s Omuro ware and acted as a mediator between the tea world and the potter. The Kyoto National Museum also preserves, as part of a complete set of utensils donated by Sowa to Tennei-ji Temple, Ninsei’s tea bowl with narcissus decoration. It would be too simple to turn this into a straight line and say, “Sowa made Ninsei.” Ceramics emerge through the potter’s own skill, patron taste, workshop conditions, and historical demand. Still, if we want to sense what kinds of forms Sowa found persuasive, Ninsei’s poised shapes and spacious designs give us a strong clue. They show a refinement that is not loud.

Even the tea rooms associated with Sowa remind us to be careful. The official Sowa-ryu history presents spaces such as Tengyokuken at Shinju-an, Rokusoan now relocated to the Tokyo National Museum, and Yukkatei at Kinkaku-ji as tea rooms traditionally linked with Sowa’s taste, while also stating that some origins remain uncertain and multiple theories exist. That caution matters. Tea history is not built from firm dates alone. It is also built from layers of memory about whose taste a room was said to embody. Part of the pleasure of studying Sowa lies not in discarding those ambiguities, but in asking why later generations saw “Sowa-ness” there at all. Was it the way windows were placed, the manner in which light entered, the delicacy of proportion, the quiet elegance of the whole? Such clusters of feeling may be exactly how a historical sensibility survives.

Drawn back into a morning bowl, Sowa may teach us that history is not shaped only by forceful personalities. Sometimes it is shaped by those who refine the atmosphere by a single degree. Not by erasing roughness, but by translating it into poise. Tea history occasionally advances through such quiet editors. Sowa was one of them. Soft, but not weak. Graceful, but not empty. This morning, it may be enough to begin by listening for that subtle strength.

## What to Notice Today

When looking at a tea room or utensil, resist dividing it immediately into “showy” or “plain.” Instead, notice where its structure remains firm and where its force has been gently released. A Sowa-like beauty often appears not where ornament has been added, but where strength has been translated into softness.

## A Small Practice

Choose one teacup or white vessel at home and look at it for one minute from a slight angle rather than straight on. Silently identify four things: where a line feels sharp, where it softens, where light is strongly reflected, and where it settles quietly. Then, instead of simply calling the vessel “beautiful,” spend ten seconds describing exactly where its calm dignity comes from. No special utensil is needed; this is a small exercise in observing how refinement takes shape.

## Vocabulary

1. **宗和好み（そうわごのみ）** — objects or spaces associated with Sowa’s taste
   Used when a tea room, utensil, or style is traditionally attributed to Kanamori Sowa.
2. **姫宗和（ひめそうわ）** — the graceful style linked with Sowa
   A historical label for Sowa’s refined tea manner; use carefully because it can oversimplify.
3. **綺麗さび（きれいさび）** — elegant simplicity
   Often used for tea aesthetics that keep restraint while gaining polish or brightness.
4. **公家（くげ）** — court nobility
   Important for understanding the courtly circles with whom Sowa associated in Kyoto.
5. **武家（ぶけ）** — warrior household or samurai class
   Useful when contrasting the tone of warrior culture with courtly refinement.
6. **剃髪（ていはつ）** — taking the tonsure
   Refers to entering a religious life or shaving the head in that context.
7. **余白（よはく）** — intentional empty space
   Can describe both visual spacing in a design and emotional openness in a room.
8. **端正（たんせい）** — poised, well-balanced, exact
   Good for describing Ninsei’s vessel shapes or a quietly disciplined style.
9. **意匠（いしょう）** — design motif or visual conception
   Use when discussing surface decoration or the design logic of a utensil.
10. **由来（ゆらい）** — origin or transmission history
   Helpful when talking about uncertain attributions in tea history.
11. **伝承（でんしょう）** — received tradition
   Use for stories or associations handed down even when documentary proof is incomplete.
12. **品位（ひんい）** — dignity, refinement
   A useful word for the kind of quiet strength this feature tries to notice.
