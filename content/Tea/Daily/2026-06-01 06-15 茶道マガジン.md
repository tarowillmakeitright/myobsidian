## 今日の特集（日本語）

### 風炉の季節、音で点前を磨く

六月に入り、茶の湯は炉から風炉へ。炭の位置、釜の座り、柄杓の運び、すべてが少し軽やかになる季節です。見た目の違いに目が行きがちですが、実はこの時期こそ「音」を手がかりにすると、点前の質がぐっと上がります。

茶道でいう「よい音」は、大きい音ではありません。必要な所作が、無理なく、道具に負担をかけずに行われた結果として生まれる音です。たとえば、柄杓を釜の縁に当てるとき。乾いた硬い音が出るときは、角度が急すぎるか、手元に力が入りすぎています。逆に、やわらかく短い音で収まるときは、手首の返しと呼吸がそろっていることが多い。

建水を扱う所作も同じです。置くときに「コトン」と一度で止まるのが理想で、「ゴトッ」と揺れるときは、置き場所の確認が遅れているサイン。茶碗を畳に戻す動きでも、畳目に対してまっすぐ下ろせていれば、余計な摩擦音は出ません。音は、先生が横にいなくても、自分の癖を教えてくれる“もう一人の指導者”です。

#### 朝稽古でできる「音チェック」3ステップ

1. **最初の5分は無言で**
   あいさつの後、あえて会話を止め、道具の触れる音だけを聞きます。視線より先に耳を使うと、所作の粗さが見つかりやすくなります。

2. **一つだけテーマを決める**
   今日は「柄杓の置き音だけ」、明日は「茶筅通しの音だけ」というように、一回の稽古で観察対象を絞る。全部を一気に直すより、上達が速くなります。

3. **終わりに一行メモ**
   「置く瞬間に息が止まる」「右手に力が残る」など、体感を短く記録。次回の稽古前に読み返すと、注意点が身体に戻りやすいです。

茶道は静けさの芸道と言われますが、静けさは“無音”ではありません。余計な音を減らし、必要な音を整えることで、亭主の心は客にまっすぐ届きます。風炉の季節は、見た目より先に音を整える。これが、夏の点前を上品にする近道です。

## Today’s Feature (English)

### Furo Season: Refining Temae Through Sound

June marks a subtle but important shift in tea practice: we move from the sunken winter hearth (*ro*) to the portable summer brazier (*furo*). Most learners first notice visual changes—layout, spacing, and overall lightness of the room. But the deeper improvement often comes from something less obvious: **sound**.

In sadō, “good sound” does not mean loud sound. It means sound that naturally appears when movement is efficient, balanced, and respectful to tools. Every contact point—ladle to kettle, bowl to tatami, kensui to floor—creates feedback. If you learn to hear it, your form improves faster.

Take the ladle (*hishaku*) as an example. When the rim contact is sharp and dry, there is usually extra force in the wrist or a rushed angle. When the sound is soft, brief, and controlled, hand position and breathing are often aligned. That tiny moment tells you whether your body is centered—or whether you are “doing” the movement instead of letting it happen.

The wastewater bowl (*kensui*) gives equally clear clues. Ideally, when you place it down, it settles once—cleanly. If it rattles or lands heavily, your placement decision came too late, or your core was not stable during descent. With the tea bowl (*chawan*), unnecessary scraping noise often signals that your descent path is not straight relative to tatami grain.

In other words, sound is a private teacher. Even when no instructor is beside you, the room tells you what your body is doing.

#### A Practical Morning Drill: 3-Step “Sound Check”

**1) Begin with five silent minutes**  
After greetings, avoid casual talk at first. Listen only to tool contact sounds. Prioritizing hearing before visual judgment makes technical roughness easier to detect.

**2) Choose one sound focus per session**  
Don’t audit everything at once. Today: ladle placement. Tomorrow: whisk rinse. Next day: bowl return. Narrow focus creates faster and more measurable improvement.

**3) End with a one-line body memo**  
Write one short observation: “I hold my breath before placing kensui,” or “my right shoulder stays tense after bowl rotation.” This bridges one session to the next and accelerates correction.

#### Why This Matters in Furo Practice

Summer temae can look deceptively simple. Because movements are lighter, small inefficiencies become more visible—and more audible. If you refine sound quality, your motions naturally become quieter, cleaner, and more gracious. Guests may not consciously identify each sound, but they feel the difference in atmosphere.

Tea is often described as an art of silence, but it is really an art of **intentional quiet**. Not the absence of all sound—rather the removal of unnecessary sound. In furo season, that distinction becomes especially clear.

So for today’s practice, try this lens: before correcting appearance, correct resonance. When sound becomes calm, form follows. And when form follows, hospitality reaches the guest without friction.
