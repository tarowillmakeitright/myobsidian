# 綴じ目を見せる、という仕事

朝の手を少しゆるめて、今日は木の道具に残された一本の綴じ目から、茶の湯の静かな几帳面さを眺めてみましょう。

今朝の東京は薄曇りで26℃、湿り気を含んだ空気のなかでは、木地の道具の乾いた線がいっそう静かに見えます。

## 日本語特集

茶席で建水は、たいてい主役として語られません。茶碗のように手に取って正面を味わうものでもなく、釜のように席の中心で湯を抱えているわけでもない。清めたあとの湯や水を受けるその役目は、どこか裏方めいていて、ことさらに目立たぬことが似合います。だからこそ、木地曲の建水に残る綴じ目が、客の目に入る向きへと整えられると知ると、茶の湯の感覚は少しだけ深く感じられます。目立たぬもののなかにも、見せるべき仕事があるのです。

裏千家の案内では、建水は「こぼし」とも呼ばれ、金属、陶磁器、木地曲などさまざまな素材のものがあると説明されます。つまり建水は、単なる排水の器ではなく、素材感を含めて席の気配を支える道具です。そのなかで木地曲の建水は、塗りもののつやとも、焼きものの景色とも違う、木の薄板を曲げて立ち上げた軽さを持っています。見た目は静かでも、作りは驚くほど能動的です。木を削り、ぬらし、熱を入れ、曲げ、綴じ、底を仕込み、ようやく一つの輪が器として落ち着く。その「輪になった痕跡」が、綴じ目として残ります。

曲物一般の説明を読むと、この綴じ目は単なる意匠ではありません。大館曲げわっぱの公的な紹介では、17世紀後半に大館城主佐竹西家のもとで副業として奨励され発展したとされ、天然秋田杉を薄く剥ぎ、熱湯でやわらかくして曲げ、山桜の皮で縫い止める工程が示されています。伝統工芸の技法説明でも、柾目板を用い、煮沸の後に手作業で曲げ、樺縫いにサクラの樹皮を用いることが明記されています。つまり綴じ目は、木が無理なく曲がるための知恵であると同時に、異なる素材が協力して器を保たせる結び目です。杉だけでは終わらず、桜皮の一手が最後に輪を閉じる。その協働の跡が、建水の側面に一筋だけ残るわけです。

ここでおもしろいのは、茶の湯がその痕跡を隠さないことです。少なくとも裏千家では、木地曲の建水について、最初は綴じ目を手前にして仕組み、蓋置と柄杓を扱ったのち、内側の綴じ目が客付に向くように回して位置を正す説明があります。客から見て、その細工が一つの景色になるようにするためです。これは「どの流儀でも必ず同じ」と言い切るべき話ではありませんが、少なくとも一つの系譜では、実用のための継ぎ目が、そのまま鑑賞に耐える部分として扱われている。私はここに、茶の湯らしい正直さを見る気がします。完璧に隠されたものを称えるのではなく、支えの痕跡を、支えのまま整えて見せるのです。

綴じ目は、傷ではありません。もちろん、荒く裂けた線であれば道具の品を損ねるでしょう。けれど、ていねいに留められた綴じ目は、「ここで器が成立している」という告白でもあります。むしろ器の成立条件がそのまま景色になる。茶の湯では、ときどきこうした逆転が起こります。使い古したものが侘びを帯び、節のある竹がかえって好まれ、木目や歪みが無言の表情になる。木地曲の建水の綴じ目も、その仲間に見えてきます。道具が無垢の均質さを競うのではなく、成り立ちを静かに差し出すとき、客は器の外面だけでなく、時間と手数まで見ることになります。

そして建水という道具の立場も、ここでは意味深く感じられます。受ける器であること。捨てるための水を預かること。茶碗や茶杓のように正面性を誇らず、それでも最後に景色を持つこと。華やかさで席を支えるのではなく、始末のよさで席を支えること。月曜日の朝に読むには、ずいぶんよい道具です。見えやすい成果よりも、崩れないための継ぎ目に気を配ること。人にも仕事にも、そういう月曜があっていいはずです。

## 今日の見どころ

木の道具を見かけたら、まず「何が美しいか」ではなく、「どこで成り立っているか」を見てみてください。輪を閉じている場所、力が集まる場所、異なる素材が出会う場所。茶の湯では、そうした支えの地点が、しばしばいちばん静かな見どころになります。

## 小さな実践

紙コップでも、箱でも、ノートの背でもかまいません。身近な物を一つ取り、その物が「どこでつながっているか」を三十秒だけ観察してください。継ぎ目、折り目、留め具、縫い目。次に、自分の今日の予定のなかで「目立たないけれど崩れると困る継ぎ目」は何かを一つ思い浮かべます。メールを一本返すことでも、机を一度拭くことでも、出かける前に水を飲むことでもいい。その小さな始末を、朝の一服になぞらえて丁寧に済ませてみてください。

## Full English Edition

# The Work of Letting the Seam Be Seen

Loosen your hands a little this morning, and let us look at a single stitched seam in a wooden tea utensil to glimpse the quiet exactness of chanoyu.

The kensui, the vessel that receives the used hot water and rinse water, is rarely spoken of as the star of the room. It is not lifted and turned like a tea bowl, nor does it sit at the center of the gathering like the kettle, holding the water that will become tea. Its role is closer to backstage work. It is there to receive what has already finished its task. That is precisely why it feels striking to learn that, in a bentwood kensui, the seam may be arranged so that the guest can see it. Even in an unobtrusive object, there is work worth showing.

An introductory Urasenke guide explains that the kensui, also called koboshi, may be made of metal, ceramic, or bent wood. In other words, it is not merely a container for discarded water. Its material participates in the atmosphere of the room. A bentwood kensui carries a different feeling from lacquer or clay. It has the lightness of a vessel raised from a thin wooden sheet and persuaded into a circle. The appearance may be calm, but the making is active: the wood is cut, moistened, heated, bent, stitched, and fitted with a base before the form can settle into usefulness. The trace of that becoming remains visible as the seam.

When we look at public descriptions of bentwood craft, that seam is clearly more than decoration. The official Odate City introduction to Odate magewappa says the craft was encouraged and developed in the late seventeenth century under the Satake Nishike in Odate, and describes thin strips of natural Akita cedar being softened in hot water, bent, and sewn with wild cherry bark. The Aoyama Square page on traditional crafts likewise specifies straight-grain cedar boards, bending by hand after boiling, and the use of cherry bark for the stitching called kaba-nui. So the seam is both practical intelligence and visible structure. It is the place where a circle becomes possible. Cedar alone does not finish the vessel; another material closes the form and lets it endure.

What is especially moving in tea is that this trace need not be hidden. In an Urasenke question-and-answer explanation about a bentwood kensui, the utensil is first arranged with the seam toward the host's side; after the lid rest and ladle are handled, it is turned so that the inner seam faces the guest, making that workmanship part of the view. I would not present this as a universal rule for all schools or lineages. Tea traditions differ, and handling conventions are not identical everywhere. But in at least this stream of practice, the join made for use is also treated as something worth seeing. That feels deeply characteristic of chanoyu. Tea does not always praise what looks flawless because its supports are invisible. Sometimes it honors a support precisely by letting it remain legible.

The seam is not a defect. Of course, a rough or careless join would diminish the dignity of the utensil. But a carefully finished seam tells the truth about how the vessel stands. In that sense it is almost a confession: this is where the form holds. Chanoyu often makes room for this kind of reversal. A node in bamboo may become more interesting than smooth perfection. Wear may deepen a sense of wabi rather than weaken it. Grain, irregularity, and restraint can become expression. The seam of a bentwood kensui belongs to that family of meanings. The utensil does not compete through uniform purity. Instead, it offers its making, quietly, as part of its face.

The role of the kensui deepens the lesson. It is a vessel that receives. It holds what has already been used and passed on. It does not insist on a proud front, yet it may still carry a view. It supports the room not through brilliance but through good finishing and orderly aftermath. For a Monday morning, that is a fine utensil to contemplate. There are days when the most important thing is not display but the seam that keeps the day from coming apart. Human work is often like that as well.

## Vocabulary

1. 綴じ目（とじめ）- seam, stitched join
   Usage: A useful word for the visible join where a bentwood utensil is fastened.
2. 建水（けんすい）- wastewater vessel in tea; kensui
   Usage: Often also called `こぼし`; it receives used hot water or rinse water.
3. 木地曲（きじまげ）- bentwood made from natural wood grain
   Usage: Refers to wooden utensils formed by bending thin wood rather than carving a solid block.
4. 曲物（まげもの）- bentwood ware
   Usage: A broader craft term; not every `曲物` is a tea utensil.
5. 樺縫い（かばぬい）- stitching with cherry bark
   Usage: A craft term used in descriptions of traditional bentwood construction.
6. 柾目（まさめ）- straight grain
   Usage: Often used when discussing boards chosen for clean, stable grain lines.
7. 煮沸（しゃふつ）- boiling, heat-softening by boiling
   Usage: In craft contexts, it can describe softening material before bending.
8. 客付（きゃくづき）- the guest-facing side
   Usage: Helpful for understanding how utensils are oriented in the tearoom.
9. 景色（けしき）- visual character, scenic feature
   Usage: In tea, this often means the interesting appearance a tool naturally offers.
10. 仕組む（しくむ）- to set up, arrange in preparation
    Usage: Used for arranging utensils in an ordered way before the procedure begins.
11. 始末（しまつ）- orderly finishing, proper handling
    Usage: In tea this can imply tidy completion, not just disposal.
12. 受ける器（うけるうつわ）- a vessel that receives
    Usage: Not a fixed technical term, but a useful phrase for the role of the kensui in this feature.
