# 床のない不審庵で、宗旦は何を残したのか

朝の東京は雲の切れ目に強い光がのぞく暑い気配ですが、そんな朝ほど、茶室から余分なものが引かれていく感覚を静かに味わいたくなります。

## 日本語特集

茶の湯の歴史を「誰が何を新しくしたか」で追うと、どうしても大きな名前ばかりが前に出ます。けれど火曜日の朝に少し立ち止まりたいのは、千利休の孫である千宗旦が、何かを足した人というより、どこまで引けるかを試した人として見えてくる場面です。その象徴のひとつが、不審庵です。

表千家の資料によれば、宗旦は寛永十年（一六三三）ごろ、床の間を持たない一畳台目の小間を建てたと考えられています。後年この茶室を不審庵と名づけ、正保三年（一六四六）には三男の江岑宗左へ譲りました。ただし、現在知られる表千家不審庵は、江岑宗左の代に三畳半へ改められた姿を含んでおり、宗旦当初の小間そのものがそのまま残っているわけではありません。裏千家の系譜資料を見ると、同じ一六四六年に宗旦は隠居し、屋敷の奥に今日庵を営みます。つまり、この年は一人の名人が静かに退いた年であると同時に、のちの三千家へつながる分岐が具体的な建物の配置として現れた年でもありました。

ここで大事なのは、「床がない」という事実を、単なる変わった設計として眺めないことです。床の間は、掛物を掲げ、季節や趣向の中心を示す場所です。そこをあえて持たない小間は、見どころをなくすための空白ではなく、客の視線をひとつの名物やひとつのことばへ固定しすぎないための厳しい選択だったのかもしれません。宗旦の茶は、利休の極小の侘びを受け継ぎながらも、それを声高に主張するより、暮らしの奥へ沈めるような静けさを持つと語られることがあります。不審庵の床なしという構えは、その静けさの建築的な言い方に見えてきます。

しかも、この小間は「何もない」わけではありません。表千家の説明には、南向き、茅葺、向切の炉、赤松の中柱、袖壁など、きわめて具体的な情報が残ります。削ぎ落とされているのは情報量ではなく、誇示です。茶室は狭く、要素は少なく、しかし一本の柱、一枚の壁、一つの切り方が、客の身体の向きや、湯の見え方や、沈黙の置き場を決めていく。茶の湯が「簡素」を目指すとき、それは雑に減らすことではなく、残すものの責任が重くなることなのだと、この小間は教えます。

宗旦のあと、江岑宗左、仙叟宗室、一翁宗守の系譜がそれぞれ表千家・裏千家・武者小路千家へつながっていきます。ただ、ここで「宗旦こそ三千家の創始者」と一直線に言い切るより、宗旦の周囲で茶室と住まいが静かに分かれ、それぞれの家がのちに自分たちの稽古と継承のかたちを育てていった、と見るほうが正確でしょう。ひとつの茶室がそのまま三つの流儀になったのではなく、引き算の美意識が、別々の家で別々の厚みを持ち始めた。その起点のひとつとして、不審庵は今もよく効く歴史のレンズです。

朝の一服に引き寄せて言えば、宗旦が残したものは、豪華な答えではなく、答えを急がせない小ささだったのかもしれません。狭いからこそ、客は自分の身体を収め直す。床がないからこそ、どこを見るかを自分で探す。名物が前に出すぎないからこそ、湯気や畳の目や柱の皮つきの気配が、思いがけず大きくなる。歴史とは出来事の年表だけでなく、何を目立たせずに残したかの積み重ねでもあるのだと、不審庵の話は静かに教えてくれます。

## 今日の見どころ

床の間がないと仮定したとき、あなたの目はどこへ落ち着くでしょう。器そのものよりも、柱と壁の間、湯気の立ち上がる位置、あるいは畳の余白に注意が向くなら、それは宗旦的な「引いたあとに残るもの」を見ているのかもしれません。

## 小さな実践

今日は机でも食卓でもかまわないので、目の前から「主役」をひとつだけ外してみてください。飾りでも、スマートフォンでも、鮮やかな包みでもよいです。そのあと一分だけ座り、残った空間のどこに視線が移るかを観察します。何かを足すのでなく、引いたあとに現れる秩序を確かめる、小さな宗旦の練習です。

## Full English Edition

# What Did Sotan Preserve in a Fushin-an Without an Alcove?

Tokyo begins this morning with harsh light breaking through gaps in the clouds and the sense of another hot day gathering strength. On a morning like this, it feels right to sit with the way a tea room can be stripped down, little by little, until what remains becomes sharper.

When the history of tea is told as a sequence of innovations, the large names naturally step forward. But the figure worth pausing over on this Tuesday morning is Sen Sotan, grandson of Sen no Rikyu, not so much as a man who added something new, but as someone who tested how far things could be pared away. One of the clearest symbols of that effort is Fushin-an.

According to Omotesenke sources, Sotan is thought to have built, around Kan'ei 10 (1633), a one-and-three-quarter-mat room of the ichijo-daime type with no tokonoma alcove. He later named this room Fushin-an, and in Shoho 3 (1646) handed it over to his third son, Koshin Sosa. At the same time, the Fushin-an known today in Omotesenke reflects changes made in Koshin's generation, when it was remodeled into a three-and-a-half-mat room, so we should not imagine that Sotan's original tiny room survives unchanged. Urasenke materials show that in the same year, 1646, Sotan retired and established Konnichian deeper within the property. That makes 1646 important not only because a tea master quietly withdrew, but because the branching that would later lead toward the three Sen houses began to take visible architectural form.

The crucial point is not to treat the lack of a tokonoma as a curiosity of design. In a tea room, the alcove is where a hanging scroll is displayed and where the host often places the central statement of season, theme, or intention. To omit it is not simply to create emptiness. It may instead be a severe decision not to force the guest's eye too firmly toward one celebrated object or one authoritative phrase. Sotan's tea is often described as carrying forward Rikyu's most concentrated wabi spirit while letting it settle more deeply into lived space rather than declaring itself loudly. A room without an alcove begins to look like an architectural form of that restraint.

And yet the room is not "nothing." Omotesenke's explanation preserves very concrete details: it faced south, had a thatched roof, used a mukogiri hearth, included a red-pine center post with bark still attached, and a sleeve wall. What has been reduced is not specificity but display. The room is tiny, the elements are few, and yet one post, one wall, one cut of the hearth governs the direction of the body, the way steam appears, and the place where silence settles. When chanoyu seeks simplicity, it does not mean reducing things carelessly. It means that whatever remains must carry more responsibility. That is one of the lessons this room offers.

After Sotan, the lines of Koshin Sosa, Senso Soshitsu, and Ichio Soshu would lead to Omotesenke, Urasenke, and Mushanokojisenke. Still, it is more accurate to say that around Sotan, tea rooms and household spaces quietly differentiated and that each family later developed its own ways of practice and transmission. One tea room did not mechanically become three schools. Rather, an aesthetic of subtraction began to acquire different thicknesses in different houses. Fushin-an remains a powerful historical lens because it lets us see one of those starting points with unusual clarity.

If we draw this close to a morning bowl of tea, what Sotan preserved may not have been a grand answer, but a smallness that refuses to hurry the answer. Because the room is narrow, the guest must settle the body anew. Because there is no alcove, the eye must search for itself. Because a famous object does not dominate too strongly, steam, tatami grain, and the bark left on a pillar unexpectedly grow in presence. History is not only a chronology of events. It is also an accumulation of choices about what may remain without being made conspicuous. The story of Fushin-an teaches that quietly and very well.

## Today's Focus

If there were no tokonoma alcove, where would your gaze come to rest? If your attention moved away from the utensils themselves and toward the interval between pillar and wall, the place where steam rises, or the open field of tatami, you might be seeing what remains after a Sotan-like act of subtraction.

## A Small Practice

Today, at your desk or dining table, remove just one thing that normally acts as the visual center: an ornament, your phone, or a brightly colored package. Sit for one minute and observe where your gaze moves in the space that remains. This is a small Sotan exercise—not adding something, but noticing the order that appears after something has been taken away.
