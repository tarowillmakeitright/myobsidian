# 朝顔の名にひそむ、ひとさじの秋

朝の花がひらく前の静けさを思いながら、今朝は「朝顔」という一語が茶席に運ぶ涼しさと、かすかな秋意をたどってみましょう。

## 日本語特集

金曜日の茶の湯は、道具そのものより、季節のことばが席の空気をどう変えるかを見たくなります。七月半ばの朝、もし主菓子の銘が「朝顔」だったら、私たちは何を受け取るでしょう。多くの人はまず、青や紫の花弁、まだ熱を帯びきらない朝の風、軒先の鉢にのぼるつるを思い浮かべるはずです。けれど茶の湯の面白さは、そこで思考を止めないところにあります。朝顔は、見た目には盛夏の花でありながら、季語の世界ではしばしば秋の入口にも触れているからです。

このずれは、茶席にとって欠点ではなく、むしろ豊かさです。茶の湯は、季節を単純なカレンダーとしてではなく、移ろいの手触りとして扱います。たとえば、朝顔は早朝に咲き、昼前にはしぼみます。その短さは「夏の勢い」よりもむしろ「消えてゆく涼」を感じさせることがあります。朝のうちだけ成り立つ美しさだからこそ、真昼の強い光より、夜明けの白さや露の気配とよく結びつく。茶席で朝顔が歓迎されるのは、花の派手さよりも、そのはかなさが朝の一服に似合うからでしょう。

さらに興味深いのは、「朝顔」という語そのものが、日本の古い季節感の中で一枚岩ではないことです。林野庁の広報でも、現在私たちが思い浮かべるアサガオの季語は旧暦に基づいて秋だと説明されています。また、秋の七草に見える「朝貌（あさがお）」は、現代のアサガオではなく桔梗を指す説が有力だと紹介されています。つまり「朝顔」という音は、時代によって、今の園芸的な朝顔をさしたり、野に咲く別の花の面影を帯びたりしてきました。茶の湯が好むのは、こうした断定しきれない境目です。名前が一つでも、見えている季節は一つではない。その重なりが、席に奥行きを生みます。

ここで和菓子に目を向けると、この感覚はいっそうはっきりします。菓銘の「朝顔」は、花の写生で終わる必要がありません。うっすらとした青のぼかし、白あんを包む葛の透け、あるいは焼印ひとつで、作り手は花そのものより「朝の涼しさ」を差し出すことができます。虎屋パリ店が2026年7月前半の生菓子「涼風」で、朝顔の焼印を添えながら、早朝の清々しい風を表したのはその好例です。ここで主役なのは植物学としての朝顔ではなく、朝顔が連れてくる空気です。茶席の菓子も同じで、銘は図鑑の名前ではなく、客の感覚をそっと導くための入口になります。

この「入口」の働きは、七夕や梶の葉の文化にも響きます。表千家不審菴の歳時記事では、七夕がもともと技芸上達を願う乞巧奠に由来し、梶の葉に詩歌を書く習わしへつながることが述べられています。七月の茶席に梶の葉や短冊の気配が添えられると、朝顔のような季節のことばは、単なる花ではなく「書くこと」「願うこと」「一夜の涼」を抱えた象徴へ変わります。花、菓子、ことば、願いごとがばらばらではなく、ひとつの朝の湿り気のなかで結び直されるのです。

私は、茶の湯における季節感の成熟は、この“ずれを嫌わない態度”にあると思います。現代の感覚で言えば、朝顔は夏です。それで間違いではありません。けれど旧暦の秋、万葉の語、七草の解釈、七夕の余韻、朝だけの開花、昼にはしぼむ姿、葛の透け感をもつ菓子の冷たさ、それらが重なると、朝顔は「夏の花」でありながら、すでに秋に触れることばになります。茶の湯は、この二重性を矛盾として処理しません。むしろ、夏のなかに先取りされた秋の気配を味わうことこそ、繊細な客の仕事だと静かに教えます。

だから、もし今朝の茶席に朝顔の菓子が出たなら、花弁の形だけを見て終わらないでいたいのです。少し透ける生地なら、そこに朝露の冷たさを読む。青のぼかしなら、空ではなくまだ明るみきらない水辺の色を思う。銘が「朝顔」でも、見ているものは花だけではない。朝にだけ開くもの、ほどなく閉じるもの、盛夏のなかに先んじて置かれた秋の兆し。そのような時間の薄い層を、ひとくちの甘みが教えてくれる。茶の湯は、季節を説明するのでなく、季節の境目に身体を置かせる文化なのだと思います。

朝顔は、茶席では大声を出す花ではありません。むしろ、早く来て早く去るものの代表として、こちらの感覚を少しだけ早起きさせる花です。暑さの本番に入る前の、わずかな白さ。願いごとを書く前の、まだ乾いていない梶の葉。菓子を口に入れる前の、ひと息の涼。そうしたものをひとつに束ねる言葉として、朝顔は今朝も十分に豊かです。真夏を見ながら秋の入口に触れる。その繊細な読み替えこそ、金曜の茶の湯が私たちにくれる静かな贈り物なのかもしれません。

## 今日の見どころ

もし茶席や写真、店先の菓子で「朝顔」に出会ったら、花の形より先に「いつの気配を運んでいるか」を見てみてください。朝の涼しさなのか、七夕の名残なのか、旧暦の秋を先取りした含みなのか。ひとつの銘に複数の季節が折りたたまれていると気づくと、見え方がぐっと深くなります。

## 小さな実践

朝、窓辺や道端で青・紫・白のどれか一色を三十秒だけ静かに見てください。花でなくても、空の反射、布、包装紙でかまいません。そのあと目を閉じて、「これは夏の色か、秋の入口の色か」を自分に尋ねてみます。正解を出す必要はありません。ひとつの色に季節を一つだけ決めつけない、その余白を味わう練習です。

## Full English Edition

# A Spoonful of Autumn Hidden in the Name of Morning Glory

With the hush that comes just before a morning flower opens, let us spend today with the word *asagao*, morning glory, and follow the coolness, and even the slight hint of autumn, that it can carry into a tea gathering.

On Fridays, I often want to look less at utensils themselves and more at the way seasonal language changes the air of the room. If, on a mid-July morning, the main sweet were given the name "Morning Glory," what would we actually receive from it? Most people would first imagine blue or purple petals, the mild breeze of an hour before the day becomes hot, and vines climbing from a pot beneath the eaves. Yet one of the quiet pleasures of tea is that it does not stop thinking there. Morning glory is visually a flower of high summer, and yet in the world of seasonal language it can also brush against the threshold of autumn.

For the tea room, that dissonance is not a flaw. It is a source of richness. Tea does not treat the seasons as a simple calendar. It treats them as texture, overlap, and transition. Morning glory opens early and withers before noon. Because of that brevity, it can suggest not so much the force of summer as the fading away of coolness. Its beauty belongs to the hours that do not last. That is why it binds itself so naturally to dawn light, dew, and the whiteness of early morning rather than to the blunt brilliance of midday. When morning glory appears in tea, it is often welcomed not because it is flamboyant, but because its fleetingness suits a morning bowl.

What makes the subject even more interesting is that the word *asagao* itself has never been entirely fixed in older Japanese seasonal culture. A Forestry Agency publication explains that the flower we now call morning glory is treated as an autumn seasonal word under the old lunar calendar. It also notes that the "asagao" named among the Seven Flowers of Autumn is widely thought to refer not to the modern garden morning glory but to kikyo, the bellflower. In other words, the sound of the word has carried different floral images in different eras. Sometimes it points toward the familiar climbing flower of summer; sometimes it preserves the shadow of another wild autumn bloom. Tea is deeply at home in precisely this kind of ambiguity. A single name can hold more than one season, and that layeredness gives the room depth.

If we turn to wagashi, this sensibility becomes even clearer. A sweet named "Morning Glory" does not need to end as botanical illustration. With a pale blue gradation, a translucent layer of kudzu, or even a single stamped motif, the maker can offer not the flower alone but the coolness of morning itself. When Toraya Paris presented its seasonal sweet *Ryofu* in early July 2026 with a morning-glory stamp, describing it as an expression of the fresh wind that meets the flower in the early hours, it showed exactly how this works. The true subject was not botany. It was atmosphere. Tea sweets work in the same way. The confection's name is not a label from a field guide; it is a doorway through which the guest is gently led toward sensation.

That doorway also resonates with the culture of Tanabata and the kaji leaf. In Omotesenke's seasonal writing, Tanabata is traced to *Kikkoden*, the rite of praying for improvement in the arts, and to the custom of writing poetry on kaji leaves. When a July tea gathering includes the suggestion of kaji leaves or strips of verse, a seasonal word such as morning glory stops being merely floral. It begins to carry writing, wishing, cool night air, and the elegance of a single summer observance. Flower, sweet, word, and aspiration are no longer separate things. They are retied within the damp delicacy of one morning atmosphere.

I think one sign of maturity in tea's seasonal awareness lies in this willingness not to fear misalignment. By modern instinct, morning glory is a summer flower. That is not wrong. And yet when the old lunar autumn, the language of early poetry, the interpretation of the Seven Flowers, the aftertaste of Tanabata, the flower's dawn-only opening, its closing by noon, and the chill translucence of a kudzu sweet all gather in one place, morning glory becomes a word that already leans toward autumn while standing firmly in summer. Tea does not treat this as contradiction to be solved. Instead, it invites the guest to taste the first hint of autumn concealed inside the height of summer. That is a refined kind of perception.

So if a sweet called "Morning Glory" were set before you today, I would hope you would not stop at the shape of its petals. If the outer layer is slightly translucent, perhaps you might read there the coolness of morning dew. If there is a blue wash across the surface, perhaps it is not simply the sky, but the color of water or air before the day has fully brightened. Even when the name is "Morning Glory," what we are seeing is not only a flower. We are seeing something that opens only in the morning, closes all too soon, and places an early sign of autumn inside midsummer. A small sweetness can teach us those thin layers of time. Tea does not explain the season. It places the body at the edge where one season begins to become another.

In the tea room, morning glory is not a flower that raises its voice. It is one of the flowers that quietly teaches our senses to wake a little earlier. The slight whiteness before the full heat begins. The kaji leaf not yet dry before a wish is written. The small breath of coolness before the sweet touches the tongue. As a word that can gather all these things, morning glory remains abundantly rich this morning. To look at midsummer and touch the entrance to autumn at the same time, that delicate rereading may be one of the finest gifts Friday tea can offer.
