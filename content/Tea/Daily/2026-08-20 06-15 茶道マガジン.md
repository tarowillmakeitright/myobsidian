# 市中の山居、街のなかに山をひらく

朝の光がまだ柔らかいうちに、街の音の奥へひとつ静かな山道を思い描いてみましょう。

東京は蒸し暑い朝にときおり日が差し、のちに朝のにわか雨も見込まれるようです。そんな移ろいやすい朝ほど、茶の湯がつくる小さな境界がよく見えてきます。

## 日本語特集

「市中の山居（しちゅうのさんきょ）」という言葉には、茶の湯のやさしくも鋭い逆説があります。山の中へ逃げていくのではなく、人の暮らしがもっとも濃い場所の内側に、山のような静けさを立ち上げること。表千家の説明では、これは「身近な生活の中に『山居』と呼ぶ非日常な場所を造り、そこで茶の湯を楽しもうという」発想として語られます。また用語解説では、室町時代のわび茶の流行とともに、市中にありながら山住まいを思わせる茶室がつくられた、と要約されています。つまり、茶室は単なる小さな部屋ではなく、都市の内部に組み込まれた感覚の転地なのです。

この感覚は、豪奢な隔絶ではありません。むしろ、徹底して小さな仕掛けによって生まれます。露地口で身をかがめること、飛石の上だけをゆっくりと歩くこと、母屋から茶室へ進むあいだに視界が少しずつ削られていくこと。表千家の露地口の解説は、狭いところを抜けて別の場所へ入る身体感覚によって、客が「全く違った空間に身を置くという気分」を持つと述べています。ここで変わるのは住所ではなく、注意の向け方です。さっきまで気になっていた用事、音、予定、暑ささえ、急に別の遠さで感じられる。茶の湯はそれを、思想だけでなく足裏の感覚で教えます。

だから「市中の山居」は、自然を模倣する装飾ではありません。木を植え、石を打ち、水を用い、草庵風の小室を設けるのは、山らしさの再現というより、心が日常の速度からほどける順序をつくるためです。裏千家の今日庵の案内でも、茶室が「世俗の喧騒から隔絶された市中の山居へと誘うかのような風情」を持つと紹介されます。ここで大切なのは、完全な無音や完全な孤立ではありません。街の中にあるからこそ、静けさは与えられるものではなく、整えられるものになるのです。

この点で、「市中の山居」は禅の言葉に似て、外界を否定する教えではなく、外界との関係の結び直しに近いものだと思います。ただし、これをどの流儀でも同じ意味で強く言い切るのは慎みたいところです。ある人は露地や茶室の構成に重点を置き、ある人は客の心の切り替えに重きを置きます。けれど少なくとも共通しているのは、茶の湯が静けさを「探しに行く対象」ではなく、「ここで立ち上げる営み」として扱ってきたことではないでしょうか。

今朝もしあなたのまわりに、車の音や通知音や、片づけきっていない机があったとしても、それは茶の湯から遠い条件ではありません。むしろ、その只中に一歩ぶんの山道をひらけるかどうか。露地は遠方の名園の中だけにあるのではなく、日常から一拍だけ深く入るための工夫として、今日の朝にもひそんでいます。

## 今日の見どころ

見るべきものは、豪華な道具ではなく「切り替わりの瞬間」です。露地口をくぐるときのわずかな屈み、飛石の間隔が歩幅をゆるめる感じ、茶室に入ったあと急に耳がひらく感覚。茶席でなくても、戸口や廊下や椅子に腰かける一瞬に、日常の続きが静かな別世界へ縫い替えられる瞬間があります。今日はその境目を、風景ではなく作用として見てみてください。

## 小さな実践

家の中で、ひとつ「境目」にしたい場所を選びます。部屋の入口でも、窓辺でも、椅子に座る前でもかまいません。

1. その場所の手前で一度立ち止まる。
2. 息をひとつだけ長めに吐く。
3. 次の一歩を、いつもより少し静かに出す。
4. 渡ったあと、見えるものではなく、注意の向きがどう変わったかを十秒だけ確かめる。

何も感じなくても十分です。大切なのは成功ではなく、境目を意識してつくることです。茶の湯の露地をまねるというより、いまいる場所に小さな「市中の山居」を設える稽古だと思ってみてください。

## Full English Edition

# Shichu no Sankyo: Opening a Mountain Dwelling Within the City

While the morning light is still soft, try imagining a quiet mountain path hidden behind the sounds of the city.

Tokyo begins this morning warm and humid with occasional sun, with morning showers also expected. On a changeable morning like this, the small thresholds created by tea practice can become especially visible.

The phrase *shichu no sankyo* carries one of the gentlest and sharpest paradoxes in tea. It does not mean fleeing into the mountains. It means raising up a mountain-like quietness within the very place where ordinary life is thickest. Omotesenke describes it as the idea of creating, within everyday life close at hand, an extraordinary place called a “mountain dwelling” and enjoying tea there. In the school’s glossary, the term is also connected with the spread of wabi tea in the Muromachi period, when tearooms were made in the city yet shaped to evoke life in the mountains. In that sense, the tea room is not merely a small room. It is a sensory relocation built into urban life.

That relocation is not produced through luxury or grand isolation. It arises through very small devices. You bow slightly to pass through the roji entrance. You walk only on the stepping stones, and therefore more slowly than usual. As you move from the main house toward the tea room, your field of attention is gently narrowed. Omotesenke’s explanation of the roji entrance says that by passing through a constricted opening, the guest feels placed in an entirely different space. What changes here is not your address but the direction of your attention. The errands that felt urgent a moment ago, the traffic noise, the day’s schedule, even the heat of the morning, begin to register at a different distance. Tea teaches this not only as an idea but through the soles of the feet.

For that reason, *shichu no sankyo* is not decorative nature. Planting trees, placing stones, using water, or building a small grass-hut style room is not simply an attempt to imitate a mountain scene. It is a way of arranging the order in which the mind loosens from the speed of ordinary life. Urasenke, in its introduction to Konnichian, says that the tearoom atmosphere seems to invite one into a secluded mountain dwelling set apart from worldly bustle, even while it exists in the city. What matters here is not perfect silence or total separation. Precisely because it is in the city, quietness becomes not something given in advance, but something carefully composed.

In that sense, *shichu no sankyo* resembles a Zen expression. It is less a rejection of the world than a re-tying of one’s relationship to the world. Even so, it is better not to force one school’s emphasis into a universal rule. Some people stress the physical design of the roji and tea room. Others stress the guest’s inward shift. But at minimum, a common thread remains: in tea, stillness is not treated as something one must travel far away to find. It is something one learns to bring forth here.

So if your morning contains car noise, phone notifications, or a desk that still feels unfinished, those are not conditions too distant from tea. They may be exactly the conditions in which the practice becomes legible. The real question is whether one step’s worth of mountain path can be opened in the middle of them. The roji does not belong only to famous gardens. It also survives as a human technique for entering, by a single beat, more deeply into the place where one already stands.

## Today’s Point of Attention

Notice not a splendid utensil but a moment of transition: the slight bend of the body when passing through an entrance, the way the spacing of stones slows the stride, the way the ears seem to open after entering a quieter room. Even outside a tearoom, there are thresholds at doorways, hallways, windows, and chairs where the continuity of ordinary life can be quietly resewn into another register. Today, try noticing that boundary not as scenery but as an active effect.

## Small Practice

Choose one place in your home to serve as a threshold: a doorway, a window, the moment before sitting down, any small transition point.

1. Pause once just before it.
2. Exhale a little longer than usual.
3. Take the next step a little more quietly than usual.
4. After crossing, spend ten seconds noticing not what you see, but how the direction of your attention has changed.

It is perfectly fine if nothing dramatic happens. The point is not success. The point is to make a threshold consciously. Rather than imitating a formal roji, think of this as practice in setting a small *shichu no sankyo* within the place where you already live.

## Vocabulary

1. 市中の山居（しちゅうのさんきょ） - mountain dwelling within the city
   Usage: A tea-aesthetic ideal of creating quiet seclusion inside ordinary urban life.
2. 露地（ろじ） - tea garden path
   Usage: The approach space leading to the tearoom, preparing the guest inwardly as well as physically.
3. 露地口（ろじぐち） - entrance to the roji
   Usage: A threshold that marks the shift from everyday space into the tea world.
4. 飛石（とびいし） - stepping stones
   Usage: Stones placed to guide pace and movement rather than mere decoration.
5. 草庵（そうあん） - grass-hut hermitage
   Usage: Often used to describe the small, rustic ideal behind many tea rooms.
6. 山居ノ体（さんきょのてい） - style of mountain dwelling
   Usage: A classical expression for tea spaces shaped to evoke secluded living.
7. わび茶（わびちゃ） - wabi tea
   Usage: Tea practice emphasizing restraint, inwardness, and pared-down beauty.
8. 喧騒（けんそう） - bustle, clamor
   Usage: Often used for worldly noise from which the tea space momentarily distances us.
9. 気配（けはい） - presence, trace, atmosphere
   Usage: Useful when describing subtle, half-felt qualities in a tea setting.
10. 境目（さかいめ） - threshold, boundary
    Usage: A practical everyday word for the transition point highlighted in today’s exercise.
11. 設える（しつらえる） - to arrange, to set in place
    Usage: A key tea word for composing space, utensils, or atmosphere with intention.
12. 世俗（せぞく） - worldly life, the secular world
    Usage: Often appears in contrast with the quiet, focused space of tea practice.
