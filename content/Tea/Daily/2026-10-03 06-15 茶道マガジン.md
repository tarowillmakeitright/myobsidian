# 城下町にひらく一服——松江を歩き、暮らしの茶に出会う

仲秋の土曜日。今日は島根県松江へ小さな旅をします。城や大名茶人だけを点で訪ねるのではなく、茶室、庭、菓子、器、そして日々抹茶を飲む習慣を一本の道として歩きます。土地の茶文化は、名品の一覧ではなく、人々が何を繰り返し、次の世代へ手渡したかの中に見えてきます。

## 今日の見どころ

- 松江藩七代藩主・松平治郷（一七五一〜一八一八）は「不昧」と号した茶人で、松江の茶文化を語る重要な存在です。
- しかし地域の茶文化は一人の美意識だけでは成り立ちません。菓子職人、陶工、庭師、茶を点てる人と飲む人の反復が、城下町の日常へ茶を根づかせます。
- 旅先では「有名なものを集める」より、茶室から菓子屋へ、庭から一服へと関係をたどると、その土地ならではの茶が立体的に見えてきます。

## 日本語特集

松江を茶の町として歩くとき、入口になる名は松平治郷、号して不昧です。松江市の解説によれば、不昧は松江藩七代藩主であり、藩政改革を進める一方、茶の湯に深く通じた人物でした。「不昧流」という名は今もその系譜を伝えます。ただし、旅の目的を「偉人の足跡確認」だけにすると、茶が町へ広がった厚みを見落としてしまいます。

一人の茶人が好みを示しても、茶碗を焼く人、菓子を作る人、茶室や庭を守る人、稽古を続ける人がいなければ、地域文化にはなりません。茶の湯は総合芸術と呼ばれますが、「総合」とは多くの美術品が同席することだけではないでしょう。異なる仕事と時間が、一服のために一時的に結ばれることです。

松江観光協会の紹介は、松江が京都・金沢と並ぶ菓子処・茶処として知られ、その背景に不昧公以来の茶の湯文化があることを伝えています。ここで大切なのは、菓子を茶の付属品として小さく扱わないことです。季節を色と形へ移し、甘みで茶を迎える菓子は、土地の素材、職人の技、客の記憶を結ぶもう一つの表現です。

小泉八雲旧居の近くにある田部美術館は、「茶の湯の美術」を柱とし、不昧公ゆかりの道具を含む茶道具を紹介しています。美術館で器を静かに見ることと、茶席で器を手に取ることは同じではありません。しかし、展示は器の輪郭、釉景、取り合わせを急がず見る訓練になります。次に一服をいただくとき、目が以前より長く器に留まるかもしれません。

松江の茶の旅では、庭も背景ではありません。松江観光協会が案内する明々庵は、不昧公の好みによる茶室として伝えられ、松江城を望む高台に移築されています。露地から席へ向かう歩みは、町の速さをそのまま茶室へ持ち込まないための時間になります。石の間隔、足元の湿り、木々の陰に気づくことが、すでに一服への準備です。

ここで「不昧好み」を、固定した意匠のカタログとしてだけ理解しないようにしたいものです。好みは、何を選び、何を組み合わせ、どのような場で人に差し出すかという判断の連続です。形だけを写しても、判断する目まで自動的に受け継がれるわけではありません。伝統とは完成した答えを保存することではなく、先人の選択を手掛かりに、その日の客と季節へ応じ直すことです。

旅人にできるのは、名物を短時間で制覇することではなく、関係を一つ多く見ることです。菓子を選んだら、その銘と季節を尋ねる。茶碗を見たら、土や窯の土地を確かめる。庭を歩いたら、席へ入る前後で音の聞こえ方が変わったかを覚えておく。知識を増やすだけでなく、町が一服をどのように支えているかを観察します。

また、地域文化を一人の英雄だけに還元しない姿勢も必要です。不昧公の存在は大きな軸ですが、二百年以上にわたり茶を日常へ残したのは、名の残らない多くの担い手です。店先で菓子を包む手、器を修繕する手、庭を掃く手、稽古場を開ける手。その手数の積み重ねが、歴史を現在形にします。

松江を実際に訪ねられない朝にも、この旅の方法は使えます。身近な町で、茶、菓子、器、庭のうち二つを結ぶ場所を探してください。地元の菓子を一つ選び、普段の茶碗で抹茶を点て、その菓子がどんな季節や土地を伝えるかを考える。遠くへ移動することだけが文化の旅ではありません。もの同士の関係に気づくことが、旅の始まりです。

一服は小さいけれど、その背後には藩政、流通、工芸、季節感、家々の習慣が折り重なっています。松江の茶文化は、茶室の中に閉じた遺産ではなく、町を歩く視線を育てる道案内です。今日は茶碗の中だけでなく、その一碗を可能にした町の広がりまで味わってみましょう。

## 小さな実践

紙に「茶・菓子・器・庭」と書き、今日触れられる二つを丸で囲みます。二つの間を一行で結んでください。たとえば「この菓子の色が、窓から見える葉の色を深く見せる」。その後、一服をゆっくり飲み、土地や季節との関係をもう一行加えます。正式な点前の再現ではなく、一服を支えるつながりを見つける稽古です。

## Complete English Edition

# A Bowl Opening onto a Castle Town — Walking Matsue and Meeting Tea in Daily Life

On this mid-autumn Saturday, we make a short journey to Matsue in Shimane Prefecture. Rather than visiting a castle and a famous tea master as isolated points, we will follow one path through tea rooms, gardens, confectionery, utensils, and the everyday habit of drinking matcha. A regional tea culture becomes visible not as a list of masterpieces, but through what people repeatedly practice and pass on.

## Today’s Highlights

- Matsudaira Harusato (1751–1818), the seventh lord of the Matsue domain, was the influential tea practitioner known by the name Fumai.
- Yet no regional tea culture is made by one person’s taste alone. Confectioners, potters, gardeners, hosts, guests, and generations of students establish tea in the life of a town.
- When traveling, follow relationships—from tea room to sweet shop, from garden to bowl—instead of merely collecting famous sights. The local character of tea will emerge with greater depth.

## Feature

The natural entry point for walking Matsue as a city of tea is Matsudaira Harusato, known as Fumai. The City of Matsue describes him as the seventh lord of the domain, a ruler who pursued financial reforms and became deeply accomplished in chanoyu. The name Fumai-ryū still carries his lineage. If a journey becomes only a confirmation of a great man’s footsteps, however, it misses the fuller story of how tea spread through the town.

A practitioner may establish preferences, but they do not become a regional culture without people who fire bowls, make sweets, maintain gardens and tea rooms, continue lessons, prepare tea, and drink it. Chanoyu is often called a composite art. “Composite” should mean more than many beautiful objects occupying one room. It means that distinct forms of work and distinct spans of time are provisionally joined for one bowl.

Matsue History Museum explains that the city is known, alongside Kyoto and Kanazawa, for confectionery, and relates this reputation to the tea culture fostered since Fumai’s time. Sweets should not be reduced to accessories for tea. By translating a season into color and form and preparing the mouth through sweetness, confectionery links local ingredients, craft knowledge, and the guest’s memory.

Near the former residence of Lafcadio Hearn, the Tanabe Museum of Art is devoted principally to “the art of the tea ceremony” and presents tea utensils, including objects associated with Fumai. Looking at a vessel in a museum is not the same as holding it in a tea gathering. Yet an exhibition trains the eye to stay with contour, glaze, and combination without haste. At the next bowl, the gaze may remain on the vessel a little longer.

In a Matsue tea journey, the garden is not mere scenery. The Matsue Tourism Association introduces Meimei-an as a tea room reflecting Fumai’s preferences, now relocated to elevated ground overlooking Matsue Castle. The walk through a roji creates time in which the speed of the city need not be carried directly into the tea room. Attention to the spacing of stones, moisture underfoot, and shade beneath trees has already begun the preparation for tea.

We should resist understanding “Fumai taste” only as a fixed catalog of designs. Taste is a continuing sequence of judgments: what to select, how to combine it, and in what setting to offer it to another person. Copying a form does not automatically inherit the eye that judged it. Tradition is less the preservation of a finished answer than the practice of responding again to today’s guest and season with earlier choices as guides.

The traveler’s task is not to conquer every specialty in the shortest time, but to notice one more relationship. When choosing a sweet, ask about its name and season. When seeing a bowl, learn about its clay and the place of its kiln. After walking a garden, remember whether sound felt different before and after entering the tea room. The point is not only to accumulate knowledge, but to observe how a town sustains a bowl.

It is equally important not to reduce a regional culture to a single hero. Fumai is a powerful axis in Matsue’s story, but countless people without recorded names kept tea present in daily life for more than two centuries: hands wrapping sweets at a counter, repairing utensils, sweeping a garden, and opening a practice room. Their repeated work keeps history in the present tense.

This way of traveling remains possible on a morning when Matsue is far away. In your own town, look for a place connecting two of these: tea, sweets, utensils, and gardens. Choose one local confection, prepare matcha in your usual bowl, and ask what season or place the sweet conveys. Cultural travel does not begin only with distance. It begins when relationships among things become visible.

One bowl is small, yet behind it overlap domain history, trade, craft, seasonal perception, and domestic habit. Matsue’s tea culture is not a legacy sealed inside a tea room. It is a guide for learning how to look while walking through a town. Today, taste not only what is inside the bowl, but also the wider place that made the bowl possible.

## A Small Practice

Write “tea, sweet, utensil, garden” on paper and circle two that you can encounter today. Connect them with one sentence—for example, “The color of this sweet makes the leaves outside the window appear deeper.” Then drink one bowl slowly and add a second sentence about its relationship to place or season. This is not a reproduction of formal procedure, but an exercise in finding the connections that sustain tea.

## 今日のことば — Vocabulary (12)

1. **城下町（じょうかまち / jōkamachi）** — a castle town
2. **不昧公（ふまいこう / Fumai-kō）** — the honorific name commonly used for Matsudaira Harusato
3. **藩主（はんしゅ / hanshu）** — lord of a feudal domain
4. **好み（このみ / konomi）** — a tea practitioner’s aesthetic preference or favored design
5. **菓子処（かしどころ / kashidokoro）** — a place celebrated for confectionery
6. **茶室（ちゃしつ / chashitsu）** — a room or small structure for tea gatherings
7. **露地（ろじ / roji）** — the tea garden and approach to a tea room
8. **釉景（ゆうけい / yūkei）** — the visual expression or pattern created by glaze
9. **取り合わせ（とりあわせ / toriawase）** — the selection and combination of utensils or elements
10. **担い手（にないて / ninaite）** — a person who sustains or carries on a tradition
11. **系譜（けいふ / keifu）** — lineage; genealogy; a line of transmission
12. **一碗（いちわん / ichiwan）** — one bowl, especially one bowl of tea
