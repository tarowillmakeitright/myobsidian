# 着せ綿は、秋を急がせずに受け取る

今朝は、菊の香りそのものではなく、香りを待つ一晩の静けさに心を向けてみましょう。雲の多い東京の朝、湿り気を含んだ空気は、露を受けるという感覚を思い出させます。

## 日本語特集

九月に入ると、茶席の秋は、色づいた景色より先に言葉のほうから近づいてくることがあります。その代表のひとつが、着せ綿です。菊の花に綿をそっとかぶせ、夜の露と香りを含ませ、翌朝それで身をぬぐう。行事として説明すればそれだけのことですが、茶の湯の感覚に引き寄せて見てみると、着せ綿はもっと静かな教えを含んでいます。季節は、取りに行くものではなく、一晩かけて受け取るものだという教えです。

重陽は九月九日、陽の数である奇数のうち最も大きい九が重なる日で、五節句のひとつです。国立国会図書館の季節コラムでも、中国で菊を飾り、菊酒を飲んで長寿や無病息災を願った風習が日本へ伝わったと紹介されています。また、同じく国立国会図書館のレファレンス協同データベースでは、平安期の宮中で菊酒や詩歌の宴が行われ、前夜に菊へ綿をかぶせて翌朝その露と香りを移した綿で身を清める説明が確認できます。ここで大切なのは、効験の真偽をいまの感覚で断定することではありません。菊の香り、夜露、朝の身支度をひと続きのものとして感じる季節感が、長く大切にされたということです。

茶の湯は、何かを完成させて見せるより、変化の途中を受け渡す文化です。朝露が降りたかどうか、花がひらききったかどうか、湯の音がまだ若いかどうか。そうした「いま決まり切っていない状態」に耳を澄ますところに、茶の稽古の面白さがあります。着せ綿もまた、まさに途中の美意識です。夜のあいだに綿が何を受け取ったかは、目にははっきり見えません。しかし、見えないものが朝の所作を変える。その構図は、露地の湿りが客の歩幅を少し変えたり、床の一輪がその日の会話の速さを変えたりするのとよく似ています。

この感覚が和菓子に移るとき、菓子は菊そのものを写すだけではなく、「含ませる」という時間まで形にしようとします。虎屋の和菓子暦では、重陽は「菊の節句」とされ、菊にまつわる意匠や季節の菓子が九月上旬に置かれます。ただ、茶席でそれをそのまま菓子銘の知識として並べるだけでは、着せ綿の本当の静けさには届きません。むしろ、白いきんとんや練切のやわらかな表面、あるいは懐紙に置いたときのほのかな水気の気配に、露を宿す想像力が働くとき、菓子は初めて着せ綿の側へ近づきます。茶の湯の季節表現は、説明より先に手触りで伝わることが多いのです。

もちろん、茶席で菊をどう扱うか、重陽の意匠をいつどこまで前倒しするかは、流儀や土地、亭主の考えで揺れます。九月に入ってすぐ菊を強く出すのを好まない人もいますし、重陽を明確に意識した菓子や掛物を早めに置く席もあります。そこを一つの正解にしてしまうのは、かえって不自然です。茶の季節は、暦と気候と席の意図が重なる場所で決まるからです。今日の朝に着せ綿を思うなら、それは「もう菊の節句だ」と言い切るためではなく、これから来る節供を、少し早く、静かに迎えるための稽古として受け取るのがよいでしょう。

着せ綿のよさは、目立たないところにあります。花の盛りを競うのでもなく、香りを派手に語るのでもなく、布という日常的なものに一晩だけ季節を預ける。その控えめさは、茶の湯が大切にしてきた取り合わせの感覚に近いものです。強く言い切らないからこそ、客の側に想像の余地が残る。露がほんとうに宿ったのかを証明しないからこそ、朝の一服に少しだけ身を慎ませる気持ちが生まれる。季節を受け取るとは、こういうことなのかもしれません。

## 今日の見どころ

菊そのものより、菊に触れていない余白を見てみましょう。綿が花を覆うとき、全部を隠すのではなく、わずかに形を残します。その「まだ見えている部分」と「見えなくなった部分」のあいだに、着せ綿の美しさがあります。茶席でも、季節はしばしば全面に出るのではなく、半分だけ姿を見せます。

## 小さな実践

特別な道具は要りません。ティッシュやハンカチ、小さな紙片でもよいので、やわらかい白いものを一枚用意して、窓辺や机の上にそっと置いてみてください。ほんの一分でよいので、その白さが「何かを拭く道具」から「空気を受ける面」へ変わるのを眺めます。

そのあと、次の三つを静かに確かめてみてください。

1. その布や紙は、置く前と置いた後で、気配が変わったように感じるか。
2. 自分は季節を見ようとしているのか、それとも受け取ろうとしているのか。
3. 目に見える変化がなくても、そこに時間が通ったと感じられるか。

菊も露もなくてかまいません。着せ綿の稽古は、物そのものより、待つ姿勢を身につけることにあります。

## English Edition

# Kisewata Receives Autumn Without Hurrying It

This morning, try turning your attention not to the fragrance of chrysanthemum itself, but to the quiet of the night that waits for fragrance to arrive. In Tokyo, the sky is cloudy and the air holds moisture; it naturally brings back the feeling of receiving dew.

Once September begins, autumn in the tearoom sometimes approaches first through language rather than through visibly colored scenery. One of the finest examples is `kisewata`. A piece of cotton is lightly placed over a chrysanthemum bloom, left through the night to take in dew and scent, and then used the next morning to wipe the body. Explained as a seasonal custom, that is simple enough. But if we look at it through the sensibility of tea, `kisewata` offers a quieter lesson. It suggests that the season is not something we seize, but something we receive over the course of a night.

`Choyo`, the Double Ninth festival on September 9, is one of the five seasonal observances. The National Diet Library's seasonal column explains that in China this was an auspicious day marked by displaying chrysanthemums and drinking chrysanthemum wine in hopes of long life and health, and that the custom later came to Japan. The National Diet Library's Reference Cooperative Database likewise summarizes courtly practice in the Heian period: banquets with chrysanthemum wine and poetry, and the custom of covering chrysanthemums the night before so that the cotton would take in dew and fragrance by morning. What matters here is not to force a modern verdict on whether such acts were literally efficacious. What matters is that chrysanthemum scent, night dew, and morning preparation were once felt as part of a single seasonal experience, and that this feeling endured.

Tea is a culture that often values what is still in transition more than what is fully completed and displayed. Has the dew already fallen or not? Has the flower completely opened or not? Is the sound of the kettle still young? Much of tea practice becomes vivid precisely in these unresolved states. `Kisewata` belongs to the same aesthetic. What the cotton receives during the night cannot really be seen. Yet the unseen alters the mood of a morning action. That structure resembles the way dampness in the garden path changes a guest's pace, or the way a single flower in the alcove changes the tempo of conversation.

When this sensibility moves into wagashi, the sweet does not simply imitate a chrysanthemum. It also tries to suggest the time of absorption itself. Toraya's seasonal wagashi calendar identifies `Choyo` as the Chrysanthemum Festival and places related sweets and motifs in early September. Still, if we only line up that information as cultural knowledge, we do not yet reach the quiet center of `kisewata`. A better approach is to notice how a soft white `nerikiri`, a pale `kinton`, or even the faint sense of moisture when a sweet rests on `kaishi` can awaken the imagination of holding dew. In tea, seasonal expression is often conveyed by touch and atmosphere before it is conveyed by explanation.

Of course, no single school, region, or host handles chrysanthemums or Double Ninth motifs in exactly the same way. Some prefer not to bring chrysanthemums forward strongly at the very start of September. Others may introduce a scroll or sweet that clearly anticipates `Choyo` before the date itself. To treat one of these approaches as universally correct would be misleading. In tea, the season emerges where the calendar, the weather, and the host's intention overlap. So if we think of `kisewata` this morning, it need not be to declare, "Now it is the Chrysanthemum Festival." It can simply be a way of quietly preparing to receive a seasonal observance that is approaching.

The beauty of `kisewata` lies in how little it insists on itself. It does not compete in floral abundance. It does not speak loudly about fragrance. It entrusts the season, for a single night, to something ordinary: cloth. That restraint is very close to the combinational intelligence of tea. Because it does not say too much, it leaves room for the guest's imagination. Because it does not prove whether dew has truly settled, it can still make the morning cup feel slightly more careful, slightly more awake. Perhaps this is what it means to receive a season.

## What to Notice Today

Look less at the chrysanthemum itself than at the remaining margin around it. When cotton covers a flower, it does not erase the whole form; some contour still shows through. The beauty of `kisewata` lives between what remains visible and what has just disappeared. In the tearoom too, the season often appears not all at once, but only halfway.

## Small Practice

You do not need special equipment. Take one soft white thing, perhaps a tissue, a handkerchief, or a small piece of paper, and place it gently near a window or on a desk. For one minute, simply watch as its whiteness shifts in your mind from "something used to wipe" into "a surface that receives air."

Then quietly ask yourself three things:

1. Does the cloth or paper feel different after being placed there, even if it looks the same?
2. Am I trying to inspect the season, or receive it?
3. Even without visible change, can I feel that time has passed through it?

You do not need chrysanthemums or dew. The practice of `kisewata` begins less with objects than with learning how to wait.

## Vocabulary

1. 着せ綿（きせわた）- cotton placed over chrysanthemums overnight. Usage: A seasonal custom associated with `重陽`, often evoked in refined autumn contexts.
2. 重陽（ちょうよう）- the Double Ninth festival. Usage: A classical seasonal observance on the ninth day of the ninth month.
3. 菊の節句（きくのせっく）- the Chrysanthemum Festival. Usage: Another name for `重陽`, common in cultural and seasonal writing.
4. 菊酒（きくざけ）- chrysanthemum wine. Usage: Traditionally associated with wishes for long life and well-being.
5. 露（つゆ）- dew. Usage: In tea writing, it often suggests not only moisture but delicacy and transience.
6. 綿（わた）- cotton. Usage: Here it refers to the soft material that receives scent and dew rather than raw textile fiber in general.
7. 懐紙（かいし）- folded paper used in tea. Usage: Often placed under sweets; it can subtly shape how moisture and form are perceived.
8. 練切（ねりきり）- a refined bean-paste confection. Usage: Common in tea settings when a seasonal motif is expressed with great delicacy.
9. きんとん - crumb-textured sweet made from worked bean paste. Usage: Often used to suggest soft surfaces such as blossoms, mist, or seasonal textures.
10. 取り合わせ（とりあわせ）- combination or pairing. Usage: In tea, it refers to the relationship among objects, season, and atmosphere.
11. 趣向（しゅこう）- aesthetic intention or thematic design. Usage: Useful for describing how a host shapes the feeling of a gathering.
12. 余白（よはく）- remaining space, reserve, margin. Usage: A key word when noticing how tea often communicates through what is only partly shown.
