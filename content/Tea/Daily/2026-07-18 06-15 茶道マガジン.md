# 城下町をひと碗ぶん歩く、松江・明々庵の朝

城の気配がまだやわらかく残る朝、今朝は松江の明々庵をたどりながら、土地に根づいた茶の湯が「歩くこと」そのものを一服に変えていく感覚を味わってみましょう。

## 日本語特集

土曜日の茶道マガジンでは、ひとつの土地を歩くように読む回にしたくなります。今朝の舞台は島根県松江市、北の城下町に今も濃く残る茶の湯文化です。全国に名だたる茶の地はいくつもありますが、松江の面白さは、名物や名器だけで完結しないところにあります。町の歩幅そのものに、茶の湯の速度がしみ込んでいる。その感覚をいちばん素直に受け取れる場所のひとつが、明々庵です。

明々庵は、松江藩七代藩主・松平治郷、いわゆる不昧公の好みによって生まれた茶室として伝えられています。公式案内によれば、もとは松江城下の殿町、有澤家の屋敷内に建てられ、不昧公もしばしば訪れたとされます。その後いったん東京へ移され、再び出雲へ戻り、現在の赤山の地へ移された経緯をもっています。建物だけ見れば移築の歴史をもつ茶室ですが、だからこそ逆に、土地にとどまる文化とは何かを考えさせます。茶室は動いたのに、茶の気風は動かなかった。あるいは、動いてもなお土地へ帰ってきたと言うべきかもしれません。

不昧公の名は、しばしば大名茶人としての華やかさと結びつけられます。けれど松江でその名に触れるとき、印象はもう少しやわらかいものになります。JNTOの地域文化紹介でも、松江の茶文化は明々庵だけでなく、城下を歩きながら抹茶や和菓子を気軽に楽しめる日常性とともに語られています。つまりここで育った茶の湯は、厳粛な儀礼としてだけ保存されたのではありません。町の人がふつうに菓子を選び、茶を喫し、季節を口にする習慣のなかで厚みを増してきたのです。

その意味で、明々庵の魅力は「中に入って終わり」ではありません。むしろ、そこへ向かうまでの時間がすでに茶事の前半のように感じられます。塩見縄手のあたりを歩き、武家屋敷の静かな線を見て、城の近さを身体で知り、少し高みにある茶室へ気持ちを運んでいく。茶の湯では露地が室内への準備になるように、松江では町そのものが大きな露地の役目を果たしているようです。旅先で茶室へ向かうとき、私たちはつい「目的地」に意識を集中させますが、松江ではそこへ至る道筋のほうが、むしろ文化の本体かもしれないと感じます。

明々庵の公式説明には、厚い藁葺きの入母屋屋根、不昧公自筆の「明々庵」の額、そして二畳台目・向切の席構えなどが記されています。ここで大切なのは、これらを様式の暗記として受け取らないことです。たとえば藁葺きの厚みは、ただ古風というだけでなく、外の明るさをやわらげ、時間を少し遅く感じさせる屋根の量感でもあります。額の文字は、観光名所としての看板である前に、主人の気配が入口にまだ残っているような感覚を生みます。二畳台目という小ささも、窮屈さより先に、茶と人との距離を濃くするための濃度として読みたいのです。

一方で、不昧公の好みをそのまま「松江の茶の湯はこうだ」と一般化しすぎるのは慎みたいところです。不昧流を中心に語られる地域的な個性は確かにありますが、現代の松江で茶に出会う場は、観光用の一服、地元菓子舗の呈茶、学校茶道、各流派の稽古など多層的です。ひとつの流儀だけで町全体を説明してしまうと、かえってこの土地の懐の深さを見落とします。地域文化の魅力は、単一の正解より、いくつもの濃淡が同じ町のなかで呼吸していることにあります。

私が松江の茶文化にひかれるのは、その「少し開かれている」感じです。京都の名席に向かうときのような緊張とは別の、町へ身を預ける落ち着きがある。明々庵の紹介でも「気軽な茶室」という言葉が使われていますが、ここでいう気軽さは軽薄さではありません。格式を消してしまうのでなく、格式に近づくための歩幅をやさしく整えてくれる、そんな開き方です。茶道に慣れていない人でも、城下町の坂や木立や菓子の甘さから入っていける。入口が建物一つではなく、町じゅうに散っているのです。

地域の茶の湯を語るとき、私たちはしばしば「何が名物か」を尋ねます。けれど松江については、「どんな速度で茶に近づける町か」と問うほうがふさわしいように思います。朝の城下を歩き、視線が少し低くなり、足音が整い、甘味の記憶が生まれ、やがて一碗の抹茶を受け取る。その連なり全体が一服です。茶室だけが茶の湯なのではなく、そこへ至る町の傾斜や湿り気や沈黙の量もまた、立派な道具立てになる。松江はそのことを、とても自然に教えてくれる土地です。

もし旅の茶道にひとつ学びがあるとしたら、名所を「消費」しないで、そこへ着くまでに感覚がどう変わるかを自分で観察することかもしれません。明々庵は、見学して終わる建物というより、客の側の速度を調える装置として読むと急に深くなります。どこで声が小さくなったか。どこで目が近くを見るようになったか。どこで、急いで理解しなくてもよいと思えたか。そうした変化を引き受けるとき、地域の茶文化は観光情報から離れ、身体の知恵として残りはじめます。

松江の朝にふさわしい一服は、きっと派手ではありません。城の見える町を少し歩き、木陰の湿り気を吸い込み、ひとつの茶室へ入るまでに心の輪郭がやわらぐ。その過程に、不昧公以来つづく町の茶の湯が静かに宿っています。茶の湯は席中だけにあるのではない。土地の坂道にも、門前の気配にも、菓子を選ぶ迷いの時間にもある。そう思える土地に出会うと、一碗は急に地域の風景を帯びはじめます。今朝の明々庵は、まさにそのことを教えてくれる場所です。

## 今日の見どころ

茶室そのものだけでなく、そこへ向かう「手前の時間」に目を向けてみてください。坂を上がる感じ、木陰の湿り、門をくぐる前の一拍、視線が遠景から足もとへ移る瞬間。地域の茶の湯は、建物の内部より先に、近づき方のなかに表れることがあります。

## 小さな実践

今朝は家の中で、飲みものを取りに行くまでの十歩ほどを小さな露地に見立ててみてください。歩きながら、足音、床の温度、光の向きだけを静かに確かめます。席についたら、最初のひと口を飲む前に「ここへ来るまでに何が変わったか」を一つだけ言葉にしてみましょう。特別な道具はいりません。移動そのものを一服の前半として味わう練習です。

## Full English Edition

# Walking a Castle Town One Bowl at a Time: Morning at Meimei-an in Matsue

On a morning when the presence of the castle still feels soft in the air, let us spend time with Meimei-an in Matsue and notice how a regional tea culture can turn the very act of walking into the first half of a bowl of tea.

For Saturday’s tea magazine, I like to read a place the way one might walk through it. Today’s setting is Matsue in Shimane Prefecture, a castle town where tea culture still rests close to the surface of daily life. There are many famous tea regions in Japan, but Matsue feels distinctive because it cannot be explained only through celebrated utensils, famous sweets, or a single lineage. The pace of tea seems to have entered the stride of the town itself. One of the clearest places to sense that is Meimei-an.

According to its official introduction, Meimei-an was created in keeping with the taste of Matsudaira Harusato, the seventh lord of the Matsue domain, better known as Lord Fumai. It was originally built on the grounds of the Arisawa family residence in Tonomachi in the castle district, and Lord Fumai is said to have visited it often. The building was later moved to Tokyo, eventually returned to Izumo, and then relocated to its present site at Akayama. Seen only as architecture, it is a teahouse with a history of relocation. But precisely for that reason it asks a deeper question: what does it mean for a culture to remain rooted in place? The structure moved, yet the tea spirit of the town did not simply dissolve. If anything, it seems to have come home again.

Lord Fumai’s name is often linked with the brilliance of the tea-loving daimyo. In Matsue, though, the impression softens. JNTO’s feature on the city’s tea culture describes not only Meimei-an itself but also the everyday ease with which one can enjoy matcha and wagashi while moving through town. That matters. Tea here was not preserved only as solemn formality. It thickened through ordinary habits: people choosing sweets, drinking tea, noticing seasons, and carrying those gestures into common life. Regional culture became durable not by staying rare, but by remaining gently usable.

That is why the charm of Meimei-an does not begin only after one enters the room. The time spent approaching it can feel like the first half of a tea gathering. One walks around Shiomi Nawate, sees the restrained lines of former samurai residences, feels the nearness of Matsue Castle, and gradually lets the mind rise toward a teahouse set a little higher on the land. In formal tea, the roji prepares the body and mind before the guest enters the room. In Matsue, the town itself can feel like an expanded roji. When traveling, we often rush our attention toward the destination. In Matsue, it may be the path toward the tea space that reveals the culture most clearly.

The official description of Meimei-an notes its thick thatched irimoya roof, the plaque bearing the name "Meimei-an" written by Lord Fumai himself, and the compact layout of a two-mat-plus-daime room with a mukogiri hearth arrangement. Yet these features become richer if we resist treating them as items for memorization. The depth of the thatch is not only old-fashioned; it also softens outer brightness and makes time feel slightly slower. The plaque is more than signage for visitors; it leaves the host’s presence at the threshold. The smallness of the room is not meaningful because it is cramped, but because it intensifies the relation between tea and person.

At the same time, it would be too simple to generalize from Lord Fumai’s taste and say, "this is what tea in Matsue is." Regional identity certainly includes the legacy of Fumai and traditions associated with him, but contemporary encounters with tea in Matsue are layered: casual bowls served to travelers, presentations at local confectioners, school tea practice, and study within different schools. If we try to explain the whole town through one style alone, we lose the generosity of the place. The beauty of regional tea culture often lies not in a single correct form, but in the way multiple shades of practice breathe within one shared landscape.

What draws me to Matsue is this slightly open feeling. It has a different tension from approaching a famous seat in Kyoto. Here there is more of a quiet willingness to let the town receive you. Meimei-an’s own official text describes it as a "casual tea house," and that phrase is worth lingering over. Casual does not mean thin or careless. It means that the approach to formality is made gentler. Even someone unfamiliar with tea can begin with the slope of the castle town, the dampness of the trees, the sweetness of a local confection, and only then arrive at the tearoom. The entrance is not confined to one doorway. It is distributed through the town.

When we speak about regional tea, we often ask what the local specialty is. With Matsue, it may be better to ask what kind of pace allows one to come close to tea there. You walk through the morning town, your gaze lowers, your footsteps settle, a memory of sweetness arises, and at last you receive a bowl of matcha. The whole sequence is one serving. Tea is not only what happens inside the room. The incline of the street, the moisture in the shade, and the quantity of silence also become part of the equipment of hospitality. Matsue teaches that with unusual naturalness.

If there is one lesson to take from tea while traveling, perhaps it is this: do not merely consume a famous site. Observe how your senses change before you reach it. Meimei-an becomes far deeper when read not only as a building to be seen, but as a device that retunes the guest’s speed. At what point did your voice become softer? When did your eyes begin to look nearer rather than farther? When did you realize there was no need to understand everything quickly? Once you accept such changes, regional tea culture leaves the level of tourist information and begins to remain as bodily knowledge.

The bowl suited to a Matsue morning is probably not a dramatic one. You walk a little through a town where the castle is still near, draw in the dampness of shade, and let the edges of the mind become less hard before entering a single teahouse. In that process lives the tea culture that has continued in this town since Lord Fumai. Tea is not found only inside the formal seat. It also lives in slopes, gate-front atmospheres, and the small hesitation before choosing a sweet. When you meet a place that makes this visible, one bowl begins to carry the whole landscape. This morning, Meimei-an is such a place.

## Vocabulary

1. **城下町**（じょうかまち） - castle town. Usage: Refers not just to old streets, but to a whole urban rhythm shaped by a former castle center.
2. **明々庵**（めいめいあん） - Meimei-an teahouse. Usage: A proper name; useful as an example of a regional tea site in Matsue.
3. **不昧公**（ふまいこう） - Lord Fumai, Matsudaira Harusato’s tea name. Usage: Often used when discussing Matsue tea culture and its historical legacy.
4. **露地**（ろじ） - tea garden path. Usage: Can be extended metaphorically to mean any approach that prepares the senses.
5. **城見**（しろみ） - view toward a castle. Usage: Not a fixed tea term, but helpful for describing Matsue’s landscape feeling.
6. **入母屋屋根**（いりもややね） - irimoya roof, a hip-and-gable roof form. Usage: Common in architectural description of historic Japanese buildings.
7. **額**（がく） - plaque or framed inscription. Usage: Often refers to a name board or calligraphic panel at an entrance.
8. **二畳台目**（にじょうだいめ） - a tea room layout of two tatami plus a daime mat area. Usage: Signals intimacy and compact design in tea architecture.
9. **向切**（むこうぎり） - a hearth arrangement cut away from the host’s side. Usage: A technical tea-room term; traditions differ in how strongly they emphasize it.
10. **気軽**（きがる） - casual, approachable, without stiffness. Usage: In tea writing, it can mean accessible without implying shallowness.
11. **調える**（ととのえる） - to arrange, tune, or bring into order. Usage: Works for both spaces and one’s internal state before tea.
12. **道すがら**（みちすがら） - along the way. Usage: Useful for describing how meaning accumulates while approaching a place.
