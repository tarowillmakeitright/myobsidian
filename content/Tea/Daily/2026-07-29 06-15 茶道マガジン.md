# 四方をひらいて、心の向きを整える

朝の一服の前に、帛紗の小さな布がなぜあれほど大きな静けさを生むのか、その四方捌きの意味をゆっくり見つめてみましょう。

今朝の東京は雲の多い空で、湿り気を含んだやわらかな明るさがあり、布をひらく手つきにも少し余白を残したくなる朝です。

## 日本語特集

茶道の所作を外から見る人にとって、帛紗さばきはしばしば「道具を拭く動作」に見えます。たしかに、点前のなかで帛紗は茶杓や茶器を清めるために用いられます。裏千家の入門向け解説でも、その基本的な役割は明快にそう説明されています。けれども、実際に稽古を重ねる人ほど、あの布の仕事が単なる清掃や消毒の代わりではないことを身体で知っていきます。四角い布を取り出し、折り、ひらき、向きを整えるあいだに、まだ触れていないはずの空気まで少し整ってしまうからです。

今朝は、その感覚を裏千家で語られる「四方捌き」の説明から考えてみたいと思います。裏千家のお家元による一問一答には、帛紗の四方を捌くのは、陰陽の考えでいう東西南北を清め、さらに春夏秋冬、一年の巡りを清めると思えばよい、という趣旨の答えがあります。ここで大切なのは、この説明をそのまま「茶道全体の唯一の正解」として広げすぎないことです。これは、少なくとも裏千家において現在公開されている、きわめて象徴的で美しい読み方の一つです。ほかの流儀や先生が、同じ所作をより実践的に、あるいは別の言葉で教えることも十分ありえます。

それでも、この説明がなぜ長く人の心をとらえるのかはよくわかります。四方捌きは、手先の器用さだけでは成立しません。布の角をどこへ運ぶかを知っていても、気持ちが急いていれば、動きはすぐに「折りたたみ作業」へ変わってしまいます。反対に、ゆっくりと四方をひらくと、布は小さいのに、場の広がりがふっと意識に入ってきます。目の前の茶器だけでなく、席の左右、前後、そして季節の移ろいまで引き受けてから、一つの道具に触れる。そう考えると、清めるとは「汚れを落とす」ことよりも、「どう向き合うかの姿勢を整える」ことに近くなります。

表千家が「形とふるまい」の解説で述べるように、茶の湯の型は、もともと主客のあいだの厚い心持ちから生まれ、その型を正しく行うことによって心を表すものです。この見方を借りれば、帛紗さばきも、布の扱い方そのものが目的なのではなく、その型を通して自分の注意の向き先を表している、と読めます。つまり、四方捌きは「布の操作」ではなく、「注意の配り方を見える形にしたもの」なのです。

ここには、朝の茶にふさわしい静かな知恵があります。朝は、まだ心が一日の外側へ散りやすい時間です。予定、通知、昨夜の続きを考えながらでは、目の前の一碗に入る前から気持ちがばらけてしまう。そんなとき、帛紗の四方を順に扱う動きは、散った意識を前後左右から集め直すように働きます。広い意味での「清め」は、対象のためだけでなく、自分のためにもあるのです。

面白いのは、帛紗が布としてはとても小さいことです。茶室の柱でもなく、掛物でもなく、花でもありません。けれど、その小ささのなかに、茶の湯はずいぶん大きな宇宙を預けています。東西南北、春夏秋冬という説明が示しているのは、手の中の布が世界の縮図になる、という感覚でしょう。もちろん、現代の私たちが毎回そこまで明瞭に方位や季節を意識しながら動けるとは限りません。それでも、せめて「自分の前の一点だけで完結しない所作なのだ」と知っているだけで、帛紗さばきの景色は変わります。急いで畳む布ではなく、場をほどき、場を結び直す布に見えてくるのです。

また、裏千家の別の公開情報を見ると、帛紗は茶杓や茶器を清めるだけでなく、亭主側のしるしとして腰につけられ、客も携える道具だと説明されています。つまり帛紗は、点前の最中だけ働く布ではありません。主と客、実用と象徴、動作と気配のあいだを行き来する、小さな媒介です。茶の湯では、こうした「小さいが境目にあるもの」がしばしば大切にされます。蓋置、茶巾、扇子。みな大きな主役ではないのに、場の秩序をそっと支えています。帛紗もその一つです。

だから今朝、四方捌きを眺めるときには、上手下手だけで見ないでみてください。角がぴたりと合うか、折り目が美しいかはたしかに大切です。でもそれ以上に、その手つきが「いまからこの場に向き合います」という宣言になっているかどうかが、茶の湯らしさの核心に近いように思います。清めとは、ものを無菌にすることではありません。向き合う自分の姿勢に、濁りを残さないことです。

四方をひらく所作は、世界を支配するための大げさな象徴ではなく、自分の散りやすい心を、もう一度四隅から部屋の中央へ戻してくるための、小さな方法なのかもしれません。そう思うと、帛紗の一連の動きは古風な規則ではなく、朝の注意力を立ち上げる非常に実践的な技術に見えてきます。茶の湯の所作が美しいのは、形が整っているからだけではありません。形が、心の向き直りを助けているからです。

## 今日の見どころ

帛紗さばきを見る機会があれば、何を「拭いたか」よりも、どこまで場の空気が変わったかを感じてみてください。布は小さいのに、手つきが落ち着くと、茶器だけでなく座る人の呼吸まで少し整って見えることがあります。

## 小さな実践

ハンカチや薄い布、あるいは何も持たなくてもかまいません。両手の前に見えない四角を思い描き、四隅を順に指先でたどるように、ゆっくり視線を左、右、前、手元へ戻してみてください。所要時間は二十秒ほどで十分です。肩や手に負担があるなら、目だけで四方を見るだけでも大丈夫です。目的は作法の再現ではなく、散った注意をやさしく一か所へ集める感覚を知ることです。

## Full English Edition

# Opening the Four Directions, Settling the Mind

Before the first bowl of the morning, let us linger with a small square of cloth and ask why it can create such a large stillness. Today’s focus is the meaning of *shihō-sabaki*, the “opening of the four directions” in the handling of the *fukusa*.

This morning in Tokyo the sky is mostly cloudy, with a soft brightness held inside the humidity. It feels like the kind of morning that asks even the hands opening a cloth to leave a little space around their movement.

To someone watching tea from the outside, *fukusa-sabaki* often looks like a practical act of wiping. That is not wrong. Introductory explanations from Urasenke say plainly that the *fukusa* is used during the tea procedure to purify the tea scoop and tea container. But anyone who practices long enough begins to feel that the cloth is not simply replacing cleaning or sterilizing. A square of silk is taken out, folded, opened, and oriented, and somehow even the air around the utensils seems to settle before the utensils are touched. That is why the gesture deserves a slower look.

This morning I want to think through that feeling by way of an explanation publicly given in Urasenke. In one of the school’s question-and-answer pages, the Grand Master explains that handling the four sides of the *fukusa* may be understood as purifying the four directions, east, west, south, and north, and also the four seasons, the cycle of the year. What matters here is not to stretch this into a universal claim about all tea practice. It is, at minimum, one beautiful and highly symbolic interpretation that Urasenke itself offers publicly. Other schools, and even individual teachers within a school, may explain the same movement in more practical language or through a different symbolic frame. Honesty about that difference is part of cultural respect.

Even so, it is easy to understand why this explanation stays in the mind. *Shihō-sabaki* cannot be carried by dexterity alone. You may know exactly where the corners are supposed to go, yet if the mind is hurried, the entire action collapses into nothing more than a folding task. If, however, the four directions are opened slowly, the cloth remains small while the sensed space around it becomes larger. One seems to acknowledge not only the tea container in front of oneself, but also left and right, before and behind, and even the passage of the seasons, before touching a single utensil. In that sense, purification begins to mean something broader than removing dirt. It becomes a way of arranging the posture of attention.

This is where an Omotesenke explanation helps, even without speaking specifically about the *fukusa*. Omotesenke says that the forms of tea arose from the deep feeling exchanged between host and guest, and that by carrying out those forms correctly, those feelings are made visible. If we borrow that perspective, the handling of the *fukusa* is not an end in itself. It is a form through which one reveals where one’s attention is directed. The movement is not merely the manipulation of a cloth. It is attention given shape.

There is a quiet wisdom here that especially suits the morning. Early hours are when the mind easily scatters outward. Plans, notifications, unfinished thoughts from the night before: all of them can remain active before a single bowl is made. In such a state, the sequential movement through the “four directions” can gather awareness back in from the edges. In this wider sense, purification is not done only for the utensil. It is also done for the self who is about to meet the utensil.

What is striking is how small the *fukusa* actually is. It is not a pillar, not a hanging scroll, not a flower arrangement. And yet tea seems to entrust a surprisingly large cosmos to this little square. East, west, south, north. Spring, summer, autumn, winter. The symbolism suggests that a cloth held in the hands can briefly become a compressed image of the world. Of course, modern practitioners will not always move with those directions and seasons vividly present in consciousness. Still, it changes the scene simply to know that the gesture does not end at one point directly in front of the body. The *fukusa* stops looking like a cloth hastily folded and starts looking like something that loosens the space and ties it back together again.

Another Urasenke introductory page notes that the *fukusa* is not only used to purify utensils but also serves as a sign of the host’s side when tucked at the waist, while guests also carry one. In other words, the *fukusa* is not a cloth that works only in the middle of the procedure. It moves between host and guest, utility and symbol, visible action and quiet atmosphere. Tea often gives great importance to such things: small objects that sit at the boundaries. The lid rest, the tea cloth, the fan. None is the dramatic center of the room, yet each helps support the room’s order. The *fukusa* belongs to that family.

So if you watch *shihō-sabaki* today, try not to judge it only by technical neatness. Yes, it matters whether the corners align and the folds are clear. But more important is whether the movement feels like a declaration: “I am now turning toward this place.” That seems close to the heart of tea. Purification is not the making of an object germ-free. It is the refusal to leave cloudiness in the attitude with which one approaches it.

The opening of the four directions may not be a grand symbol of mastering the world. It may be something far more intimate and useful: a small way of bringing a scattered mind back from the four corners into the center of the room. Seen like that, the movement of the *fukusa* stops being an old-fashioned rule and becomes a practical morning technique for awakening attention. Tea gestures are beautiful not only because the form is orderly, but because the form helps the heart turn back toward what it is doing.

## What to Notice Today

If you have a chance to watch *fukusa-sabaki*, notice not only what was “wiped,” but how much the atmosphere changed. The cloth is small, yet when the hands settle, even the breathing of the seated person can seem to settle with it.

## Small Practice

You may use a handkerchief, a thin cloth, or nothing at all. Imagine a small square in front of both hands, and slowly trace its four corners with your fingertips or just with your gaze: left, right, forward, and back to the place nearest you. Twenty seconds is enough. If your shoulders or hands are uncomfortable, let the eyes do all the work. The goal is not to recreate tea procedure exactly. It is to notice what it feels like when scattered attention is gently gathered back into one place.

## Vocabulary

1. 帛紗（ふくさ）- silk cloth used in tea. Usage: A formal cloth used in temae, especially for purifying utensils and shaping the rhythm of movement.
2. 帛紗さばき（ふくささばき）- handling of the fukusa. Usage: Refers to the sequence of folding and opening the cloth during tea procedure.
3. 四方捌き（しほうさばき）- opening the four directions. Usage: In Urasenke, this can be explained symbolically as purifying the four directions and seasons.
4. 清める（きよめる）- to purify. Usage: In tea this often means ritually preparing or honoring an object, not merely cleaning it.
5. 茶杓（ちゃしゃく）- tea scoop. Usage: One of the utensils commonly purified with the fukusa.
6. 茶器（ちゃき）- tea container. Usage: A general term for the container holding matcha.
7. 点前（てまえ）- tea procedure. Usage: The performed sequence through which tea is prepared before guests.
8. 主客（しゅかく）- host and guest. Usage: A key tea term that highlights the relational heart of the gathering.
9. 型（かた）- form; pattern. Usage: A repeated, formalized movement that carries feeling as well as technique.
10. 気配（けはい）- subtle atmosphere; presence. Usage: Useful for describing what changes in a room before anyone speaks.
11. 向き合う（むきあう）- to face; to turn toward. Usage: In tea writing, often used for the inward attitude with which one approaches an object or person.
12. 余白（よはく）- open space; margin. Usage: Can describe visual emptiness or a felt spaciousness in movement and attention.
