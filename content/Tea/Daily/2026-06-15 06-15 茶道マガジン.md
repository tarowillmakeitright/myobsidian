## 日本語版｜今朝の特集：掛物――茶室で最初に出会う「無言の先生」

茶室に入ると、まだ釜の音も遠く、茶碗も見えていないのに、すでに稽古は始まっています。最初に客を迎えるのは、亭主の言葉ではなく、床の間の掛物です。茶の湯では、掛物はただの飾りではありません。その日の場の心を、最も静かに、そして最もはっきり示すものです。だからこそ「茶席の第一声は掛物にある」と言ってよいでしょう。

茶会で床を見るとき、私たちはつい「何と書いてあるか」を急いで読みたくなります。けれども、掛物は意味だけで味わうものではありません。まず大切なのは、空間の空気がどう変わるかを感じることです。太く勢いのある墨の線なのか、やわらかく余白を含んだ筆なのか。一字が強く立っているのか、全体が霧のようにつながっているのか。文字を読む前に、筆の呼吸がこちらに届いてきます。

たとえば「和敬清寂」のようなよく知られた言葉でも、いつ、どの筆者の、どの裂地とともに掛けられるかで、受け取り方は変わります。冬の朝に力ある筆で見る「清」は、身を引き締めるように響くかもしれません。梅雨の湿り気の中で見るやわらかな一行は、清らかさを「乾いたもの」ではなく、濁りを抱えながら澄んでいく働きとして感じさせるでしょう。茶の湯の面白さは、同じ語句がいつも同じ意味に固定されないところにあります。

掛物は、亭主の説明書でもありません。むしろ、客に対する静かな問いかけです。今日はどんな心でこの席に入りますか。あなたはこの言葉を、頭で理解するだけでなく、今日の光や風や自分の気分とともに受け取れますか。良い掛物は、正解を押しつけません。その代わり、客の感受性を少しだけ深くします。

ここに、茶の湯らしい厳しさがあります。華やかな道具組は、見る人の目をすぐ喜ばせます。しかし掛物は、派手に自己主張しません。見過ごそうと思えば見過ごせてしまう。だからこそ、床を丁寧に拝見する人と、ただ座っただけの人の差がよく出ます。茶室では、最も静かなものが、しばしば最も多くを語ります。

また、掛物は一会の時間感覚を整えます。花はその日の生気を示し、香は見えない気配を満たし、菓子はこれから来る味を予告します。それに対して掛物は、席全体の「方角」を決める役目です。今日の一会は、涼を求めるのか、節目を祝うのか、別れを惜しむのか、あるいはただ雑念を払って一碗に集中するのか。床の一幅が、その方向を言葉少なく定めます。

初心者にとって掛物は難しく感じられます。禅語が読めない、筆者がわからない、古筆の良し悪しも判断できない。けれども、最初から専門知識だけで向き合う必要はありません。まずは「この掛物の前では、自然に背筋が伸びるか」「やわらかく息が深くなるか」を感じれば十分です。茶の湯の理解は、情報の量より、注意の質から始まります。

床の間での一礼には、実は小さな決意が含まれています。私はこの場を受け取ります、という決意です。掛物は、その決意を促す存在です。言葉を書いた紙ではなく、場の中心を静かに作る軸。だから名物の茶碗が出なくても、床がよければ席は深くなります。逆に道具が豪華でも、掛物が場と離れていれば、心は散りやすい。

朝の一服を思い浮かべてみてください。もし家でお茶を飲むだけでも、小さな紙片に好きな一句を書いて置くと、不思議と所作が変わります。急いで飲んでいたお茶が、「今ここで飲む一碗」になります。掛物の力とは、ものを増やすことではなく、注意を集めることなのです。

茶室で最初に見るものが掛物であるのは偶然ではありません。客はまず目で教えられ、しかも説明されすぎない。その余白の中で、自分の心の速度が少しずつ整っていく。掛物は、無言の先生です。茶の湯は、その先生に一礼するところから始まります。

## English Version | Today’s Feature: The Hanging Scroll — The Silent Teacher You Meet First in the Tearoom

When you enter a tearoom, the lesson has already begun before you hear the kettle or see the bowl. The first thing that receives the guest is not the host’s voice, but the hanging scroll in the tokonoma alcove. In sadō, the scroll is not mere decoration. It is the quietest and clearest expression of the heart of the gathering. You could almost say that the first greeting of a tea gathering comes from the scroll.

When people look at the alcove, they often rush to ask, “What does it say?” But a scroll is not appreciated by meaning alone. The first task is to feel how the atmosphere of the room changes because of it. Are the ink lines bold and forceful, or soft and spacious? Does a single character stand upright with intensity, or does the whole line drift like mist? Before you “read” the words, the breath of the brush has already reached you.

Even a familiar phrase such as *Wa-kei-sei-jaku*—harmony, respect, purity, tranquility—does not feel the same in every setting. It changes according to season, calligrapher, mounting, light, and the emotional weather of the gathering. In a winter morning seat, the character for purity may strike you with a bracing sharpness. In the damp softness of the rainy season, a gentler hand may make purity feel less like dryness and more like clarity that emerges while still holding moisture and shadow. One of the delights of tea is that words never remain fixed in a single interpretation.

A scroll is also not an instruction manual from the host. It functions more like a quiet question offered to the guest: With what state of mind are you entering today? Can you receive this phrase not only with the intellect, but together with the day’s light, breeze, fatigue, gratitude, or restlessness? A good scroll does not force a correct answer. Instead, it deepens the guest’s sensitivity by a small but real degree.

There is a tea-like severity in this. Splendid utensils can please the eye at once. A fine lacquer container or an unusual bowl may attract instant admiration. The scroll does not work that way. It rarely announces itself loudly. If you are careless, you can overlook it. That is precisely why it reveals the difference between someone who truly attends to the room and someone who has merely sat down inside it. In the tearoom, the quietest thing often speaks the most.

The hanging scroll also establishes the temporal direction of the gathering. Flowers show the living freshness of the day. Incense fills the room with an unseen presence. Sweets anticipate the taste still to come. The scroll, by contrast, gives the entire meeting its orientation. Is this a gathering that seeks coolness? Marks a transition? Honors a farewell? Celebrates renewal? Or simply strips away distraction so attention can rest in one bowl of tea? A single scroll determines that direction with very few words.

Beginners often feel intimidated by scrolls. They may not read Zen phrases, may not know the calligrapher, and may have no confidence in judging old brushwork. But tea does not require scholarship first. A more useful beginning is to notice your bodily response. Does your spine lengthen naturally in front of this scroll? Does your breath deepen? Does the room feel tightened, brightened, softened, or clarified? Understanding in tea begins less with the quantity of information than with the quality of attention.

Even the bow before the alcove contains a small resolution: I accept this space. The scroll encourages that resolution. It is not simply paper with words, but an axis that quietly forms the center of the room. That is why a gathering can become profound even without famous utensils, if the alcove is right. And conversely, even expensive objects may fail to gather the heart if the scroll feels disconnected from the occasion.

Think about your own morning tea. Even at home, if you place a slip of paper nearby with a line you love, your manner may subtly change. Tea that might have been swallowed in a hurry becomes a bowl received here and now. That is the power of the scroll. It does not add more things; it gathers attention.

It is no accident that the scroll is the first thing the guest encounters. The guest is taught first through the eyes, and not over-explained. In that margin of quiet, the speed of the heart begins to settle. The hanging scroll is a silent teacher, and sadō begins by bowing to it.

## Study Section｜Vocabulary (12)

1. 掛物（かけもの）— hanging scroll
2. 床の間（とこのま）— alcove in a traditional room
3. 一会（いちえ）— a once-in-a-lifetime gathering/encounter
4. 亭主（ていしゅ）— host of the tea gathering
5. 客（きゃく）— guest
6. 余白（よはく）— blank space; meaningful margin
7. しつらえ — arrangement; setting of a space
8. 筆致（ひっち）— brushwork; style of the brush
9. 感受性（かんじゅせい）— sensitivity; receptivity
10. 背筋が伸びる（せすじがのびる）— to straighten instinctively; feel one’s posture lift
11. 雑念（ざつねん）— distracting thoughts; mental clutter
12. 受け取る（うけとる）— to receive; to take in
