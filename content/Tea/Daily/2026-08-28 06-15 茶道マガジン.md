# 尾花一本、秋はまだ細い

今朝は、まだ夏の気配が残る部屋に、一本の細い秋の線を迎えるつもりで読んでみてください。

## 日本語版

暑さがまだ居座っている朝でも、茶席には先に秋が入ることがあります。それは、何か大きな飾りつけによってではなく、たった一本の細い線によってです。花入にすっと立った尾花。あの姿を見ると、秋とは気温が下がってから来るものだけではなく、まず眼差しの置き方から始まる季節なのだと気づかされます。

尾花は、薄、つまりススキの古い呼び名です。『万葉集』で山上憶良が数えた秋の七草のなかにも「尾花」は入り、のちの私たちが「秋」を思い描くときの輪郭のひとつになりました。けれど、茶席で尾花が面白いのは、その文化的な知名度の高さよりも、見え方の慎ましさにあります。萩のように花がこぼれるでもなく、桔梗のように星形を結ぶでもなく、ただ穂先が光を受けてほどけ、風の記憶だけを持ち込む。その控えめさが、茶の湯ではかえって深く働きます。

晩夏の席で尾花を見るとき、そこにあるのは「もう秋です」と言い切る強さではありません。むしろ、「秋は、これからこの場に滲んでくる」という予感です。茶の湯は、季節を標本のように並べる文化ではなく、移り変わりの途中をもっとも大切に見つめる文化だとよく言われます。尾花は、その途中を引き受けるのが上手です。盛りの花ではなく、境目の気配を見せる草だからです。

この感覚は、菓子にも通じます。秋そのものを大声で呼ぶのではなく、まだ暑さの残る口のなかへ、少しだけ野の気配を入れること。たとえば白や淡い茶の意匠、あるいは野辺を思わせるやわらかな銘は、それだけで尾花の細さと響き合います。茶席では、花と菓子が同じことを別の言語で語ることがあります。一本の尾花が床で語ることを、菓子は掌の上でそっと繰り返すのです。

ただし、どの流儀でも、どの席でも、尾花をそのまま同じ意味で扱うわけではありません。花の取り合わせや用いる時季の感覚には、家ごとの好み、土地の気候、亭主の趣向が大きく関わります。だからこそ尾花は、「秋の決まり文句」として覚えるより、今朝その線がどれほど細く、どれほど静かに置かれているかを見るほうが、茶の学びに近いように思います。

尾花は、秋を完成させる花ではありません。秋が来つつあることを、まだ言い切らずに知らせる草です。茶室にその一本があるだけで、客の呼吸は少し長くなり、目は派手な色よりも余白へ向かいます。茶の湯が季節から学んできたのは、満ちた姿だけではなく、満ちる手前のいちばん細い徴だったのかもしれません。

## 今日の見どころ

花入の中で、尾花の穂そのものより「線の立ち上がり」を見てみましょう。まっすぐか、わずかに傾いているか、途中で折れずに空気へ抜けているか。その一本が、床の間に秋を置くというより、まだ夏の残る室内へ秋の入口を描いているように見えたら、今朝の見どころをつかめています。

## 小さな実践

特別な道具はいりません。紙切れでも窓枠でも一本の細い縦線を見つけて、その前で三呼吸だけ静かに座ってみてください。そして「これは何を主張している線ではなく、何を招き入れる線だろう」と考えてみます。茶花がなくても、季節を迎える感覚の稽古になります。

## English Edition

# One Stem of Obana: Autumn Still Arrives as a Fine Line

Even on a morning when the summer heat still refuses to leave, autumn may enter the tearoom first. It does not arrive through a grand arrangement, but through a single fine line: one stem of obana standing upright in a flower container. When you see it, you realize that autumn in tea is not something that begins only after the air cools. It begins earlier, in the way attention is placed.

Obana is an old name for susuki, Japanese pampas grass. In the *Man'yoshu*, Yamanoue no Okura included obana among the seven flowers of autumn, and that poetic naming helped shape how later generations imagined the season. Yet what makes obana especially compelling in a tea setting is not its fame in literature, but its restraint. It does not spill like bush clover, nor does it form a clear star like bellflower. Its plume simply loosens in the light and seems to carry in only a memory of wind. In tea, that modesty works deeply.

When we encounter obana in a late-summer gathering, it does not declare, “Autumn is here,” with certainty. Instead, it gives a quieter message: autumn is about to seep into this place. People often say that tea is not a culture that displays the seasons like specimens lined up in a case; it treasures the very moment of transition. Obana is especially good at holding that threshold. It is not the flower of fullness, but a grass that reveals the feeling of a border.

The same sensibility can appear in sweets. Rather than loudly announcing autumn, a confection may place only a hint of the field into a mouth that still remembers heat. A design in white or pale brown, or a poetic name that suggests open grassland, can resonate with the slenderness of obana. In the tearoom, flowers and sweets sometimes say the same thing in different languages. What one stem of obana says in the alcove, a sweet may quietly repeat in the palm.

That said, no single school and no single host uses obana in exactly the same way. Pairings, timing, and seasonal judgment vary with family tradition, local climate, and the host’s intention. For that reason, it is more useful not to memorize obana as a fixed “autumn symbol,” but to look carefully at how thin the line feels this morning, and how quietly it has been placed. That is closer to the kind of seeing tea asks of us.

Obana is not a flower that completes autumn. It is a grass that lets you know autumn is on its way without stating it too strongly. A single stem in the tearoom can lengthen the guest’s breathing and turn the eyes away from bright color toward intervals and empty space. Perhaps what tea has always learned from the seasons is not only the moment of fullness, but the finest sign that appears just before fullness arrives.

## What to Notice Today

In the flower container, look not only at the feathery plume of the grass, but at the rise of the line itself. Is it straight, or slightly leaning? Does it move upward without breaking? If that single stem seems less like a decoration that “places autumn” in the alcove and more like a doorway that draws autumn into a room still holding summer, you have found today’s point of attention.

## Small Practice

You need no special tools. Find one narrow vertical line near you, perhaps the edge of a window frame or a strip of paper, and sit quietly before it for three breaths. Then ask yourself: this is not a line that insists on itself, so what is it inviting in? Even without a tea flower, this becomes a small exercise in learning how to receive a season.

## Vocabulary

1. 尾花（おばな）- obana; old name for susuki. Usage: A poetic or seasonal word often heard in literary or tea contexts.
2. 薄（すすき）- Japanese pampas grass. Usage: The ordinary plant name; useful when speaking plainly about the flower material.
3. 茶花（ちゃばな）- tea flowers. Usage: Flowers arranged for the tearoom with simplicity rather than showiness.
4. 花入（はないれ）- flower container or vase. Usage: In tea, the vessel matters as much as the plant placed within it.
5. 床の間（とこのま）- alcove. Usage: The visual and spiritual focal point of a traditional tearoom.
6. 穂（ほ）- plume, ear, or seed head. Usage: Often used for grasses such as susuki when discussing shape and movement.
7. 秋の七草（あきのななくさ）- the seven flowers of autumn. Usage: A classical seasonal grouping known from poetry rather than from food.
8. 万葉集（まんようしゅう）- *Man'yoshu*, the early Japanese poetry anthology. Usage: A foundational literary source for many seasonal associations.
9. 山上憶良（やまのうえのおくら）- Yamanoue no Okura. Usage: The Nara-period poet associated with the poems on the autumn seven flowers.
10. 趣向（しゅこう）- aesthetic intention or design of a gathering. Usage: Refers to the host’s overall seasonal and artistic direction.
11. 見立て（みたて）- imaginative seeing or poetic re-reading. Usage: Common in tea when an object or motif suggests something beyond itself.
12. 余白（よはく）- empty space, interval, reserve. Usage: A key word for noticing how tea often works through what is not filled.
