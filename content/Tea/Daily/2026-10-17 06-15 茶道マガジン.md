# 一服のまわりに町が育つ——金沢を歩き、茶の湯と工芸の連なりを読む

晩秋の土曜日、金沢を「名所の一覧」ではなく、一服を支える仕事の連なりとして歩きます。茶碗だけを見て終わらず、釜、菓子、庭、そしてそれらを用いる人へ視線を移す旅です。加賀藩の茶の湯は、藩主の嗜みから工芸の育成へ、さらに町の暮らしへと裾野を広げました。一碗の内側に町全体を映してみましょう。

## 今日の見どころ

- 加賀藩では前田家の歴代藩主が茶の湯を重んじ、金森宗和や千仙叟宗室ら茶人との結びつきが、金沢の茶文化を厚くしました。
- 大樋焼は、仙叟宗室に伴って金沢へ来た長左衛門が寛文六年（一六六六）に窯を築いたことに始まると伝えられ、茶の湯と土地の工芸が結びついた代表例です。
- 庭、器、釜、菓子を別々の名物として数えるのではなく、「一服の時間をどう支え合うか」という関係として観察します。

## 日本語特集

金沢の茶の湯を訪ねるとき、最初から茶室へ急がない歩き方があります。城下町の道幅、水の流れ、工房の看板、菓子屋の季節の意匠を順に見るのです。茶の湯は四畳半の中だけで完結しません。客が席へ着く前から、土地の素材を選ぶ人、道具を作る人、庭を守る人、菓子を整える人の仕事が重なっています。

石川県立図書館の展示資料は、藩祖・前田利家が千利休や織田有楽斎に茶の湯を学び、三代利常が小堀遠州らと交流しながら美術工芸の収集と職人の育成に力を入れたと紹介します。金沢市の歴史資料にも、利常が金森宗和や千仙叟宗室を招き、茶の湯が工芸や作庭、菓子文化へ影響した歩みが記されています。茶を好む権力者の趣味が、注文、技術、働く場を生み、やがて町の文化を形づくったのです。

その結び目をよく示すのが大樋焼です。石川県の文化財解説によれば、裏千家四代の仙叟宗室が寛文六年（一六六六）に金沢へ出仕した際、茶碗師の長左衛門が同行し、河北郡大樋村に窯を築きました。京都の楽焼の系譜を受けながら、金沢の地で茶碗や水指を作る。技法は移動しても、土地、注文主、使い手との関係によって新しい名前と歴史を持ち始めます。

大樋焼に見られる飴釉は、深い褐色のうちに光を抱えます。派手な絵付けで遠くから主張するのではなく、手に取る距離で釉の厚みや揺らぎが立ち上がる色です。茶碗を眺めるときは「茶色」と一語で閉じず、口縁、胴、腰、高台へ光がどう移るかを追ってみます。器は固定された色ではなく、席の明るさと手の動きで変わる景色になります。

ここで大切なのは、大樋焼だけを孤立した名品にしないことです。同じ一服には湯を沸かす釜があり、菓子を載せる器があり、客を日常から導く庭があります。石川県立図書館は、大樋長左衛門とともに茶の湯釜の宮崎寒雉という、現在まで系譜の続く名工を挙げています。茶碗の柔らかな土味と鉄釜の張り、菓子の短い命と庭の長い時間。異なる素材と速度が一席で出会います。

西田家庭園玉泉園へ目を移すと、茶の湯と庭の関係が見えます。金沢市観光協会は、加賀藩上級武士の脇田家により作庭された庭で、金沢最古とされる茶室「灑雪亭」があると紹介しています。庭は茶室の外に付属する飾りではありません。歩幅を緩め、視線を低くし、町の騒がしさから一服の時間へ身体を切り替える装置です。

旅人はつい、限られた時間で多くの「名物」を回ろうとします。しかし茶の湯の町を味わうなら、数を減らす方がよいこともあります。一つの庭で濡れた石と苔の色を見、一つの茶碗で釉の流れを追い、一つの菓子で季節の名を確かめる。三つの観察をつなぐと、土地の文化が単なる観光情報ではなく、感覚の記憶になります。

また、藩の保護を美談だけにしない視点も必要です。工芸は権力者の注文によって発展する一方、作り手の分業、材料の調達、技術の継承、使い手の日々の稽古がなければ続きません。名の残る藩主や茶人の背後に、名の見えにくい多くの手があります。茶碗を見るとき、作者名だけでなく、土を掘り、薪を運び、箱を作り、道具を手入れした人々まで想像することが、町の文化を立体的にします。

金沢を歩く旅の中心は、結局「関係を見る」ことです。茶人と陶工、器と光、庭と歩幅、菓子と暦、客と亭主。一つひとつは別の専門領域でも、一服の場では互いを引き立てます。優れた取り合わせは、すべてを豪華にすることではありません。どれか一つが強ければ、ほかを静かにする。土地の来歴が濃い道具には、説明を増やしすぎない。そうした加減に茶の湯の編集があります。

旅から帰った後も、金沢で学んだ見方は家庭で続けられます。手元の茶碗、湯を沸かす音、小さな菓子、窓辺の一枝を、別々の物ではなく一つの時間の協力者として整えるのです。名品がなくても、関係は作れます。一服のまわりに小さな町を育てるように、道具と手をつないでみましょう。

## 小さな実践

今日の一服に「土・金属・植物」を一つずつ集めます。土は茶碗、金属は湯沸かしや匙、植物は庭の枝でなくても茶葉や菓子の意匠で構いません。三つを並べる前に、それぞれが一服で担う役割を一語で書きます。飲み終えたら、最も強く感じたものと、背景に退いたものを記録してください。取り合わせを、物の価格ではなく役割の釣り合いで見る稽古です。

## Complete English Edition

# A Town Growing Around a Bowl of Tea — Walking Kanazawa Through Chanoyu and Craft

On this late-autumn Saturday, we walk through Kanazawa not as a checklist of famous sights but as a network of work supporting one bowl of tea. We look beyond the bowl toward the kettle, sweets, garden, and people who make and use them. In the Kaga domain, chanoyu spread from the practice of lords into the cultivation of crafts and eventually into the life of the city. Let us see an entire town reflected inside one bowl.

## Today’s Highlights

- Generations of the Maeda family valued chanoyu. Their relationships with leading tea figures, including Kanamori Sōwa and Sensō Sōshitsu, helped deepen Kanazawa’s tea culture.
- Ōhi ware is traditionally traced to 1666, when the potter Chōzaemon accompanied Sensō Sōshitsu to Kanazawa and established a kiln. It is a vivid example of tea practice joining a regional craft.
- Rather than counting gardens, bowls, kettles, and sweets as separate attractions, observe how they support one shared interval of tea.

## Feature

There is a way to visit Kanazawa’s tea culture without hurrying first to a tea room. Begin with the width of the castle-town streets, the movement of water, signs of workshops, and seasonal designs in confectioners’ windows. Chanoyu does not end within four and a half tatami mats. Before a guest sits down, the work of people who select local materials, make utensils, tend gardens, and prepare sweets has already converged.

An Ishikawa Prefectural Library exhibition explains that Maeda Toshiie learned chanoyu from Sen no Rikyū and Oda Urakusai, while the third lord, Toshitsune, interacted with prominent tea practitioners such as Kobori Enshū and fostered the collection of art and the training of craftspeople. Historical materials from Kanazawa likewise record Toshitsune inviting Kanamori Sōwa and Sensō Sōshitsu. The taste of powerful patrons generated commissions, techniques, and places of work that gradually shaped urban culture.

Ōhi ware makes this connection particularly visible. According to Ishikawa Prefecture’s cultural-property commentary, when Sensō Sōshitsu, the fourth-generation head associated with Urasenke, entered service in Kanazawa in 1666, the bowl maker Chōzaemon accompanied him and built a kiln in Ōhi village. A ceramic lineage related to Kyoto Raku practice took on a new name and history through local clay, patrons, and users.

The amber glaze associated with Ōhi ware holds light within a deep brown surface. It does not announce itself from across a room with brilliant painted decoration. Its thickness and variation emerge at the distance of the hands. Instead of closing the observation with the single word “brown,” follow light around the rim, body, hip, and foot. A vessel is not a fixed color; it becomes a changing view through the lighting of the room and the movement of the hand.

The bowl should not be isolated as a masterpiece. The same serving of tea requires a kettle, a vessel for sweets, and a garden that guides the guest away from ordinary pace. The Ishikawa Prefectural Library places the Ōhi ceramic lineage alongside Miyazaki Kanchi’s continuing lineage of tea-kettle makers. Soft clay and taut iron, the brief life of a sweet and the long duration of a garden: materials with different tempos meet in one gathering.

Nishida Family Garden Gyokusen-en reveals the relation between tea and garden. Kanazawa’s official tourism association describes it as a garden created by the Wakita family, senior retainers of the Kaga domain, and as the site of Saisetsu-tei, said to be Kanazawa’s oldest tea room. The garden is not decoration attached to a tea room. It slows the stride, lowers the gaze, and changes the body from the tempo of the city to the time of tea.

Travelers often try to collect as many celebrated sites as possible. To experience a city of tea, however, it may be better to reduce the number. At one garden, notice wet stone and moss. With one bowl, follow the movement of glaze. With one sweet, confirm a seasonal name. Joined together, these three observations turn cultural information into sensory memory.

Patronage should not be treated only as a pleasing story. Crafts may develop through elite commissions, but they cannot continue without divided labor, materials, transmitted skills, and the daily practice of users. Behind the named lord or tea master stand many less visible hands: those who dug clay, carried fuel, made boxes, and cared for utensils. Imagining those hands gives the city’s culture dimension.

The heart of a Kanazawa journey is therefore the act of seeing relationships: tea practitioner and potter, vessel and light, garden and stride, sweet and calendar, host and guest. Each belongs to a distinct field, yet in a gathering each strengthens the others. A successful arrangement does not make everything equally splendid. When one element is strong, another may become quiet. When an object bears a rich history, explanation need not overwhelm it. Chanoyu includes this art of editing.

After the journey, the same way of seeing can continue at home. Treat a familiar bowl, the sound of heating water, a small sweet, and one branch near the window as collaborators in a single interval rather than separate objects. Famous treasures are unnecessary for making relationships. Connect tools and hands as though growing a small town around one bowl of tea.

## A Small Practice

Gather one element each of earth, metal, and plant for today’s tea. Earth may be the bowl; metal may be a kettle or spoon; plant may be tea itself or a motif on a sweet. Before arranging them, write one word for the role each plays. After drinking, note which element felt strongest and which receded into the background. Practice judging an arrangement through balance of roles rather than price.

## 今日のことば — Tea Vocabulary (12)

1. **加賀藩（かがはん / Kaga-han）** — the Edo-period domain ruled by the Maeda family
2. **城下町（じょうかまち / jōkamachi）** — a town developed around a feudal castle
3. **大樋焼（おおひやき / Ōhi-yaki）** — a Kanazawa ceramic tradition closely connected with chanoyu
4. **飴釉（あめゆう / ameyū）** — a translucent amber-to-deep-brown glaze
5. **茶碗師（ちゃわんし / chawanshi）** — a craftsperson specializing in tea bowls
6. **茶の湯釜（ちゃのゆがま / chanoyu-gama）** — an iron kettle used to heat water for tea
7. **作庭（さくてい / sakutei）** — the designing and making of a garden
8. **茶室（ちゃしつ / chashitsu）** — a room or small building made for tea gatherings
9. **取り合わせ（とりあわせ / toriawase）** — the considered combination of utensils and elements for a gathering
10. **土味（つちあじ / tsuchi-aji）** — the felt visual and tactile character of ceramic clay
11. **口縁（こうえん / kōen）** — the rim or lip of a vessel
12. **高台（こうだい / kōdai）** — the foot ring supporting a ceramic vessel
