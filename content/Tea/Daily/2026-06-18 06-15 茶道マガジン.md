## 日本語版｜今朝の特集：躙口（にじりぐち）――小さな入口が、人の心を大きく整える

茶道の道具や作法を学び始めると、つい目は茶碗、茶杓、茶筅、掛物、菓子へ向かいます。どれも美しく、どれも意味が深い。けれど、茶室の学びには、まだ手に取らないものの中にも大事な先生がいます。その代表の一つが、**躙口（にじりぐち）**です。

躙口は、草庵風の茶室に設けられる小さな出入口です。武家屋敷や寺院の大きな門とは対照的で、体をかがめ、膝を折り、手をついて入るほど低くつくられています。立ったまま胸を張って通り抜けることはできません。自然に姿勢が変わり、歩き方が変わり、気持ちまで変わります。茶道は、茶を点てる技術だけでなく、**どのように場に入るか**を教える文化だと、躙口は静かに語っています。

この入口の面白さは、「小さいから不便」という話で終わらないところにあります。むしろ、その不便さが心を整える仕掛けになっています。人は広い場所では、無意識に自分を大きく見せようとします。しかし狭い入口の前では、誰でも少し身を縮めなければなりません。刀を差した武士も、身分の高い人も、茶室の前では同じように頭を下げて入る。そこには、茶の湯が大切にしてきた**平等の感覚**が宿っています。

もちろん、現代の私たちは刀を差して茶室へ行くわけではありません。けれど、躙口の思想は今でも十分に生きています。会社でも家庭でも、つい人は肩書きや役割を持ち込みます。「教える側」「評価する側」「詳しい側」といった立場が強くなりすぎると、目の前の相手や場の空気が見えにくくなります。そんなとき、躙口のことを思い出すといいのです。茶室に入る前に一度、自分を少し小さくする。声を一段静かにする。知識を見せるより、まず相手の言葉を聞く。そうした身の縮め方こそ、現代の躙口かもしれません。

茶室では、躙口をくぐったあとも、その影響が続きます。入室の動作は急げません。畳の感触を確かめ、次の動きを意識し、自然に呼吸が落ち着いていきます。急ぎ足で情報だけを取りに行く空間ではなく、ひとつひとつを見て、気づきながら進む空間へと、体が切り替わるのです。掛物を見る前に、釜の音を聞く前に、まだ茶碗に触れる前に、もう稽古は始まっています。躙口は、茶会の最初の一服とも言えるでしょう。

また、躙口は「外」と「内」の境目をはっきり意識させます。外には天気、時間、仕事、社会の流れがあります。内には、静けさ、約束された手順、季節への注意、もてなしの心があります。もちろん茶室の中でも現実から完全に切り離されるわけではありません。しかし、境目を一度くぐることで、人は気分を切り替えやすくなります。これは現代の暮らしにも応用できます。たとえば朝、机に向かう前に一呼吸置くこと。スマホを置いてからお茶を飲むこと。仕事と休息の間に小さな儀式を持つこと。そうした境目を自分で作れる人は、時間に追われにくくなります。

躙口には、侘びの思想も感じられます。豪華さよりも節度、広さよりも凝縮、見せびらかしよりも内面の深まり。大きいもの、速いもの、便利なものが称賛されやすい時代だからこそ、この小さな入口は逆方向の価値を示してくれます。ほんとうに大切なことは、広く見せることではなく、深く受け取ることではないか。多くを所有することではなく、今ここにある一つを丁寧に扱うことではないか。躙口は、建築でありながら、一つの問いでもあります。

今朝もし茶道の稽古に行く予定がなくても、今日一日のどこかで「自分の躙口」を作ってみてください。部屋に入る前に姿勢を整える。会話に入る前に一拍置く。朝の一杯のお茶を、立ったままではなく座って飲む。そうするだけで、世界への入り方は少し変わります。茶道が教えるのは特別な茶室の中のことだけではなく、**日常に丁寧な入口をつくる技術**なのだと、躙口は教えてくれます。

## English Version | Today’s Feature: Nijiriguchi — How a Small Entrance Greatly Arranges the Heart

When people begin studying the tea ceremony, their attention usually goes first to the visible objects: the tea bowl, the tea scoop, the whisk, the hanging scroll, the sweets. All of them are beautiful, and all of them carry meaning. But in tea, some of the most important teachers are things you do not exactly “pick up” with your hands. One of the best examples is the **nijiriguchi**, the small crawl-through entrance of a rustic tearoom.

A nijiriguchi is intentionally low and small. It stands in sharp contrast to the grand gates of warrior residences or temples. To pass through it, you must bend your body, lower your head, and enter carefully. You cannot stride in proudly with your chest lifted. Your posture changes first, then your movement, and then your state of mind. The nijiriguchi quietly teaches that tea is not only about the skill of making a bowl of matcha. It is also about **how one enters a space**.

What makes this entrance fascinating is that its meaning goes far beyond simple inconvenience. It is not merely small; its smallness serves a purpose. In wide and open places, people often try, without noticing, to make themselves look larger—more important, more authoritative, more central. Before a tiny entrance, however, everyone must make themselves smaller. In earlier times, even a samurai wearing swords had to bow and enter in the same humble way as anyone else. In that gesture lives one of tea’s deepest ideas: a feeling of **equality within the space of the gathering**.

Of course, modern people do not arrive at the tearoom carrying swords. Yet the philosophy of the nijiriguchi is still alive. In offices, homes, and classrooms, we bring our roles with us: the one who teaches, the one who evaluates, the one who knows more, the one who leads. When those roles become too large, we stop seeing the person in front of us and lose sensitivity to the atmosphere of the place. At such moments, it helps to remember the nijiriguchi. Before entering a conversation, make yourself a little smaller. Lower your voice. Listen before displaying knowledge. In that sense, the modern nijiriguchi may not be a physical doorway at all, but a discipline of attitude.

Its effect continues after you pass through it. Once you enter that way, you cannot rush thoughtlessly into the room. You become aware of the tatami beneath you, of the next movement, of your breathing. The body shifts from a mode of grabbing information quickly to a mode of noticing carefully. Before you look at the hanging scroll, before you listen to the kettle, before you touch the tea bowl, practice has already begun. In this way, the nijiriguchi is almost like the first bowl of tea in architectural form.

The entrance also sharpens awareness of the boundary between outside and inside. Outside are weather, schedules, work, and the movements of society. Inside are quiet, shared procedure, seasonal attention, and hospitality. The tearoom does not erase reality, but it does help people cross into a different quality of attention. That idea is surprisingly useful in daily life. Pausing before you sit at your desk, putting your phone down before drinking tea, creating a small ritual between work and rest—these are all modern ways of making thresholds. People who can create such thresholds for themselves are often less ruled by haste.

There is also an unmistakable spirit of wabi in the nijiriguchi. It points toward restraint rather than display, concentration rather than expansion, inward depth rather than outward show. In an age that praises what is bigger, faster, and more convenient, this little entrance offers a counter-value. Perhaps what matters most is not making life look large, but receiving it deeply. Perhaps it is not possessing many things, but handling the one thing before us with care. The nijiriguchi is architecture, yes—but it is also a question posed to the heart.

Even if you are not going to tea practice this morning, you can still create your own nijiriguchi somewhere in the day. Straighten your posture before entering a room. Leave one beat of silence before speaking. Sit down, rather than stand, for your morning tea. Such small acts change the way you enter the world. The tea ceremony teaches not only what happens inside a special tearoom, but also **how to build a careful entrance into ordinary life**.
