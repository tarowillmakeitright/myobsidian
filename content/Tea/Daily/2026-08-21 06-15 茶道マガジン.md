# 常夏という名前が、夏を引き留めず秋を急がせもしない

今朝の東京は雲がちで、残暑のなかに雨の気配もある朝です。茶席でいう「涼」を、物の冷たさではなく心の置き方として思い出したくなります。

## 日本語特集

夏の終わりが近づくころ、茶席では「もう秋です」と言い切るより、まだ残る暑さのなかに先の季節の気配をそっと差し入れる工夫が好まれます。そういう朝にふさわしい言葉の一つが、**常夏（とこなつ）**です。現代の耳には南国めいた響きがありますが、和歌や和菓子の文脈では、これはしばしば**撫子の古名**として扱われます。虎屋は葛製の生菓子「常夏」について、撫子は秋の七草の一つであり、夏の盛りから秋にかけて咲き続けるためこの名があると説明しています。名前のうえでは「常に夏」なのに、実際には夏がそのまま続くことを言うのではなく、**夏が少しずつほどけ、秋へ移る長い継ぎ目**を指している。そこに茶の湯らしい季節感があります。

撫子は、声の大きい花ではありません。牡丹のように場を占めず、蓮のように象徴を背負いすぎず、ただ細い茎の先で、切れ込みのある花弁を軽く震わせます。けれど、その控えめさが茶席ではむしろ強い印象になります。露地や床で目立ちすぎないものほど、客のまなざしは近づき、かえって深く見ようとするからです。裏千家の教室記事でも、晩夏の花として秋海棠と撫子が取り合わせられていました。そこでは撫子は解説の主役ではありませんが、まさにその脇役らしさが茶花としての品を語っています。茶花は植物図鑑の代表選手を並べる場ではなく、**いまこの数日の空気にもっとも正確な花を置く場**だからです。

ここでおもしろいのは、撫子が**秋の七草**に数えられながら、体感としてはまだ夏の名残のなかに咲いていることです。国立国会図書館のレファレンス協同データベースでは、秋の七草は『万葉集』巻八の山上憶良の歌二首に由来すると整理されています。つまり、撫子は古典の上でははっきり秋の側に立っている。しかし茶席の感覚では、その秋は九月の完成形ではなく、八月の終わりにやって来る**未完成の秋**です。まだ蝉の声が濃く、畳に座ると膝裏に熱がこもる。それでも菓子銘や一輪の花が、場の重心をほんの少しだけ先へ移す。その小さな移動こそ、茶の湯が季節を扱うときの繊細さです。

葛製の「常夏」を思い浮かべると、その感覚はさらによく分かります。葛は見た目に涼しく、舌ざわりはやわらかく、光を受けるとどこか水気を帯びて見えます。けれど撫子を表す菓子である以上、それは単なる「冷たい夏菓子」ではありません。透ける生地の向こうに、暑さの終わりと秋の入口が二重写しになる。しかも茶席で菓子は、食べる前からすでに季節の役目を果たしています。懐紙へ取り、黒文字を入れ、切り口を見た瞬間に、客は味覚より先に時間の移ろいを受け取る。**甘さを味わうより前に、名と姿で季節をいただく**のです。

山上憶良の歌にある七草は、もともと春の七草のように食して無病息災を祈るための一覧ではなく、眺め、感じ、秋の野を数え上げるための花々です。だから撫子も、役に立つかどうかより先に、風の細さや光の傾きと結びついています。茶の湯がこの花を好むのは、その小ささに「足りなさ」があるからではなく、**言い切らない季節**を預けやすいからでしょう。真夏を断ち切るほど強くなく、秋を完成させるほど重くない。その中間のかすかな揺れが、一服の茶の前にちょうどよいのです。

金曜の朝にこの花と菓子名を読むと、茶の湯の季節感は先取りの競争ではないと分かります。誰より早く秋を言い当てることではなく、今ある暑さを粗末にせず、その奥に変化の筋を見つけること。**常夏**という名は、夏を引き留める言葉ではありません。むしろ、過ぎていく夏に無理な別れを告げず、次の季節へ静かに席を譲るための言葉です。撫子の細い花弁は、その譲り方をよく知っています。

## 今日の見どころ

菓子や花の「秋らしさ」を大きく探すのではなく、**まだ夏なのに、もう同じ夏ではない**と感じる境目に注目してみてください。葛の透け感、撫子の細い線、懐紙に置いたときの余白の広さは、どれも強い主張ではなく、季節の重心が少し移ったことを知らせる合図です。

## 小さな実践

今日は飲み物が水でもお茶でもかまいません。口にする前に一度だけ器を見て、次の二つを静かに確かめてみてください。  
1. これは「真夏の気分」を強める形か、それとも少し鎮める形か。  
2. いま自分が欲しいのは冷たさそのものか、それとも落ち着きか。  

答えを言葉にできなくても大丈夫です。茶の湯の稽古は、はっきり分類することより、**違いが生まれつつある瞬間に気づくこと**から始まります。

## Full English Edition

### Tokonatsu: A Name That Neither Clings to Summer Nor Rushes Autumn

This morning in Tokyo is cloudy and humid, the kind of morning that recalls a tea truth: coolness in the tearoom is not only a matter of temperature, but of how the heart is arranged.

As the end of summer approaches, the tearoom often prefers not to declare, “Now it is autumn.” Instead, it gently introduces the next season into heat that still remains. One of the finest words for such a moment is **tokonatsu**. To modern ears it may sound tropical, as if it simply meant “eternal summer,” but in waka and wagashi contexts it is often treated as an old name for **nadeshiko**, the wild pink that belongs to the autumn seven grasses. Toraya explains its sweet called “Tokonatsu” in exactly this way: nadeshiko is one of the autumn seven grasses, and because it continues blooming from the height of summer into autumn, the name arose from that long span. So although the word seems to say “summer without end,” what it actually names is something subtler: **the seam where summer loosens and autumn begins**. That seam is very close to the heart of tea.

Nadeshiko is not a loud flower. It does not occupy space like a peony, nor does it carry symbolism as heavily as a lotus. It simply lifts finely cut petals on a slender stem, almost as if the blossom were listening more than speaking. Yet that reserve can make it stronger in the tearoom. The less a flower insists on itself, the more the guest’s gaze moves toward it, and the more deeply it is seen. In one Urasenke lesson report, nadeshiko appeared in late summer together with shukaido. It was not treated there as a grand subject, and that is precisely the point. In tea, flowers need not dominate to matter. Tea flowers are not chosen as champions from a botanical guide; they are chosen because they are **the most accurate flowers for the air of these few days**.

What makes nadeshiko especially compelling is that it belongs, in classical terms, to autumn, while in lived sensation it still blooms inside the residue of summer. The National Diet Library’s reference database notes that the autumn seven grasses are traced to two poems by Yamanoue no Okura in Book Eight of the *Man'yoshu*. So on the page, nadeshiko stands clearly on the side of autumn. But in the tearoom, that autumn is not the finished autumn of September. It is an **unfinished autumn**, arriving in late August: cicadas still sounding, heat still gathered beneath the knees on tatami, yet a sweet name or a single stem quietly shifting the center of the room forward. That slight shift is one of tea’s most refined seasonal gestures.

If we imagine the kuzumade sweet called “Tokonatsu,” the feeling becomes even clearer. Kuzumade confections look cool, feel soft on the tongue, and catch light with a watery transparency. But when such a sweet bears the name of nadeshiko, it is no longer just “a refreshing summer sweet.” Through the translucent body of the confection, the end of heat and the entrance of autumn seem to overlap. And in tea, a sweet begins its work before it is eaten. The guest receives the season in the name, the form, the way it rests on the paper, the way the cutting edge reveals the inside. **Before tasting sweetness, one tastes time.**

Okura’s autumn grasses were not assembled in the same spirit as the spring seven herbs that are eaten for health and ritual renewal. They belong more to seeing, counting, and dwelling in the field of autumn. Nadeshiko, then, is tied not only to botany but to a certain thinness of wind and tilt of light. Tea seems to love this flower because it can hold a season that has not fully declared itself. It is not strong enough to sever summer in two, nor heavy enough to complete autumn in a single stroke. It carries that small interval in between, and that interval is perfectly suited to a bowl of tea.

Read on a Friday morning, this flower and this confectionary name remind us that tea does not treat the seasons as a contest in prediction. The point is not to announce autumn before everyone else. The point is to refrain from treating the present heat carelessly, while sensing the line of change within it. **Tokonatsu** does not try to hold summer in place. It offers a gentler courtesy: not forcing farewell, but quietly yielding the seat to what comes next. The narrow petals of nadeshiko seem to know how to yield in exactly that way.

### Today’s Point of Attention

Do not look for a dramatic sign of autumn. Instead, notice the threshold where you feel, “It is still summer, but not the same summer anymore.” The translucence of kuzumade sweets, the fine lines of nadeshiko, and the spacious margin around them on the paper all signal that the season’s center of gravity has moved a little.

### Small Practice

Today, whether you drink tea or only water, pause once before taking the first sip and quietly ask:

1. Does this vessel strengthen the feeling of midsummer, or soften it?
2. What I want right now, is it coldness itself, or calm?

You do not need a perfect answer. Tea practice often begins not by classifying things completely, but by noticing **the moment when a difference is beginning to appear**.

| 常夏 | とこなつ | old poetic name for nadeshiko; also a wagashi name | Useful when discussing seasonal confection names rather than climate. |
| 撫子 | なでしこ | dianthus; one of the autumn seven grasses | Often appears in seasonal poetry, wagashi, and tea-flower talk. |
| 秋の七草 | あきのななくさ | the seven grasses of autumn | Refers to flowers appreciated for viewing, unlike the spring herbs commonly eaten. |

| 名残 | なごり | lingering trace; the remains of a season | A key tea word for the emotional tone of something passing. |

| 余白 | よはく | empty space; margin | Helpful for noticing how spacing affects atmosphere in tea aesthetics. |
