# 茶道マガジン — 2026-06-22

## 今朝の特集
**炉から風炉へ — 季節が点前を動かすという感覚**

茶道を学び始めた人が最初に驚くことのひとつは、同じ「お茶を点てる」という行いであっても、季節によって道具の置き方、体の向き、客の感じ方、場の空気までもが変わることです。茶道は単なる作法の集まりではなく、季節に対する感受性をかたちにする文化です。その象徴的な変化が、**炉**から**風炉**への移り変わりです。

炉は冬の茶の中心です。畳に切られた炉は、火を客の近くに招き寄せます。そこには暖を分ける意味があり、寒い季節に人と人がひとつの火を囲む親しさがあります。釜の湯がしっかりと沸き、炭の気配が座に深みを与えます。冬の茶室には、静けさのなかにも内へ向かうぬくもりがあります。身体が縮こまりやすい時期だからこそ、火の存在が強く意識され、亭主のもてなしもまた「寒さをどう和らげるか」という方向に働きます。

一方で、風炉は夏へ向かう茶のしつらえです。畳の上に置かれた風炉は、炉とは違って空間に軽さを生みます。火を少し遠ざけ、見た目にも涼やかさを意識させます。冬の炉が「近づく火」だとすれば、夏の風炉は「整えられた火」です。火を消すのではなく、火との距離感を工夫する。ここに茶道らしい発想があります。自然を力で変えるのではなく、受け止め方を調えることで快適さをつくるのです。

この変化は、道具組にも現れます。暑さを感じ始める頃には、見込みの深い茶碗より、口が少し開いた茶碗のほうが涼やかに見えることがあります。蓋置や水指の素材も、竹、籠、ガラス、焼締など、季節感を含んだ選び方が意識されます。掛物もまた、強い言葉で場を引き締めるより、風や水を感じさせるものが座に合うことがあります。茶室の中で実際の気温を大きく変えることはできなくても、人の感じ方は道具と空間の工夫で変えられる。茶道はその繊細な領域を扱う芸術でもあります。

ここで大事なのは、季節の道具を「暗記項目」として覚えるだけでは足りないということです。なぜ炉を閉じ、なぜ風炉を据えるのか。その理由に身体感覚で近づくことが、学びを深くします。六月の朝に湯気を見たとき、冬なら心地よく感じる熱気が、初夏には少し重たく感じられるかもしれません。逆に、風の通りや水の気配には心が動きやすくなります。茶道の季節感は、気候の変化を美意識へ翻訳する作業だと言えます。

また、炉から風炉への移り変わりには、稽古の姿勢そのものを見直すきっかけがあります。初心者はつい「手順を間違えないこと」に集中します。もちろんそれは大切です。ただ、手順は目的ではなく、もてなしを成立させる骨組みです。季節が変わると、その骨組みに何をのせるべきかも変わります。冬には安心感、春にはやわらかなほどけ、夏には軽さと清潔感。亭主は点前を通じて、季節に合った心地よさを客へ渡します。

だからこそ、風炉の季節に入ったら、単に「置き方が変わる」と考えるより、「場の呼吸を軽くする」と考えるほうが自然です。歩き方、道具を置く音、柄杓の扱い、水の見せ方。どれも少しずつ印象を変えます。強く主張せず、しかし確かに違う。その積み重ねが、茶道の季節表現です。

現代の生活では空調があり、季節の厳しさは昔より和らいでいます。それでも茶道が今なお季節を大切にするのは、人が快適さを温度だけで決めていないからでしょう。視覚、音、手触り、距離感、期待感。そうした要素が合わさって「涼しい」「あたたかい」と感じます。茶道はその複合的な感覚に、驚くほど意識的です。

今日のお稽古や読書では、ぜひ「炉と風炉の違い」を形式としてではなく、**もてなしの温度調整**として眺めてみてください。すると、ひとつひとつの作法が急に生きた理由を持ちはじめます。茶道は、季節を説明する文化ではありません。季節を感じさせる文化です。その入口として、炉から風炉への移り変わりほどわかりやすく、そして奥深い題材はありません。

## English Feature
**From Ro to Furo — How the Seasons Move the Tea Procedure**

One of the first surprises for a beginning student of sadō is that even though the act seems simple—prepare and serve tea—the placement of utensils, the direction of movement, the feeling of the guest, and even the atmosphere of the room all change with the season. Tea is not just a collection of rules. It is a culture that gives form to sensitivity toward time, weather, and human comfort. One of the clearest examples is the transition from **ro** to **furo**.

The **ro** is the sunken hearth used in the colder season. Cut directly into the tatami, it brings the fire physically and emotionally closer to the guests. It offers warmth, but more than that, it creates intimacy: people gathering around one source of heat. The kettle boils firmly, charcoal presence deepens the room, and the winter tea space carries a kind of inward warmth. Because the body tightens in cold weather, the presence of fire is felt more strongly, and the host’s hospitality naturally turns toward easing the season’s severity.

The **furo**, by contrast, belongs to the warmer season. Placed above the tatami, it changes the visual balance of the room. It creates more openness and lightness. The fire is still present, but it is handled differently: not eliminated, not denied, simply placed at a more considerate distance. If the winter ro is “fire brought near,” then the summer furo is “fire carefully arranged.” That distinction is deeply characteristic of tea culture. Instead of conquering nature, tea adjusts human perception so that the same reality can be experienced more gently.

This shift appears clearly in utensil selection as well. As the weather warms, a bowl with a slightly more open shape may feel visually cooler than one with a deeper form. Materials begin to matter in different ways: bamboo, basketry, glass, unglazed ceramics—each can suggest seasonality without explanation. The lid rest, the water jar, the hanging scroll, even the emotional weight of the calligraphy may be chosen with a lighter touch. A room cannot radically alter the outside climate, but it can shape how that climate is received. Tea works in that subtle space between physical condition and felt experience.

That is why memorizing seasonal differences as if they were exam items is not enough. A student should ask: why close the ro, and why set the furo? The answer becomes richer when felt through the body. Steam that seems welcome in winter can feel a little heavy in early summer. A hint of moving air or the visual presence of fresh water becomes more meaningful. In this sense, tea translates climate into aesthetic awareness.

The transition from ro to furo also offers an important lesson about practice itself. Beginners often focus so intensely on not making mistakes that they treat procedure as the final goal. Of course, correct procedure matters. But procedure is really a structure that supports hospitality. When the season changes, what that structure must carry also changes. Winter calls for reassurance and warmth. Spring may suggest softness and release. Early summer asks for lightness, freshness, and cleanliness of feeling. Through the same basic actions, the host delivers a seasonally appropriate comfort.

So when furo season begins, it is more useful to think not merely, “the arrangement changes,” but rather, “the breathing of the room becomes lighter.” The walking, the sound of placing utensils, the handling of the ladle, the appearance of water—each contributes in a small way. None needs to be theatrical. Tea is powerful precisely because the difference is understated yet unmistakable.

Modern life has air conditioning, and seasonal hardship is softened compared with earlier centuries. Yet tea still values seasonality because human comfort is never decided by temperature alone. Visual impression, sound, texture, distance, expectation, and mood all combine to create the feeling of coolness or warmth. Sadō remains astonishingly conscious of this layered reality.

So in today’s study, try looking at the difference between ro and furo not merely as formal classification, but as a way of **adjusting the temperature of hospitality**. Once you see it that way, individual procedures begin to acquire living reasons. Tea does not simply explain the season. It makes the season perceptible. And as an entrance into that world, the shift from ro to furo is both accessible and profound.

## Study Section — Vocabulary (12)
1. 炉（ろ）— sunken hearth used in the colder season
2. 風炉（ふろ）— portable brazier used in the warmer season
3. しつらえ — arrangement; seasonal setting of a space
4. 点前（てまえ）— tea procedure; formal preparation of tea
5. 亭主（ていしゅ）— host of a tea gathering
6. 客（きゃく）— guest
7. 道具組（どうぐぐみ）— selection and combination of utensils
8. 掛物（かけもの）— hanging scroll displayed in the tea room
9. 蓋置（ふたおき）— utensil rest for the kettle lid or ladle
10. 水指（みずさし）— fresh water container
11. 涼やか（すずやか）— cool-looking; visually refreshing
12. もてなし — hospitality; thoughtful care for the guest
