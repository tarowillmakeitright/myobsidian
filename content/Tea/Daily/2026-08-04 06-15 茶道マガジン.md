# 規矩の内側に、一客を迎える——片桐石州の武家茶

朝の一碗の前に、「武家茶道」を堅い作法の集積としてではなく、多くの人へ茶を伝えるために形を整えた仕事として見つめます。

## 日本語特集

片桐石州という名には、どこか端正で近寄りがたい響きがあります。武家茶道、将軍家の茶道指南役、規矩、伝書。こうした言葉だけを並べれば、石州の茶は自由な侘び茶とは反対側にある、格式の体系のように見えるかもしれません。しかし今朝は、その印象を少しほどいてみたいと思います。形を定めることは、心を閉じ込めることなのでしょうか。それとも、立場も経験も異なる人々が一つの席を共有するために、迷いを減らす働きなのでしょうか。

片桐石州、本名貞昌は1605年に生まれ、1673年に没した江戸時代前期の大名茶人です。大和小泉藩の藩主であり、石州流の流祖として知られます。大和郡山市の人物紹介は、石州が四代将軍徳川家綱のために『茶道軌範』を作り、石州流の基礎を確かなものにしたと説明しています。石州流宗家も、石州が家綱の茶道指南役に推挙され、その茶が将軍家をはじめ武家社会へ定着したと伝えています。ここで重要なのは、「将軍に教えた」という華やかな肩書だけではありません。個人の感覚に留まりやすい茶の湯を、他者が学び、受け渡せる形へ整えた点です。

武家の社会では、所作は一人だけの美意識では済みません。身分や役目の異なる人が同席し、限られた空間で互いの位置を読み取ります。どこに座るか、いつ一礼するか、どの順で道具を扱うか。約束が共有されていれば、客は次に何が起こるかを予想でき、亭主も客を不安にさせずに済みます。規矩は威圧のためだけにあるのではなく、席中の交通整理にもなるのです。石州の歴史を「規則が増えた」とだけ読むより、「見知らぬ者どうしでも一碗を囲める共通語が磨かれた」と読むと、その仕事の温度が変わります。

もちろん、石州流を一枚岩の作法として語るのは正確ではありません。後世には複数の系統へ展開し、伝承や点前にもそれぞれの姿があります。また「武家茶道」という語から、勇ましさや権威だけを想像するのも早計です。石州の茶が制度の場に受け入れられたことと、一席の心が機械的になったことは同じではありません。規矩は茶の目的ではなく、客へ注意を向けるための足場です。足運びや置き合わせに迷い続けなければ、そのぶん亭主は湯の加減、客の様子、席の呼吸へ意識を向けられます。

東京国立博物館には、石州作の竹茶杓「稲羽州サマ」が所蔵されています。博物館はこれを江戸時代前期の大名茶人・片桐石州による作とし、17世紀の品として紹介しています。細い竹の茶杓一本を前にすると、将軍家や制度という大きな言葉が、急に手の大きさへ戻ってきます。茶杓は抹茶をすくい、茶碗へ運ぶ小さな道具です。どれほど広く流儀が伝わっても、最後に起こるのは、一本の茶杓が一人分の茶を量るという具体的な動作です。歴史は大きくても、一服は常に一客へ向かいます。

石州の影響はその死後にも続きました。石州流は諸大名家へ広がり、幕末の大名茶人・井伊直弼もその伝統を学びました。ただし、後世のすべてを石州一人の意図へ還元するべきではありません。伝承は受け手によって読み直され、枝分かれします。だからこそ歴史を見るときは、「誰が最初に決めたか」だけでなく、「定められた形が、次の人の手でどう生き直したか」まで見る必要があります。型が残るとは、同じ動きを凍結することではなく、問いを繰り返し手渡せることです。

今朝、石州から受け取りたいのは、形式を無条件にありがたがる心ではありません。形式の役目を確かめる目です。その一礼は、誰を安心させるのか。その順序は、何を見えやすくするのか。その決まりがなくなれば自由になるのか、それとも誰かが戸惑うのか。良い規矩は、人を小さくする壁ではなく、互いにぶつからず近づくための縁になります。

盛夏の朝は、動きを増やすほど身体も心も熱を持ちます。そんなとき、所作の順を一つ整えておくことは、堅苦しさではなく涼しさにもなります。次の動作を探して慌てず、決めた順に湯を沸かし、器を置き、一服を差し出す。石州の歴史は、茶の湯が個人のひらめきだけでなく、共有できる形によっても深くなることを教えます。規矩のいちばん奥にあるのは規矩そのものではなく、目の前の客へ無理なく心を向けられる静けさなのです。

## 今日の見どころ

「武家茶道＝厳格」と一語で閉じず、決められた順序が席中の不安をどう減らすかに注目してください。大きな制度の歴史と、茶杓一すくいの小さな動作が同じ一席の中でつながるところが、今日の見どころです。

## 小さな実践

いつもの飲み物を用意する前に、動作を三つだけ決めます。たとえば「器を置く、湯を注ぐ、一呼吸してから持つ」。その順を急がずに一度行い、決まりが自分を縛ったか、それとも迷いを減らしたかを観察してください。型の上手下手ではなく、空いた注意を目の前の一杯へ戻す稽古です。

## Full English Edition

# Welcoming One Guest Within a Shared Form: Katagiri Sekishū and Warrior Tea

Before the morning bowl, let us look at “warrior tea” not as a collection of rigid manners, but as the work of shaping tea so that it could be taught and shared.

The name Katagiri Sekishū can sound formal and slightly distant. Warrior tea, tea instructor to the shogun, rules, transmitted texts: placed together, these words may make his tea seem like a system of rank standing opposite the freedom of wabi tea. Yet establishing form does not necessarily imprison feeling. It can also reduce uncertainty so that people of different experience and position may share one room.

Katagiri Sekishū, whose personal name was Sadamasa, lived from 1605 to 1673. He was an early Edo-period daimyō tea master, lord of the Koizumi domain in Yamato, and founder of the Sekishū tradition. Yamato Koriyama City explains that he prepared the *Sadō Kihan* for the fourth Tokugawa shogun, Ietsuna, helping establish his tea tradition. The Sekishū head-family site likewise records that he was appointed Ietsuna’s tea instructor and that his tradition became established among the shogunal and warrior houses. The important point is not simply the brilliance of teaching a shogun. Sekishū helped turn tea from an individual sensibility into forms that others could learn and transmit.

In warrior society, movement could not remain a private aesthetic choice. People of different duties and ranks occupied a limited room and had to read one another’s positions. Where to sit, when to bow, and in what order to handle utensils all mattered. When conventions are shared, the guest can anticipate what comes next, and the host can avoid creating needless uncertainty. Rules can therefore serve as the traffic pattern of a gathering. Sekishū’s achievement feels warmer when read not merely as an increase in regulation, but as the refinement of a common language through which even unfamiliar people could share tea.

It would be inaccurate to describe Sekishū tea as one unchanging block of procedure. It later developed through several lines, each with its own transmission. Nor should “warrior tea” be reduced to authority or severity. Acceptance within institutional settings does not mean the heart of the gathering became mechanical. Form is not the final purpose of tea; it is scaffolding that allows attention to reach the guest. When the host is not continually lost over footwork or placement, more attention becomes available for the water, the guest, and the breathing rhythm of the room.

The Tokyo National Museum preserves a bamboo tea scoop made by Sekishū called *Inaushū-sama*. The museum identifies it as the work of the early Edo daimyō tea master and dates it to the seventeenth century. Before one slender scoop, large words such as “shogunate” and “institution” return suddenly to the scale of a hand. A tea scoop performs a small task: it carries matcha into a bowl for a serving of tea. However widely a tradition spreads, the final act remains particular—one scoop measures tea for one guest. History may be large, but a bowl is always directed toward someone.

Sekishū’s influence continued after his death. His tradition spread among daimyō houses, and the later lord and tea thinker Ii Naosuke studied within its inheritance. Still, we should not reduce everything that followed to Sekishū’s personal intention. Transmission is reread by those who receive it and grows into branches. Tea history therefore asks not only who first determined a form, but how that form became alive again in later hands. For a pattern to endure is not for movement to be frozen. It is for a question to remain transferable.

What we can receive from Sekishū this morning is not blind reverence for form, but an eye for what form does. Whom does a bow reassure? What does an order of actions make easier to see? If a convention disappeared, would everyone become freer, or would someone be left uncertain? A good rule is not a wall that diminishes people. It is an edge that lets them approach without colliding.

On a midsummer morning, the body and mind grow hotter as unnecessary movements multiply. Setting one sequence in order can offer coolness rather than stiffness: boil the water, place the vessel, and offer the bowl without anxiously searching for the next action. Sekishū’s history shows that tea deepens not only through private inspiration but through forms that can be shared. At the deepest point of discipline is not discipline for its own sake, but the quiet ability to return attention to the guest before you.

## What to Notice Today

Do not close the subject with the equation “warrior tea equals strictness.” Notice instead how an agreed sequence can reduce uncertainty in the room. Today’s key connection is between the large history of institutions and the tiny movement of one scoop of tea toward one guest.

## Small Practice

Before preparing your usual drink, choose only three actions—for example: place the cup, pour the water, take one breath before lifting it. Perform them once without rushing. Notice whether the sequence restricted you or freed attention for the drink itself. This is not a test of correct form; it is practice in returning spare attention to what is present.

## Vocabulary

1. **片桐石州（かたぎりせきしゅう）** — Katagiri Sekishū; an early Edo daimyō tea master and founder of the Sekishū tradition.
2. **武家茶道（ぶけさどう）** — warrior-class tea traditions; avoid reducing the term to mere severity.
3. **大名茶人（だいみょうちゃじん）** — a daimyō who was also an accomplished tea practitioner.
4. **流祖（りゅうそ）** — founder of a tradition or school.
5. **茶道指南役（さどうしなんやく）** — official tea instructor or adviser.
6. **規矩（きく）** — rules, standards, and disciplined form that guide practice.
7. **伝書（でんしょ）** — transmitted instructional text within a tradition.
8. **茶道軌範（さどうきはん）** — *Sadō Kihan*, a text associated with Sekishū’s instruction for Tokugawa Ietsuna.
9. **一客（いっきゃく）** — one guest; a useful word for returning grand history to personal hospitality.
10. **茶杓（ちゃしゃく）** — bamboo tea scoop used to transfer matcha into the bowl.
11. **所作（しょさ）** — composed physical action or procedure.
12. **伝承（でんしょう）** — transmission across generations, including later interpretation and branching.
