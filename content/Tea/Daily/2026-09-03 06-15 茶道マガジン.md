# 見立ては、足りなさではなく眼の仕事

朝の招き: ありふれたものが、見方ひとつで静かな名品に変わることを思いながら、一服ぶんの眼を澄ませてみましょう。

今朝の東京は雲が厚く湿り気の残る朝で、湯気の白さがいつもよりやわらかく見えます。

## 日本語版

茶の湯で「見立て」というと、つい倹約や工夫の話として受け取られがちです。けれども本当は、足りないから何かで間に合わせる、という後ろ向きの知恵だけではありません。むしろ、すでにそこにある物のなかに、まだ表に出ていない別の姿を見つける眼の働きです。茶人は物を「別の物として見る」ことで、物の用途だけでなく、その場に流れる時間まで組み替えてきました。

表千家不審菴の解説では、「見立て」はもともと漢詩や和歌の技法に由来する言葉だと説明されています。つまり茶の湯における見立ては、台所や納戸の工夫が先にあるのではなく、まず言葉と想像力の文化に根をもつ、かなり繊細な美意識なのです。千利休が日常の生活用品を茶道具に取り入れたこと、たとえば瓢箪を花入として用いた逸話や、船の出入り口のような潜り口から茶室のにじり口を発想したと伝えられる話は、その感覚をよく示しています。大事なのは、珍しい物を珍しがったのではなく、場にふさわしい緊張と余白を見抜いたことでした。

ここで面白いのは、見立てが「安いものを高く見せる」技法ではない点です。茶の湯はしばしば名物や由緒の世界と結びついて語られますが、その一方で、無名の物や日用の器に深い価値を見いだしてきました。メトロポリタン美術館の茶室展示解説でも、茶道具には装飾の少ない日用品がその美質や簡素さによって転用されることがあったと述べられ、例として釣りのおもりが蓋置として用いられたことが紹介されています。つまり見立ては、格式を壊す反抗ではなく、「何がこの席にほんとうに要るのか」を問い直す作法でもあるのです。

利休の時代、既成の器を茶に取り込む見立ての感覚は、やがて茶のために新たな道具を生み出す創造へもつながりました。表千家の別の解説は、利休の茶が天正十年代に大成へ向かう一つの根拠として、利休の意匠のもとに新しい茶道具が現れたことを挙げています。そのなかには楽長次郎による楽茶碗があり、天正14年、すなわち1586年の茶会記に「宗易形」の茶碗が見えることから、この頃には利休好みの茶碗が用いられていたと考えられるそうです。ただし、利休と長次郎の関係を具体的に示す史料は残っていないとも明記されており、このあたりは断定よりも、当時の茶会記から輪郭をたどるほうが誠実でしょう。

この流れを見ていると、見立てとは「なんでも茶道具にしてしまう自由」ではなく、むしろ自由を厳しく支える審美眼だとわかってきます。何を持ち込んでもよいのではありません。持ち込まれたとたんに、その場の沈黙、客の視線、季節のわずかな傾きに耐えられる物だけが残る。だから見立てには、派手な発明より、静かな選び直しの気配があります。

私たちの日常でも同じかもしれません。棚の上の小皿、使い込んだ木の盆、窓辺に置いた一輪の草。茶の湯の見立ては、それらを無理に「茶風」に飾ることではなく、もともとその物が持っていた輪郭を見直し、いちばん静かに立ち上がる置き方を探すことに近い。見立ては、物を変える前に、自分の見方を少し正す稽古なのだと思います。

## 今日の見どころ

今朝どこかで茶室の写真や道具の写真を見るなら、「これは本来何だったか」より先に、「なぜこの場ではこれでなければならないように見えるのか」を考えてみてください。見立ての妙は変身の派手さではなく、場に入った途端に不自然さが消えるところにあります。瓢箪がただの瓢箪に見えなくなる瞬間、あるいは小さな金属片が蓋置として急に姿勢を持ち始める瞬間に注目してみましょう。

## 小さな実践

家の中で、ふだんは実用品としてしか見ていない小さな物をひとつ選びます。鍵皿、箸置き、紙包みを押さえる文鎮代わりの石でもかまいません。それを机や棚の上に静かに置き直し、三十秒だけ眺めてみてください。「何に使う物か」はいったん脇に置き、「どんな線を持っているか」「どの向きでいちばん落ち着くか」だけを見るのです。使わないまま終えてよく、意味づけを急ぐ必要もありません。見立ての最初の稽古は、名づけることより、見る速度を落とすことです。

## English Edition

# Mitate Is the Work of the Eye, Not a Sign of Lack

Morning invitation: Let this morning begin with the thought that an ordinary thing can become quietly precious when it is seen with a clearer eye.

When people hear the tea term *mitate*, they often assume it means thrift, improvisation, or making do with what is available. But that is only the shallowest layer. In tea, *mitate* is not mainly a strategy for shortage. It is an act of perception: seeing in an existing thing a second life that has not yet fully appeared. By seeing one thing as another, tea practitioners did more than change the function of an object. They changed the quality of time inside the room.

According to Omotesenke Fushin'an, the word *mitate* originally comes from techniques of Chinese poetry and waka. That matters. It suggests that *mitate* in tea does not begin as household cleverness. It begins in a literary and imaginative culture, in the refined ability to recognize resonance between unlike things. Stories associated with Sen no Rikyu show this clearly: a gourd used as a flower container, or the idea that the crawl-through entrance of the tea room, the *nijiriguchi*, drew on the image of a small hatch used for entering a boat. The point was not novelty for its own sake. It was the ability to recognize which forms could create tension, humility, and breathing space in the gathering.

What makes this especially interesting is that *mitate* is not a method for making cheap things look expensive. Tea is often described through famous objects, lineages, and treasured names, yet it has also long valued anonymous things and everyday vessels. In the Metropolitan Museum of Art's explanation of a tea room display, some utensils are described as plain daily objects repurposed for their simplicity or aesthetic force; one example is a fishing weight used as a *futaoki*, a rest for the kettle lid or ladle. Seen this way, *mitate* is not rebellion against formality. It is a disciplined way of asking what the gathering truly needs.

In Rikyu's time, the sensibility that repurposed existing objects gradually opened into the creation of entirely new utensils made for tea. Another Omotesenke essay explains that one sign of the maturity of Rikyu's tea in the Tensho 1580s was the appearance of newly conceived tea utensils based on his designs. Among them were Raku bowls associated with Raku Chojiro. The same source notes that a tea gathering record from Tensho 14, or 1586, mentions a bowl in the style of Soeki, Rikyu's earlier name, suggesting that such bowls were already in use by then. At the same time, the essay is careful to say that no concrete historical record survives to define the relationship between Rikyu and Chojiro in detail. That restraint is important. Tea history often becomes more trustworthy when we let the edges remain visible.

From this angle, *mitate* is not permission to turn anything whatsoever into a tea utensil. It is almost the opposite. It demands an exacting eye. Not every object can bear the silence of a tea room, the attention of guests, or the subtle tilt of the season. Only some forms can enter the room and immediately shed their awkwardness. That is why *mitate* feels less like flamboyant invention and more like a quiet act of choosing again.

This has meaning outside the tearoom as well. A small plate on a shelf, a worn wooden tray, a single stem by the window: tea does not ask us to force these things into a stylized Japanese mood. It asks us to see their outlines again, and to find the position in which they stand most quietly and most truly. *Mitate* changes the object only after it has first changed the eye.

## What To Notice Today

If you look at a tea room image or utensil today, pause before asking, “What was this originally?” Ask instead, “Why does it seem inevitable here?” The subtle power of *mitate* is not theatrical transformation. It is the way strangeness disappears once the object enters the right setting. Notice the moment when a gourd stops looking merely like a gourd, or when a small metal piece suddenly acquires posture as a *futaoki*.

## Small Practice

Choose one small object at home that you normally regard only as useful: a dish for keys, a chopstick rest, even a stone that holds down loose paper. Place it quietly on a desk or shelf and look at it for thirty seconds. Set aside the question of function. Notice only its lines, its weight, and which direction lets it feel most at rest. You do not need to use it differently, and you do not need to force a meaning onto it. The first practice of *mitate* is not naming. It is slowing down the speed at which you look.

## Vocabulary

1. 見立て（みたて）- *mitate; aesthetic repurposing or imaginative re-seeing* - Used when an object is perceived as something more than its original function.
2. 茶道具（ちゃどうぐ）- *tea utensils* - Refers broadly to the implements and objects used in tea practice.
3. 瓢箪（ひょうたん）- *gourd* - Often appears in tea writing as an everyday object that can be transformed by context.
4. 花入（はないれ）- *flower container* - A vessel used to place flowers in the tearoom; not every one is originally made for tea.
5. にじり口（にじりぐち）- *crawl-through entrance* - The small entrance associated with humility and bodily awareness in a tea room.
6. 蓋置（ふたおき）- *lid rest* - A small support for the kettle lid or ladle, often revealing subtle choices of taste.
7. 茶室（ちゃしつ）- *tea room* - The architectural space where tea gathering meaning is concentrated.
8. 侘び（わび）- *wabi; austere, refined humility* - Use carefully; it suggests cultivated simplicity, not mere poverty.
9. 余白（よはく）- *margin, breathing space* - Useful for describing the felt openness around an object or gesture.
10. 審美眼（しんびがん）- *discerning aesthetic eye* - A good word for the judgment needed in tea selection and arrangement.
11. 宗易形（そうえきがた）- *Soeki-style* - Refers to a form associated with Sen no Rikyu under his earlier name, Soeki.
12. 茶会記（ちゃかいき）- *record of a tea gathering* - Important historical documents used to trace practice and attribution.
