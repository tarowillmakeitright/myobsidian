# 斜面を読む、和束の茶畑

朝の視線を少し遠くへ送り、茶碗の一服が生まれる前から山の仕事は始まっていることを、和束の斜面から静かに受け取ってみましょう。

## 日本語特集

京都府南部の和束町を訪ねると、まず目に入るのは「茶畑がある風景」ではなく、山の起伏そのものが茶畑になっている光景です。平らな地面に畝を置いたのではなく、丘の肩から谷へ向かう線に沿って、茶が山の呼吸をなぞるように植えられている。茶の湯に親しむ者がこの景色に惹かれるのは、ここに美しさがあるからだけではありません。抹茶や煎茶や玉露へ分かれてゆく前の、日本茶の時間の厚みが、まだ地形のうえに見えているからです。

京都府教育委員会は、和束町の宇治茶の茶畑景観について、山裾から山頂近くまで丘陵の起伏に沿って茶畑が広がること、そして地形を大きく改変せずに営まれてきたことを示しています。この説明は、和束の景色をただの「映える茶畑」から救い出します。整えられた観光写真として見ると、私たちはつい緑の縞だけを見ます。けれど茶の湯の眼で見るなら、大切なのは縞の規則正しさより、規則が地形に従って少しずつ揺れていることです。一直線に見える畝も、実際には斜面の傾き、水の流れ、日当たり、手入れのしやすさと折り合いをつけた結果としてそこにあります。つまり、和束の美しさは設計図の美ではなく、長く続いた判断の美なのです。

和束町の案内では、この土地が宇治茶の主産地であることが簡潔に述べられていますが、その言い方には重みがあります。主産地とは、単に量をつくる場所ではありません。暮らしの単位で茶が繰り返され、山あいの集落、茶畑、茶工場、道の曲がり方までが茶に合わせて形づくられていく場所です。京都府が進める「宇治茶の文化的景観」の説明でも、山城地域は約七百年にわたって宇治茶を生産し、抹茶・煎茶・玉露といった日本の喫茶文化を支えてきたとされます。そのなかで和束は、茶室の内側に置かれる一碗の前史を、野外の起伏として今も見せてくれる場所だと言えるでしょう。

茶席では、道具はしばしば手の中に収まる大きさで現れます。茶杓も、茶碗も、蓋置も、触れられる距離にある。ところが和束の茶畑は、その感覚を少し裏返します。ここでは道具のように見えるのは山のほうです。畝は櫛目のようであり、斜面は大きな器の内側のようでもある。もし石寺あたりの茶畑を眺めながら「きれいだ」とだけ思って終わるなら、それは十分ではありません。どの線が機械のためで、どの線が人の歩みのためで、どの線が雨を受け流すためなのか。そう問い始めたとき、景色は急に茶の湯に近づきます。茶の湯が用と美を分けずに見る文化だとすれば、和束の斜面はその考え方を屋外に拡大したものだからです。

私は和束の話題に触れるとき、あえて「絶景」という言葉をあまり使いたくありません。絶景という語は、見る人を中心に据えすぎることがあるからです。和束の茶畑は、こちらが眺めて消費する景色というより、そこで働いてきた無数の朝の蓄積です。茶の湯で一碗を前にしたとき、私たちはしばしば亭主の準備の見えない時間に思いを向けます。同じように、和束の斜面を見るときも、目に入る緑の前に、剪定した手、運んだ肥料、開いた道、水を読んだ判断があると知ることが、土地への礼になるでしょう。

だから今日の主題は、名所案内ではなく、斜面を読むということです。茶室の床を読むように、茶碗の見込みを読むように、山の線を読む。そこに表れているのは自然そのものではなく、自然に逆らいきらず、しかし任せきりにもせず、茶のために折り合いをつけ続けてきた人の気配です。茶の湯が一服のなかに世界を縮める営みだとすれば、和束の茶畑は、その世界が最初にひらかれる野のページなのだと思います。

## 今日の見どころ

畝の線が完全な直線になっていないところに注目してみてください。少し曲がる、そのわずかな揺れの中に、地形に従う知恵と、茶を育てる手の判断が見えます。

## 小さな実践

今朝、近所の坂道や歩道のわずかな傾き、雨水の流れそうな筋、植え込みの並び方を一分だけ眺めてみてください。平らに見える場所にも実は地形の都合があり、人の工夫がそれに寄り添っていると気づけたら、その観察を茶碗の高台や茶杓の反りを見る目へ静かに移してみましょう。

## Full English Edition

# Reading the Slope: Wazuka’s Tea Fields

Let your gaze travel a little farther this morning, and receive quietly the fact that the work behind a single bowl of tea begins long before the bowl itself.

When you visit Wazuka in southern Kyoto Prefecture, what first comes into view is not simply a landscape that happens to contain tea fields. It is a landscape in which the hills themselves seem to have become tea fields. The rows were not laid down on flat ground and then admired afterward. They follow the shoulders of the hills and the pull of the valleys, as though tea had been planted to trace the breathing of the mountain. Those who care about tea culture are drawn to this sight not only because it is beautiful, but because the deep time behind Japanese tea is still visible on the land before it becomes matcha, sencha, or gyokuro in the cup.

The Kyoto Prefectural Board of Education describes Wazuka’s Uji tea landscape as tea fields spreading from the foot of the hills toward the ridges, following the undulations of the terrain, and cultivated without radically remaking the land. That explanation rescues Wazuka from being reduced to a photogenic green pattern. If we look only as tourists, we may notice the stripes. If we look with the eye of tea, what matters is that the stripes are not rigid. Their order trembles slightly as it obeys slope, drainage, sunlight, and the practical realities of care. Even a row that appears straight has arrived there through a long series of adjustments between land, water, labor, and growth. In that sense, Wazuka’s beauty is not the beauty of a perfect design imposed from above. It is the beauty of sustained judgment.

Wazuka Town’s own materials describe the area plainly as a major production center of Uji tea. The phrase is modest, but it carries weight. A major producing region is not only a place that makes a great quantity of tea. It is a place where tea has been repeated at the scale of daily life so persistently that settlements, paths, processing buildings, and the shape of work itself begin to take their form from tea. Kyoto Prefecture’s explanation of the “cultural landscape of Uji tea” goes further, noting that this wider region has supported tea production for roughly seven hundred years and has nurtured Japanese tea cultures including matcha, sencha, and gyokuro. Within that long history, Wazuka feels like a place where the prehistory of the tea room remains visible outdoors, as topography.

In the tea room, utensils usually appear at a human scale. A tea scoop, a bowl, a lid rest: all are near enough to touch. Wazuka quietly inverts that feeling. Here, the mountain begins to look like the utensil. The furrows resemble comb marks. The slope feels like the inside wall of a very large vessel. If we stand before the fields around Ishidera and say only, “How beautiful,” we have not yet finished seeing them. Which lines are shaped for machines, which for walking, which for carrying water away after rain? Once those questions begin, the scenery moves much closer to chanoyu. Tea culture does not separate use from beauty; Wazuka’s slopes show that principle outdoors at full scale.

For that reason, I hesitate to call Wazuka merely a “spectacular view.” That phrase can place too much importance on the viewer. These tea fields are less a landscape for our consumption than an accumulation of countless mornings of work. In the tea room, when we receive a bowl, we are often asked to remember the unseen time of preparation that made the encounter possible. Wazuka asks for a similar courtesy. Before the visible green, there were hands that pruned, paths that were cut, fertilizer that was carried, and judgments made by reading water, weather, and incline. To know that is a form of respect toward the land.

So today’s theme is not a scenic destination, but the act of reading a slope. Read it the way you would read an alcove, or the interior of a tea bowl. What appears there is not raw nature alone. It is the trace of people who never fully surrendered to nature, yet never tried to erase it either, and who kept negotiating with it for the sake of tea. If chanoyu is an art of compressing a world into one bowl, then Wazuka’s tea fields may be one of the first outdoor pages on which that world opens.

## What to Notice Today

Notice where the rows are not perfectly straight. In those slight bends, you can see intelligence yielding to terrain and hands making decisions in service of tea.

## A Small Practice

This morning, spend one minute looking at a nearby slope, curb, path, or planted border. Notice where water would run, where the ground subtly rises, and how human design has adjusted to that fact. Then carry that same way of seeing into a tea bowl’s foot or the curve of a tea scoop. The practice is simply this: learn to notice form as a conversation, not a command.

| 茶畑 | ちゃばたけ | tea field | Use for cultivated tea-growing fields, especially in regional landscape contexts. |
| 和束町 | わづかちょう | Wazuka Town | A place name in southern Kyoto Prefecture, often associated with Uji tea production. |
| 宇治茶 | うじちゃ | Uji tea | Refers to tea produced within the recognized Uji tea region and tradition. |
| 文化的景観 | ぶんかてきけいかん | cultural landscape | Useful when talking about land shaped by long human practice, not just scenery. |
| 丘陵 | きゅうりょう | hill / hilly ridge | A precise word for rolling upland terrain rather than a single mountain. |
| 畝 | うね | furrow / planted row | Often used for the raised or lined rows visible in cultivated fields. |
| 斜面 | しゃめん | slope | A key word for reading landform and movement in garden or tea-field observation. |
| 生業 | なりわい | livelihood / way of making a living | Conveys work embedded in community life, not just an occupation label. |
| 茶工場 | ちゃこうじょう | tea processing factory | Refers to the local facilities where harvested tea is processed. |
